Reward model, DPO, GRPO and state-space models

Seven derivations from the Nov 7 class (with the Nov 10 recording filling gaps): Bradley-Terry reward learning, the closed-form KL-regularised policy, the staged DPO derivation, the group-relative advantage of GRPO, ZOH discretisation of a state-space model, its unrolling into a convolution, and the S4 / Mamba ideas. Each proof has a picture, numbered algebra steps with reasons, and a worked number check.

Notation

$x$ = prompt, $y$ = response (an LLM's output sequence), $y_w \succ y_l$ = "winner preferred over loser", $r(x,y)$ = scalar reward, $\pi_\theta$ = policy being trained, $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ = frozen reference policy (the "old" policy of the PPO part), $\beta>0$ = KL strength, $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$. For SSMs: $u$ input, $x(t)$ hidden state, $y$ output, $\Delta$ sampling step. (Note the clash: in the SSM part $x$ is the state, not the prompt; the professor flagged this.)

1. Bradley-Terry reward model and its loss

Statement

(Lecture Nov 7 / Nov 10.) If the probability that $y_w$ beats $y_l$ is modelled as $\dfrac{e^{r_w}}{e^{r_w}+e^{r_l}}$, then it equals $\sigma(r_w-r_l)$, depends only on the reward difference, and fitting $r_\phi$ by maximum likelihood on preference pairs is exactly minimising $-\mathbb E\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)$. Used later: this loss is the starting point of DPO (proof 3).

Derivation

  1. Give every candidate a positive "strength" $e^{r}$. Bradley-Terry says the chance that item $i$ wins a pairwise contest is its share of the total strength: $$P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\frac{e^{r(x,y_w)}}{e^{r(x,y_w)}+e^{r(x,y_l)}}$$ Bradley-Terry (Luce choice) model definition; the model class we assume
  2. Divide numerator and denominator by $e^{r(x,y_w)}$: $$P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l))}}$$ algebra
  3. Recognise the logistic function: $$P_\phi(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)$$ $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$
  4. Invariance: replace $r\to r+c(x)$ for any function of the prompt alone. The difference $r_w-r_l$ is unchanged, so the probability is unchanged: $$\sigma\big((r_w+c)-(r_l+c)\big)=\sigma(r_w-r_l)$$ $\sigma(a-b)$ depends only on the difference Hence $r$ is only identified up to a per-prompt constant. (This is exactly the freedom that lets $Z(x)$ vanish in proof 3.)
  5. Data are pairs $(x_i,y_{w,i},y_{l,i})$ with the winner labelled by the human. The likelihood of the dataset, taking pairs as independent, is the product of $P_\phi(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)$: $$\mathcal L(\phi)=\prod_i\sigma\big(r_\phi(x_i,y_{w,i})-r_\phi(x_i,y_{l,i})\big)$$ maximum likelihood, i.i.d. pairs
  6. Take the log (monotone, turns the product into a sum) and flip sign to get a loss to minimise; the average over data is the expectation: $$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{RM}}(\phi)=-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)$$ negative log-likelihood
  7. Gradient (step filled in — not written out in class). Let $\Delta r=r_w-r_l$. Using $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\sigma(-z)$, $$\nabla_\phi\big[-\log\sigma(\Delta r)\big]=-\sigma(-\Delta r)\,\big(\nabla_\phi r_w-\nabla_\phi r_l\big)$$ chain rule The weight $\sigma(-\Delta r)=1-P(y_w\succ y_l)$ is the model's current error probability: confident-correct pairs barely update, mistakes update strongly.
▪
Worked check

$r_w=2$, $r_l=0.5$: gap $=1.5$; $P=\sigma(1.5)=0.8176$; loss $=-\ln 0.8176=0.2014$. Direct form: $e^{2}/(e^{2}+e^{0.5})=7.389/(7.389+1.649)=0.8176$. ✓. Gradient weight $\sigma(-1.5)=0.1824$. Shift test: $r_w=12,\ r_l=10.5$ gives the same $0.8176$. If the model were wrong ($r_w=0.5,\ r_l=2$): $P=0.1824$, loss $=1.70$, weight $0.8176$.

Remember for the exam
  • Data is $(x,y_w,y_l)$: pairwise comparison is easier and less subjective than scoring 0 to 10 and regressing (professor's motivation; scale.ai-style data collection).
  • Write both forms: ratio of exponentials $=\sigma(r_w-r_l)$, and the boxed loss $-\mathbb E\log\sigma(r_w-r_l)$.
  • The reward model is a separate transformer from the policy; it takes $(x,y)$ and returns a real number.
Common mistake

Reading $r$ as a calibrated absolute score. Only differences are identified (step 4), so the same loss is minimised by $r$ and $r+c(x)$; also never write the loss as $-\log\sigma(r_l-r_w)$ (that trains the model to prefer the loser).

2. Closed-form optimum of the KL-regularised objective

Statement

(Lecture Nov 7 / Nov 10; the professor said it follows by differentiating and "variational calculus"; the completing-the-KL route below is filled in.) For a fixed prompt $x$, the maximiser of $\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)]-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ over distributions $\pi$ is $\pi^*(y|x)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}/Z(x)$. Used later: inverting it gives the reward in terms of policies, the key move of DPO.

Derivation (completing the KL)

  1. Fix $x$ and write the objective as a sum over responses: $$J(\pi)=\sum_y\pi(y|x)\,r(x,y)-\beta\sum_y\pi(y|x)\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}$$ definition of expectation and KL
  2. Factor $-\beta$ and combine the logs, using $r=\beta\log e^{r/\beta}$: $$J(\pi)=-\beta\sum_y\pi(y|x)\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}}$$ $\log a-\log b$ combined
  3. The denominator is not a distribution; define its normaliser (sum over all responses, independent of $\pi$ and of any particular $y$): $$Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta},\qquad \pi^*(y|x)=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}}{Z(x)}$$ normalisation constant independent of $y$
  4. Write $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}e^{r/\beta}=Z\,\pi^*$ inside the log and pull $\log Z$ out; $\sum_y\pi=1$ makes it a constant: $$J(\pi)=-\beta\sum_y\pi\log\frac{\pi}{\pi^*}+\beta\log Z(x)=-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^*)+\beta\log Z(x)$$ $\sum_y\pi=1$
  5. Gibbs inequality: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^*)\ge0$ with equality iff $\pi=\pi^*$. So $J(\pi)\le\beta\log Z(x)$ for every $\pi$, with equality exactly at $\pi^*$: $$\max_\pi J(\pi)=\beta\log Z(x),\qquad \arg\max_\pi J=\pi^*$$ KL $\ge0$ (Gibbs variational principle)
▪

Same result via the Lagrangian (the route the professor described)

  1. Add a multiplier $\lambda$ for $\sum_y\pi=1$: $\ \mathcal L=\sum_y\pi\,r-\beta\sum_y\pi\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}-\lambda\big(\sum_y\pi-1\big)$ constrained optimisation
  2. Differentiate with respect to $\pi(y)$ and set to zero: $$r(x,y)-\beta\Big(\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}+1\Big)-\lambda=0$$ $\partial(\pi\log\pi)/\partial\pi=\log\pi+1$
  3. Solve for $\pi$: $\ \pi(y)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}\,e^{-(1+\lambda/\beta)}$. The last factor does not depend on $y$ and is fixed by $\sum_y\pi=1$ to be $1/Z(x)$. normalisation
▪
Worked check ($\beta=1$)

$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=(0.5,0.3,0.2)$, $r=(0,1,2)$. Weights $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}e^{r}=(0.5,\,0.8155,\,1.4778)$, so $Z=2.7933$ and $\pi^*=(0.179,\,0.292,\,0.529)$. Objective values: $J(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=\mathbb E[r]-0=0.7$; $J(\pi^*)=1.0272=\ln Z$ ✓ (the bound $\beta\log Z$ is attained). For a rival $\pi=$ uniform: $J=0.9298$, and $\beta\log Z-J=0.0975=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\text{unif}\|\pi^*)$ ✓ matches step 4. With $\beta=0.5$: $\pi^*=(0.037,0.163,0.801)$, sharper toward the best response.

Remember for the exam
  • $\pi^*=\frac1{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)$; $\beta\to\infty$ gives $\pi^*\to\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, $\beta\to0$ concentrates on $\arg\max r$.
  • The professor stressed that $\pi^*$ is "only theoretical": $Z(x)$ sums over all possible responses and cannot be computed, and the true $r$ is unknown. DPO sidesteps both.
  • This is the same constrained problem as TRPO/PPO (reward subject to KL to the old/reference policy), here as the penalised Lagrangian form.
Common mistake

Forgetting that $Z$ depends on $x$ (it is a sum over $y$ for that prompt), or dropping $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$: the optimum is $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ tilted by the reward, not $\mathrm{softmax}(r/\beta)$ alone.

3. Direct Preference Optimization, staged derivation

Statement

(Lecture Nov 7 / Nov 10.) Substituting the optimal-policy identity of proof 2 into the Bradley-Terry model makes $Z(x)$ cancel, and maximum likelihood on preference pairs becomes a loss in $\pi_\theta$ alone: $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\mathbb E\log\sigma\big(\beta[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)}-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)}]\big)$. No reward network, no RL loop. This is the central result of the RLHF part; proofs 1 and 2 are its ingredients.

Stage 1. Start from Bradley-Terry with the (unknown) true reward

  1. $$P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma\big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\big)$$ Bradley-Terry (proof 1)

Stage 2. Invert the optimal policy to express the reward through policies

  1. $$\log\pi^*(y|x)=\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)+\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}-\log Z(x)$$ take log of the closed form
  2. $$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$$ multiply by $\beta$, rearrange

Stage 3. Take winner minus loser: the partition function cancels

  1. Write Stage 2 for the winner and for the loser (same $x$): $$r(x,y_w)=\beta\log\tfrac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)}+\beta\log Z(x),\qquad r(x,y_l)=\beta\log\tfrac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$$
  2. Subtract: $$r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)}$$ $Z(x)$ does not depend on $y$

Stage 4. Plug into Bradley-Terry; replace the optimal policy by the trainable policy

  1. Substituting the Stage 3 difference into Stage 1 gives the preference probability under the optimal policy: $$P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma\Big(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)}-\beta\log\tfrac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Big)$$ substitution
  2. $\pi^*$ is unknown and incomputable, but it is the policy we are after: model it with the network $\pi_\theta$. This is a reparameterisation of the reward model, $r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}$ (the $\beta\log Z$ part is dropped; by proof 1 step 4 a per-prompt constant does not change any preference probability): implicit reward

Stage 5. Maximum likelihood gives the loss; two pipelines collapse into one step

  1. Maximum likelihood over preference pairs, as in proof 1, now with $P_\theta$ from Stage 4: $$\max_\theta\ \mathbb E\log P_\theta(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)$$ maximum likelihood
  2. Negate to obtain the DPO loss: $$\boxed{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\log\sigma\Big(\beta\Big[\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)}-\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Big]\Big)}$$ negative log-likelihood
  3. Sequence log-probabilities (step filled in — not written out in class): $\log\pi(y|x)=\sum_t\log\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})$, i.e. sums of token log-probs from the LLM's log-softmax, so the loss needs only forward passes of $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ on $y_w$ and $y_l$. chain rule of probability
  4. Gradient (step filled in — not written out in class). With implicit reward $\hat r_\theta=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}$ and $\hat\Delta=\hat r_\theta(y_w)-\hat r_\theta(y_l)$, using $\frac{d}{dz}[-\log\sigma(z)]=-\sigma(-z)$: $$\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb E\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l|x)\big)\Big]$$ chain rule; $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ has no $\theta$ Raise the winner's likelihood, lower the loser's, each pair weighted by how wrongly the implicit reward currently ranks it.
▪
Worked check

(a) Identity of Stage 2 with proof 2's numbers ($\beta=1$): $\log(\pi^*/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=(-1.0272,\ -0.0272,\ 0.9728)$ and $\log Z=1.0272$; adding gives $(0,1,2)=r$ ✓. The gap $r_3-r_1=2$ equals $0.9728-(-1.0272)=2$ ✓ with $\log Z$ gone. (b) Loss on one pair: $\beta=0.1$, $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w)}=+3.0$, $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l)}=-2.0$. Margin $=0.1\times5=0.5$; $\sigma(0.5)=0.6225$; loss $=0.474$; gradient weight $\sigma(-0.5)=0.3775$, times the factor $\beta=0.1$ gives $0.0378$. At init $\pi_\theta=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$: margin $0$, loss $\ln2=0.693$.

Remember for the exam
  • Chain to reproduce: Bradley-Terry $\to$ optimal Boltzmann policy $\to$ $r=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}+\beta\log Z$ $\to$ winner minus loser kills $\log Z$ $\to$ boxed loss.
  • DPO still needs preference data $(x,y_w,y_l)$ and needs a trainable policy and a reference policy; it does not train a scalar reward model; it turns alignment into a classification-style supervised objective.
  • Professor on trade-offs (practical, not a theorem): PPO-style RLHF is more stable and slightly more data-efficient thanks to an explicit reward model; DPO is simpler. Only the final actor is deployed.
  • Pairwise data discards absolute scores but avoids subjective calibration; ordering suffices (SQL example: correct beats wrong; if both correct, faster wins).
Common mistake

Saying DPO "needs no reference model" or "no preference data". It needs both. Also, $Z(x)$ cancels only because $y_w$ and $y_l$ answer the same prompt; and the ratio is $\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ per response, not $\pi_\theta(y_w)/\pi_\theta(y_l)$.

4. GRPO: group-relative advantage replaces the critic

Statement

(Lecture Nov 7 / Nov 10.) PPO needs four networks (actor, old/reference policy, reward model, critic) because the advantage is $A=r-V$. GRPO samples a group of $G$ responses to one prompt and uses $\hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(r_{1:G})}{\mathrm{std}(r_{1:G})+\varepsilon}$, which needs no critic; the group mean is a baseline that leaves the policy gradient unbiased (up to a known factor). Used later: this is the training rule behind DeepSeek-style RLVR.

Derivation

  1. Policy-gradient with a baseline (from the PPO part; notation $x$ prompt, $y$ response): $\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[A(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y|x)\big]$ with $A=r-V$ and $V(x)=\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r(x,y)]$. PPO learns $V_\psi$ as a fourth network. advantage = reward minus value
  2. Any $y$-independent baseline $b(x)$ is allowed (step filled in — not written out in class): $\mathbb E_y[b\,\nabla\log\pi]=b\sum_y\nabla\pi(y|x)=b\,\nabla\sum_y\pi(y|x)=b\,\nabla 1=0$. log-derivative trick; $\sum_y\pi=1$
  3. Estimate the baseline $V(x)$ by sampling instead of learning: draw $G$ responses $y_1,\dots,y_G\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot|x)$ with rewards $r_i=r(x,y_i)$ and use the sample mean $$\hat V(x)=\frac1G\sum_{j=1}^G r_j$$ Monte Carlo estimate of $\mathbb E[r]$
  4. Subtract it to get a per-sample advantage; divide by the group standard deviation to put every prompt on a common scale (easy and hard prompts then give updates of similar size): $$\hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)+\varepsilon}$$ baseline + standardisation; $\varepsilon$ avoids division by 0
  5. Consequences: $\sum_i\hat A_i=0$ (centred); with population std, $\sum_i\hat A_i^2=G$ (step filled in). If all rewards in the group are equal, $\hat A_i=0$ and the prompt gives no gradient. algebra
  6. Plug $\hat A_i$ into the PPO-style surrogate in place of the critic-based advantage. The critic network $V_\psi$ (and its training) is gone; only actor, reference/old policy and reward (a model, or a verifier for RLVR) remain. no value network
▪
Caveats (filled in, not from class)

Because $\hat V$ contains $r_i$ itself, the baseline is not strictly independent of $y_i$; the leave-one-out mean would be exactly unbiased, while the full-group mean scales the gradient by $(1-1/G)$, harmless for large $G$. The std division is a per-prompt rescaling, not a baseline. The published GRPO objective also uses the PPO clipped ratio and a KL penalty to a reference policy; the lecture mentioned only the advantage estimator.

Worked check

$r=(0.2,0.5,0.9,0.4)$: mean $0.5$; population std $=\sqrt{(0.09+0+0.16+0.01)/4}=0.2550$. $\hat A=(-1.177,\ 0,\ +1.569,\ -0.392)$; sum $=0$; sum of squares $=1.385+0+2.462+0.154=4=G$ ✓. For verifiable 0/1 rewards $(1,0,0,1)$: mean $0.5$, std $0.5$, $\hat A=(+1,-1,-1,+1)$.

Remember for the exam
  • PPO-RLHF uses 4 roles: actor $\pi_\theta$, old/reference policy, reward model, critic $V_\psi$. GRPO removes the critic; DPO removes the reward model; combining them removes both.
  • Write the advantage formula with the group mean and std; say "each response is evaluated relative to its peers, supplying a baseline without a value network".
  • Clarification printed in the notes: GRPO is group-relative normalisation, not just "sum of rewards" as the transcript said.
Common mistake

Claiming GRPO needs no reward at all. It still needs a reward per sampled response (a learned model or a verifier such as test-pass fraction); it only drops the learned value network.

5. SSM discretisation (zero-order hold)

Statement

(Lecture Nov 7 / Nov 10; the class gave the result and said "skip the algebra", so the derivation is filled in.) For the continuous system $\dot x=Ax+Bu$, $y=Cx+Du$ sampled every $\Delta$ with the input held constant over each step, the exact state update is $x_k=\bar Ax_{k-1}+\bar Bu_k$ with $\bar A=e^{\Delta A}$ and $\bar B=(\Delta A)^{-1}(e^{\Delta A}-I)\Delta B$. Used later: this recurrence is what gets unrolled into a convolution (proof 6).

Derivation

  1. Multiply $\dot x-Ax=Bu$ by $e^{-At}$; since $\frac{d}{dt}\big(e^{-At}x\big)=e^{-At}(\dot x-Ax)$, $$\frac{d}{dt}\big(e^{-At}x(t)\big)=e^{-At}Bu(t)$$ integrating factor
  2. Integrate from $t_0$ to $t$ and multiply by $e^{At}$: $$x(t)=e^{A(t-t_0)}x(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s)\,ds$$ variation of constants
  3. Apply to one step, $t_0=(k-1)\Delta$, $t=k\Delta$, and write $x_k=x(k\Delta)$: $$x_k=e^{\Delta A}x_{k-1}+\int_{(k-1)\Delta}^{k\Delta}e^{(k\Delta-s)A}Bu(s)\,ds$$ exact over one interval
  4. Zero-order hold: $u(s)=u_k$ on the interval, so it leaves the integral: $$x_k=e^{\Delta A}x_{k-1}+\Big(\int_{(k-1)\Delta}^{k\Delta}e^{(k\Delta-s)A}ds\Big)Bu_k$$ $u$ constant on the step
  5. Substitute $\tau=k\Delta-s$ ($s=(k-1)\Delta$ gives $\tau=\Delta$ and $s=k\Delta$ gives $\tau=0$; $ds=-d\tau$ flips the limits back to $0\to\Delta$): $$\int_{(k-1)\Delta}^{k\Delta}e^{(k\Delta-s)A}ds=\int_0^{\Delta}e^{\tau A}d\tau$$ change of variable
  6. For invertible $A$, $\frac{d}{d\tau}\big(A^{-1}e^{\tau A}\big)=e^{\tau A}$, hence $$\int_0^\Delta e^{\tau A}d\tau=A^{-1}\big(e^{\Delta A}-I\big)=(\Delta A)^{-1}\big(e^{\Delta A}-I\big)\Delta$$ fundamental theorem of calculus
  7. Read off the discrete system, and since $y=Cx+Du$ is algebraic it carries over unchanged: $$\bar A=e^{\Delta A},\quad \bar B=(\Delta A)^{-1}(e^{\Delta A}-I)\,\Delta B,\quad \bar C=C,\quad \bar D=D$$ $$x_k=\bar Ax_{k-1}+\bar Bu_k,\qquad y_k=\bar Cx_k+\bar Du_k$$ matches the lecture's recurrence
▪
Worked check (scalar, $A=-1$, $B=1$, $\Delta=0.1$)

$\bar A=e^{-0.1}=0.904837$. $\bar B=(-1)^{-1}(e^{-0.1}-1)\cdot1=1-e^{-0.1}=0.095163$. Sanity: from $x(0)=0$ with constant $u=1$, the exact ODE solution is $x(t)=1-e^{-t}$, so $x(0.1)=0.095163=\bar B$ ✓. Other discretisations for comparison: forward Euler $\bar A=I+\Delta A=0.9$, $\bar B=\Delta B=0.1$ (the first-order truncation of the exact pair); bilinear / Tustin (the transform the professor named; formulas filled in) $\bar A=(I-\tfrac\Delta2A)^{-1}(I+\tfrac\Delta2A)=0.95/1.05=0.904762$, $\bar B=(I-\tfrac\Delta2A)^{-1}\Delta B=0.1/1.05=0.095238$. Both approximate the exact ZOH values; ZOH is exact for piecewise-constant input.

Remember for the exam
  • Discretise by sampling $u_k=u(k\Delta)$; the new matrices $\bar A,\bar B,\bar C$ are functions of $\Delta$ and the continuous $A,B,C$; $D$ is usually dropped and handled by a skip connection.
  • Write the recurrence $x_k=\bar Ax_{k-1}+\bar Bu_k$, $y_k=\bar Cx_k$; the state $x_k$ is the "state space" that links input and output sequences.
  • Notation flag from class: here $x$ is the hidden state, $u$ is the input; multi-dimensional inputs are handled channel by channel with learned mixing.
Common mistake

Writing $\bar A=\Delta A$ or $\bar B=B$. The exact transition is the matrix exponential, and $\bar B$ carries the integral factor $A^{-1}(e^{\Delta A}-I)$, which tends to $\Delta I$ only when $\Delta\|A\|$ is small.

6. Unrolling the recurrence into a convolution

Statement

(Lecture Nov 7 / Nov 10.) With $x_{-1}=0$ and $D=0$, the SSM output is $y_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\bar C\bar A^{k-j}\bar B\,u_j=(\bar K*u)_k$ with kernel $\bar K=(\bar C\bar B,\ \bar C\bar A\bar B,\ \bar C\bar A^2\bar B,\dots)$. Hence the same model can be trained as a convolution (parallel over time, via FFT) and run as a recurrence (O(1) state per step) at inference. Used later: S4 and Mamba are different ways of computing $\bar K$.

Derivation

  1. Start the recurrence from rest and run it for three steps: $$x_0=\bar Bu_0,\quad x_1=\bar A\bar Bu_0+\bar Bu_1,\quad x_2=\bar A^2\bar Bu_0+\bar A\bar Bu_1+\bar Bu_2$$ substitute $x_{-1}=0$, then repeatedly
  2. Claim: $x_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\bar A^{\,k-j}\bar B\,u_j$. Induction: true for $k=0$; if true for $k$, then $$x_{k+1}=\bar A\sum_{j=0}^{k}\bar A^{k-j}\bar Bu_j+\bar Bu_{k+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k+1}\bar A^{k+1-j}\bar Bu_j$$ induction on the recurrence
  3. Apply the output map ($D$ omitted, handled by a skip connection): $$y_k=\bar Cx_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\bar C\bar A^{\,k-j}\bar B\,u_j$$ linearity of $\bar C$
  4. Define the kernel entries $\bar K_m=\bar C\bar A^{m}\bar B$ and reindex with $m=k-j$: $$y_k=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\bar K_m\,u_{k-m}=(\bar K*u)_k,\qquad \bar K=(\bar C\bar B,\bar C\bar A\bar B,\bar C\bar A^2\bar B,\dots)$$ definition of (causal) convolution
  5. Equivalently $y=Tu$ with a lower-triangular Toeplitz matrix $T_{kj}=\bar K_{k-j}$ (step filled in): the output at step $k$ is a fixed linear function of inputs $u_0,\dots,u_k$ only. causality
  6. Parallel computation: by the convolution theorem $\mathcal F(\bar K*u)=\mathcal F(\bar K)\odot\mathcal F(u)$ (zero-pad to length $2L$ to avoid wrap-around, step filled in), so $y=\mathcal F^{-1}\big(\mathcal F(\bar K)\odot\mathcal F(u)\big)$ in $O(L\log L)$ with no sequential dependency between time steps. FFT, convolution theorem
  7. Two equivalent computation modes of one model: training sees the whole sequence, so build $\bar K$ once and convolve (parallel, unlike an RNN's step-by-step backprop); inference generates token by token, so keep only the state $x_k$ and apply the recurrence, with memory independent of how many tokens have been seen (unlike attention's growing cache). same $y$ from both views

Remember for the exam
  • Say it as: "unroll the recurrence like an RNN with $x_{-1}=0$; the output is $\bar C\bar A^{k}\bar B u_0+\bar C\bar A^{k-1}\bar Bu_1+\dots$, which is a convolution of the SSM kernel with $u$".
  • Fitting an SSM is equivalent to estimating the kernel $\bar K$ (S4/S5/Mamba differ in how); output = IFFT of ($\mathrm{FFT}(\bar K)\cdot\mathrm{FFT}(u)$).
  • Advantages listed in class: no quadratic attention, long context, streaming recurrent inference, convolutional parallelism in training, usable as a layer inside hybrids.
Common mistake

Mixing up the power: it is $\bar A^{\,k-j}$ (age of the input), so the newest input $u_k$ gets $\bar C\bar B$ with no power of $\bar A$. Also the convolution form requires fixed $(\bar A,\bar B,\bar C)$; once they depend on the input (Mamba, next section) there is no single kernel.

7. S4 kernel generating function and Mamba selectivity

Statement

(Lecture Nov 7 / Nov 10; the professor skipped the algebra and assigned it for self-study.) (a) The kernel's generating function is $K(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}\bar K_nz^{-n}=\bar C(I-\bar Az^{-1})^{-1}\bar B$, a closed form that turns "compute powers of $\bar A$" into "invert one matrix", which S4 exploits with a structured $A$. (b) Mamba lets $\bar B,\bar C,\Delta$ depend on the input, which breaks the single-kernel form; this part is conceptual only, not derived in class.

(a) Generating function of the kernel (step filled in)

  1. Definition of the generating (Z-transform) function of the kernel sequence $k_n=\bar K_n=\bar C\bar A^n\bar B$: $$K(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}k_nz^{-n}$$ Z-transform, as in the lecture
  2. Insert $k_n$ and pull constants out: $$K(z)=\bar C\Big[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(\bar Az^{-1}\big)^n\Big]\bar B$$ $\bar A^nz^{-n}=(\bar Az^{-1})^n$
  3. Neumann (matrix geometric) series, valid when $|z|$ exceeds the spectral radius of $\bar A$: $$\sum_{n\ge0}M^n=(I-M)^{-1}\ \Rightarrow\ K(z)=\bar C\big(I-\bar Az^{-1}\big)^{-1}\bar B$$ geometric series
  4. Consequence: no powers $\bar A^n$ are formed. Evaluating at points $z$ on the unit circle gives the kernel in the frequency domain directly, where convolution is a pointwise product; the cost is one matrix inverse per frequency, cheap when $A$ is structured (diagonal, or diagonal-plus-low-rank $\Lambda-PQ^*$). why S4 constrains $A$ (details not derived in class)

(b) Mamba: input-dependent selectivity (conceptual, not derived)

What the professor said Nov 7

Standard (linear time-invariant) SSMs keep the parameters fixed across positions. Mamba (S6) makes the parameters depend on the input, permitting selective retention or forgetting. The notes clarify that precisely it is $B$, $C$ and the step size $\Delta$ that are input-dependent, while $A$ stays structured and acts through $\bar A=e^{\Delta A}$; the lecture's phrase "$A,B,C$ vary with time" is loose. Reading the figure: with $A=-1$, $\Delta=0.1$ keeps $90\%$ of the old state per step and $\Delta=3$ keeps only $5\%$, so a large input-dependent $\Delta_k$ effectively resets the memory. The three-way comparison S4 / S5 / Mamba, the Z-transform kernel algebra and structured $A$ were assigned as self-study.

Remember for the exam
  • S4 = Structured State Space for sequence modelling: constrain $A$ (diagonal, or DPLR $A=\Lambda-PQ^*$) so long kernels are computable; kernel via generating functions / Z-transform in the frequency domain, then multiply spectra and inverse-transform.
  • Mamba = selective SSM: $B$, $C$, $\Delta$ depend on the input, giving content-based memory; S4 is time-invariant.
  • SSMs are sequence-to-sequence, not inherently multimodal; images become patch sequences, as in ViT.
Common mistake

Saying Mamba makes "$A$, $B$, $C$ all time-varying" without qualification, or claiming it can still be trained by one FFT convolution; time variation destroys the Toeplitz structure of proof 6.
