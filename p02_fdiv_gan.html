f-divergence, variational bound and GAN

Every derivation of the Aug 29 and Sept 05 lectures (plus the BCE tutorial), in the professor's order and notation: why $D_f\ge0$, which $f$ gives which divergence, the Fenchel conjugate, pushing the supremum inside the integral, the variational bound, the min–max (VDM) problem, GAN as the special case $f(u)=u\log u-(u+1)\log\frac{u+1}{2}$, the optimal discriminator, the BCE view and the classifier-guided story.

One-paragraph summary

We want to minimise a divergence $D_f(P_X\|P_\theta)$ but we only have samples, never densities. Fenchel duality writes $f$ as a supremum of straight lines $f(u)=\sup_t[tu-f^*(t)]$; moving the supremum outside the integral turns it into a supremum over functions $T(x)$ and gives the lower bound $D_f\ge\sup_T\big(\mathbb E_{P_X}[T]-\mathbb E_{P_\theta}[f^*(T)]\big)$, which is made of expectations and therefore estimable by sample averages. Minimising this over the generator $\theta$ and maximising it over the critic $T_w$ is a saddle-point (adversarial) problem; choosing the GAN $f$ makes the critic a sigmoid classifier and the objective the binary cross-entropy of a real/fake classifier.

Notation (as in the lecture)

Data density $p=P_X$, model density $q=P_\theta$ (the law of $\hat X=G_\theta(Z)$, $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$). The density ratio is $u(x)=p(x)/q(x)\in\mathbb R^+$. $D_f(P_X\|P_\theta)=\int q(x)\,f\big(u(x)\big)\,dx$ with $f:\mathbb R^+\to\mathbb R$ convex. I assume $f(1)=0$ throughout (all the examples satisfy it; the lecture lists it implicitly through "$D_f=0$ iff $P=Q$").

1. Non-negativity of the f-divergence

Statement

For convex $f$ with $f(1)=0$: (i) $D_f(P\|Q)\ge0$; (ii) $D_f(P\|Q)=0$ iff $P=Q$ (for $f$ strictly convex at 1). Class Aug 29 — set as homework ("prove these two properties from the definition"). Used later: every divergence-minimisation argument (GAN, MLE=KL, ELBO gap) relies on "minimum value 0 is reached exactly when the model equals the data".

  1. Definition convexity (lecture): for weights $\alpha_i\ge0$, $\sum\alpha_i=1$, $\sum_i\alpha_if(u_i)\ge f(\sum_i\alpha_iu_i)$. The integral version is Jensen's inequality for the random variable $U=u(X)$, $X\sim Q$. Jensen: f convex $$\mathbb E_{X\sim Q}\big[f(U)\big]\;\ge\;f\big(\mathbb E_{X\sim Q}[U]\big)$$
  2. Write $D_f$ as an expectation under $Q=P_\theta$. definition of $D_f$ $$D_f(P\|Q)=\int q(x)\,f\!\Big(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\Big)dx=\mathbb E_{X\sim Q}\big[f(U)\big]$$
  3. Compute the mean of the ratio: the $q$ cancels. $p$ is a density $$\mathbb E_{Q}[U]=\int q(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx=\int p(x)\,dx=1$$
  4. Combine 1–3 and use $f(1)=0$. $f(1)=0$ $$D_f(P\|Q)\;\ge\;f(1)\;=\;0$$ This is property (i).
  5. If $P=Q$ then $u\equiv1$ and $D_f=\int q\,f(1)\,dx=0$. plug in This is "$\Leftarrow$" of (ii).
  6. Conversely let $D_f=0$. Then equality holds in Jensen. For $f$ strictly convex at the point $1=\mathbb E[U]$, equality in Jensen forces $U$ to be constant $Q$-almost surely, $U\equiv c$. equality case of Jensen
  7. A constant ratio must be 1: both integrate to 1 $$1=\int p\,dx=\int c\,q\,dx=c\quad\Rightarrow\quad p(x)=q(x)\ \text{a.e.}$$ This is "$\Rightarrow$" of (ii).
▪

(Steps 1–7 were homework, not written out in class. The remark that $f$ must be strictly convex at 1 and $f(1)=0$ is added here. For $f(u)=\tfrac12|u-1|$ (total variation, not strictly convex) property (ii) is true directly: $\tfrac12\int|p-q|=0\Rightarrow p=q$ a.e.)

Worked numeric check

$P=(0.7,0.3)$, $Q=(0.5,0.5)$ on two points, $f(u)=u\log u$ (natural log). Ratios $u=(1.4,\,0.6)$; $\mathbb E_Q[U]=0.5\cdot1.4+0.5\cdot0.6=1$ ✓. $\mathbb E_Q[f(U)]=0.5(1.4\ln1.4)+0.5(0.6\ln0.6)=0.5(0.4711)+0.5(-0.3065)=\mathbf{0.0823}\ \ge\ f(1)=0$. The strictly positive gap is exactly the Jensen gap of Figure 1.

Remember for the exam
  • The whole proof is three lines: $D_f=\mathbb E_Q[f(p/q)]$, $\mathbb E_Q[p/q]=1$, Jensen.
  • Equality $\Leftrightarrow$ the ratio is constant $\Leftrightarrow$ $P=Q$ (constant must be 1 because both densities integrate to 1).
Common mistake

Taking the expectation under $P$ instead of $Q$: the mean of $p/q$ is 1 only under $q$. Also forgetting $f(1)=0$: without it the bound is $D_f\ge f(1)$, not $0$.

2. The f's behind KL, reverse KL, JS, total variation and chi-squared

Statement

Plugging the lecture's table of $f$'s into $D_f=\int q\,f(p/q)$ reproduces the named divergences. Class Aug 29, "Examples of F-divergences" (the professor: "plug it in and a simple algebra gives KL"). Used later: GAN uses a JS-type $f$; forward vs reverse KL (mode covering vs seeking) is the choice between the first two rows.

  1. Forward KL, $f(u)=u\log u$: $q\cdot\frac pq=p$ $$D_f=\int q\,\frac pq\log\frac pq\,dx=\int p\log\frac pq\,dx=D_{KL}(P\|Q)$$
  2. Reverse KL, $f(u)=-\log u=\log(1/u)$: invert the ratio $$D_f=\int q\,\Big(-\log\frac pq\Big)dx=\int q\log\frac qp\,dx=D_{KL}(Q\|P)$$
  3. Total variation, $f(u)=\tfrac12|u-1|$: $q$ is positive, pull inside $|\cdot|$ $$D_f=\int\tfrac12\,q\,\Big|\frac pq-1\Big|dx=\tfrac12\int|p-q|\,dx$$
  4. Chi-squared, $f(u)=(u-1)^2$: expand, common denominator $$D_f=\int q\Big(\frac{p-q}{q}\Big)^2dx=\int\frac{(p-q)^2}{q}\,dx$$
  5. Jensen–Shannon, $f(u)=\tfrac12u\log u-\tfrac12(u+1)\log\frac{u+1}2$. With $m=\frac{p+q}{2}$: first term $q\cdot\frac12\frac pq\log\frac pq=\tfrac12p\log\frac pq$; second term $q\cdot\frac12\big(\frac pq+1\big)\log\frac{p+q}{2q}=m\log\frac mq$. $q\,(p/q+1)=p+q=2m$ $$D_f=\tfrac12\!\int p\log\frac pq\,dx-\int m\log\frac mq\,dx$$
  6. Expand logs: $\log\frac pq=\log p-\log q$ and $\log\frac mq=\log m-\log q$, and use $\int m\log q=\tfrac12\int p\log q+\tfrac12\int q\log q$. $m=\tfrac12p+\tfrac12q$ $$D_f=\tfrac12\!\int p\log p+\tfrac12\!\int q\log q-\int m\log m$$
  7. Compare with $\mathrm{JS}(P,Q)=\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(P\|M)+\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(Q\|M)$: expanding gives $\tfrac12\int p\log p+\tfrac12\int q\log q-\int(\tfrac12p+\tfrac12q)\log m$, identical. same expression $$D_f(P\|Q)=\mathrm{JS}(P,Q)$$ which is visibly symmetric in $P\leftrightarrow Q$.
▪

(The JS algebra, steps 5–7, was stated but not expanded in class — step filled in. Convexity of each $f$ is immediate: $u\log u$ has $f''=1/u>0$; $-\log u$ has $f''=1/u^2>0$; $|u-1|$ and $(u-1)^2$ are convex.)

Remember for the exam
  • Forward vs reverse KL differ only by $f(u)=u\log u$ vs $f(u)=-\log u$ — they swap the roles of $P$ and $Q$.
  • JS and TV are symmetric, KL is not. Different $f$ give different properties of the resulting algorithm; the GAN's $f$ is "JS-like" (see section 7).
Common mistake

Writing $D_f=\int p\,f(q/p)$ or evaluating $f$ at $p$ or $q$ instead of at the ratio $p/q$ with the weight $q$ outside.

3. The Fenchel (convex) conjugate and the representation f = f**

Statement

For convex $f$ define $f^*(t)=\sup_{u\in\mathrm{dom}f}[ut-f(u)]$. Then (a) $f^*$ is convex, (b) $f(u)+f^*(t)\ge ut$ for all $u,t$, (c) $f(u)=\sup_t[tu-f^*(t)]$, i.e. $(f^*)^*=f$. Class Aug 29 — (a) and (c) were used "as fact"; the proofs below are added (c is proved for differentiable $f$). Used later: (c) is the engine of the whole variational bound (section 5).

  1. Definition (point-wise in $t$): for each fixed $t$ solve a maximisation over $u$. definition $$f^*(t)=\sup_{u}\,\big[\,ut-f(u)\,\big]$$
  2. (b) Fenchel–Young. The sup is at least the value at any particular $u$. sup $\ge$ any member $$f^*(t)\;\ge\;ut-f(u)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(u)+f^*(t)\ge ut$$
  3. (a) convexity of $f^*$. For fixed $u$, $t\mapsto ut-f(u)$ is affine, so for $t=\alpha t_1+(1-\alpha)t_2$: affine in $t$ $$ut-f(u)=\alpha\big(ut_1-f(u)\big)+(1-\alpha)\big(ut_2-f(u)\big)\le\alpha f^*(t_1)+(1-\alpha)f^*(t_2)$$ Taking $\sup_u$ on the left: $f^*(\alpha t_1+(1-\alpha)t_2)\le\alpha f^*(t_1)+(1-\alpha)f^*(t_2)$. sup of affine functions is convex
  4. (c), the easy half. Rearranging (b): Fenchel–Young $$tu-f^*(t)\le f(u)\ \ \text{for every }t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sup_t\,[tu-f^*(t)]\le f(u)$$
  5. (c), the other half (differentiable $f$). Fix $u_0$ and set $t_0=f'(u_0)$. The map $u\mapsto ut_0-f(u)$ is concave with zero derivative at $u_0$, so it is maximised there: concave + stationary $\Rightarrow$ max $$f^*(t_0)=u_0t_0-f(u_0)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_0u_0-f^*(t_0)=f(u_0)$$ So the value $f(u_0)$ is attained at $t=f'(u_0)$ (the tangent line at $u_0$ touches $f$ at $u_0$, Figure 3).
  6. Steps 4 and 5 together: $\le$ and attained $$f(u)=\sup_{t}\,\big[\,tu-f^*(t)\,\big]=f^{**}(u),\qquad\text{maximiser }t^*=f'(u)$$ $f=f^{**}$
▪

(Lecture: properties (a) and (c) "taken as fact"; the proofs above are filled in. For non-differentiable or non-closed convex $f$ one needs lower semicontinuity — not needed for the $f$'s of this course.)

Worked numeric check — conjugate of KL's $f(u)=u\log u$

Stationarity $t=f'(u)=1+\ln u\Rightarrow u=e^{t-1}$. Then $f^*(t)=ut-u\ln u=u(t-\ln u)=u\cdot1=\mathbf{e^{t-1}}$. Check $f^{**}$ at $u=2$: the sup of $2t-e^{t-1}$ is at $t=1+\ln2=1.693$ giving $2(1.693)-2=\mathbf{1.386}$, and directly $f(2)=2\ln2=1.386$ ✓. A non-optimal $t=1$ gives $2-1=1\le1.386$ (as Fenchel–Young demands).

Remember for the exam
  • $f^*(t)=\sup_u[ut-f(u)]$ is a point-wise definition: a separate maximisation over $u$ for every $t$.
  • $f=f^{**}$, so $f(u)=\sup_t[tu-f^*(t)]$ with the roles of $u,t$ swapped — this is what lets us replace $f(p/q)$ by something linear in $p/q$.
  • The optimal $t$ for a given $u$ is $t^*=f'(u)$ (reused as $T^*=f'(p/q)$).
Common mistake

Confusing sup with max: sup is the least upper bound and need not be attained; for the well-behaved $f$ of the course treat it as max. Also: the domain of the sup in $f^*$ is $\mathrm{dom}(f)$ (in $u$), for each fixed $t$.

4. Pushing the supremum outside the integral (variational step)

Statement

$\displaystyle\int q(x)\sup_t g(x,t)\,dx\;\ge\;\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\int q(x)\,g\big(x,T(x)\big)dx$ with $g(x,t)=t\,u(x)-f^*(t)$; the scalar $t$ becomes a function $T(x)$. Equality holds iff the class $\mathcal T$ contains $T^*(x)=f'\big(p(x)/q(x)\big)$. Class Aug 29, "Variational calculus: pushing the supremum outside the integral" (given as fact; proof added). Used later: this is the origin of the lower bound and hence of the "adversarial" network $T_w$.

  1. Why a scalar is not enough: $g(x,t)=t\,u(x)-f^*(t)$ depends on $x$ through $u(x)=p(x)/q(x)$, so the maximiser $t^*(x)$ changes with $x$ (left of Figure 4). student's insight in class
  2. Take any function $T\in\mathcal T$ (a map from $x$ to $\mathrm{dom}f^*$; it need not be convex — only $f$ is). At every single $x$: sup is an upper bound $$g\big(x,T(x)\big)\;\le\;\sup_t g(x,t)$$
  3. Multiply by $q(x)\ge0$ (does not flip the inequality). $q\ge0$ $$q(x)\,g\big(x,T(x)\big)\;\le\;q(x)\sup_t g(x,t)$$
  4. Integrate over $x$. integral is monotone $$\int q\,g\big(x,T(x)\big)dx\;\le\;\int q\sup_t g\,dx=D_f(P\|Q)$$ The right side is $D_f$ by section 3 (c).
  5. The left side is $\le D_f$ for every $T\in\mathcal T$, hence so is its supremum. sup of lower bounds $$\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\int q\,g\big(x,T(x)\big)dx\;\le\;D_f(P\|Q)$$
  6. Equality: find the pointwise maximiser. $g$ is concave in $t$ ($f^*$ convex), so set $\partial_tg=u-f^{*\prime}(t)=0$. By the envelope computation $f^*(t)=u(t)\,t-f(u(t))$ with $f'(u(t))=t$, we get $f^{*\prime}(t)=u(t)$; hence the stationarity condition says first-order condition $$t^*(x)=f'\big(u(x)\big)=f'\!\Big(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\Big)=:T^*(x)$$
  7. If $T^*\in\mathcal T$ then step 2 holds with equality at every $x$, so equality holds in 4 and 5. If $T^*\notin\mathcal T$ (finite neural network) the gap is strictly positive in general: only a lower bound. class too small
▪

(Step filled in: the lecture says the inequality arises "because all integrand terms are non-negative"; the clean reason is the pointwise bound of step 2, valid regardless of sign. The professor also stressed this is not Jensen — it is "sup of a restricted class is at most the unrestricted sup".)

Worked numeric check ($f=u\log u$, so $f^*(t)=e^{t-1}$)

At a point with $u=p/q=2$: $g(t)=2t-e^{t-1}$. Choice $T=1$ gives $g=2-1=1$. Optimal $T^*=f'(2)=1+\ln2=1.693$ gives $g=3.386-2=\mathbf{1.386}=f(2)$. So $1\le1.386$: restricting $T$ can only lose. ✓

Remember for the exam
  • Scalar $t$ $\to$ function $T(x)$ (variational calculus: optimising over a "bag of functions" $\mathcal T$).
  • Equality iff $T^*(x)=f'(p/q)\in\mathcal T$; with a finite neural network you only get an inequality. Bigger networks give a tighter bound and (empirically) better samples.
  • It is "similar in spirit" to Jensen (convexity) but not Jensen's inequality.
Common mistake

Saying "we pulled the sup out of the integral so it is an equality". Sup does not commute with the integral for a restricted $T$; the inequality is the price paid, together with the new inner optimisation over $T$.

5. The variational lower bound on the f-divergence

Statement

$$D_f(P_X\|P_\theta)\;\ge\;\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\Big[\mathbb E_{X\sim P_X}\big[T(x)\big]-\mathbb E_{X\sim P_\theta}\big[f^*\big(T(x)\big)\big]\Big]$$ Class Aug 29, "The final variational lower bound" (steps 1–5 of the professor's sketch, spelled out), recapped Sept 05. Used later: it contains only expectations, so by the law of large numbers it can be computed from samples of the data and of the generator.

  1. Start from the definition and replace $f$ by its double conjugate with $u=p/q$. section 3: $f=f^{**}$ $$D_f(P_X\|P_\theta)=\int P_\theta(x)\,\sup_t\Big[t\,\frac{P_X(x)}{P_\theta(x)}-f^*(t)\Big]dx$$
  2. Push the sup outside, scalar $t\to$ function $T(x)$. section 4: $\ge$ for class $\mathcal T$ $$D_f\;\ge\;\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\int P_\theta(x)\Big[T(x)\frac{P_X(x)}{P_\theta(x)}-f^*\big(T(x)\big)\Big]dx$$
  3. Distribute $P_\theta(x)$ into the bracket; in the first term it cancels. $P_\theta\cdot\frac{P_X}{P_\theta}=P_X$ $$=\sup_T\int\Big[T(x)\,P_X(x)-P_\theta(x)\,f^*\big(T(x)\big)\Big]dx$$
  4. Split into two integrals. linearity of the integral $$=\sup_T\Big[\int T(x)P_X(x)\,dx-\int P_\theta(x)f^*\big(T(x)\big)dx\Big]$$
  5. Recognise each integral as an expectation. $\int h\,p=\mathbb E_p[h]$ $$=\sup_T\Big[\mathbb E_{X\sim P_X}[T(x)]-\mathbb E_{X\sim P_\theta}\big[f^*(T(x))\big]\Big]$$
  6. Estimate with samples $x_1,\dots,x_n\sim P_X$ and $\hat x_1,\dots,\hat x_m\sim P_\theta$ — no density needed. law of large numbers $$\approx\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nT(x_i)-\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^mf^*\big(T(\hat x_j)\big)$$
▪

Remember for the exam
  • Two costs: (1) equality became a lower bound; (2) a new optimisation over $T$ appeared — which is the discriminator.
  • The professor: "there is no intuition for the right-hand side — it is a pure mathematical equivalence derived from the definition of $D_f$." Do not try to invent one.
  • We can never compute $D_f$ itself, only this lower bound.
Common mistake

Putting $f^*$ on the data expectation: the data term is $\mathbb E_{P_X}[T]$ (linear), the model term is $\mathbb E_{P_\theta}[f^*(T)]$.

6. Variational Divergence Minimisation: the min–max problem and its gradients

Statement

Replacing $D_f$ by its variational bound turns generator learning into the saddle-point problem $\min_\theta\max_wJ(\theta,w)$ with $J=\mathbb E_{P_X}[T_w(x)]-\mathbb E_{Z}\big[f^*\big(T_w(G_\theta(z))\big)\big]$; the generator gradient involves only the second term. Class Aug 29 ("VDM", "Saddle point", "Implementing $T(X)$ as a network", training algorithm). Used later: the alternating algorithm of every GAN variant.

  1. Goal: $\theta^*=\arg\min_\theta D_f(P_X\|P_\theta)$. Neither density is known. problem set-up
  2. Substitute the bound of section 5 for $D_f$ (exactly $D_f$ if $T^*\in\mathcal T$). section 5 $$\theta^*=\arg\min_\theta\ \sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\Big[\mathbb E_{P_X}[T(x)]-\mathbb E_{P_\theta}[f^*(T(x))]\Big]$$
  3. The model expectation needs no density of $P_\theta$: $\hat X=G_\theta(Z)$ so change of variables (LOTUS) $$\mathbb E_{\hat X\sim P_\theta}\big[f^*(T(\hat x))\big]=\mathbb E_{Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)}\Big[f^*\big(T(G_\theta(z))\big)\Big]$$
  4. Represent $T$ by a network $T_w(x)=\sigma_f\big(V_w(x)\big)$ — a linear last layer $V_w$ followed by an $f$-specific activation that forces the output into $\mathrm{dom}f^*$. neural-network class $\mathcal T$ $$J(\theta,w)=\mathbb E_{X\sim P_X}\big[T_w(x)\big]-\mathbb E_Z\Big[f^*\big(T_w(G_\theta(z))\big)\Big],\qquad \min_\theta\max_wJ(\theta,w)$$
  5. Critic gradient, $\theta$ fixed (ascent on $w$). chain rule $$\nabla_wJ=\mathbb E_{P_X}\big[\nabla_wT_w(x)\big]-\mathbb E_Z\Big[f^{*\prime}\big(T_w(\hat x)\big)\,\nabla_wT_w(\hat x)\Big],\quad\hat x=G_\theta(z)$$ estimated on independent batches of $B_1$ real and $B_2$ generated samples (no pairing needed). LLN
  6. Generator gradient, $w$ fixed (descent on $\theta$). The first term does not contain $\theta$, so it drops: $\theta$ only enters through $G_\theta$ $$\nabla_\theta J=-\,\mathbb E_Z\Big[f^{*\prime}\big(T_w(\hat x)\big)\,\nabla_xT_w(x)\big|_{x=\hat x}\;\frac{\partial G_\theta(z)}{\partial\theta}\Big]$$ i.e. back-propagate through the critic into the generator without updating $w$.
  7. Alternate the updates: alternating saddle-point optimisation $$w\leftarrow w+\alpha\nabla_wJ,\qquad\theta\leftarrow\theta-\alpha\nabla_\theta J$$ After training the critic is discarded; sample $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and output $G_{\theta^*}(z)$.
▪

(Steps 3, 5, 6 are the chain-rule bookkeeping behind the lecture's forward/backward-pass walk-through. Honest caveat added: minimising a lower bound is only equivalent to minimising $D_f$ when the inner sup is attained, i.e. $T^*\in\mathcal T$; otherwise it is the best surrogate available, and the lecture notes only say a tighter bound gives better samples.)

Worked numeric check — a toy saddle

Take $J(\theta,w)=\theta^2-w^2$ (curves up in $\theta$, down in $w$, Figure 7). $\nabla_wJ=-2w$ pushes $w\to0$ by ascent; $\nabla_\theta J=2\theta$ pushes $\theta\to0$ by descent. Starting at $(\theta,w)=(1,1)$ with $\alpha=0.25$: $w\leftarrow1+0.25(-2)=0.5$, $\theta\leftarrow1-0.25(2)=0.5$; next $w=0.25,\theta=0.25$. Both converge to the saddle $(0,0)$. (Real GANs are not this benign — see section 11.)

Remember for the exam
  • $\theta$ (generator) minimises, $w$ (critic) maximises the same $J$: saddle-point = adversarial optimisation.
  • Generator update: gradient descent using only the second term, passing through the frozen discriminator. Discriminator update: gradient ascent, batches $B_1$ (real) and $B_2$ (fake) independent.
  • $T_w=\sigma_f(V_w)$: same architecture for every $f$, only the final activation changes (GAN is one "instance" of the VDM "class").
Common mistake

Updating $\theta$ with the discriminator gradient formula, or letting $w$ change during the generator step. Each step freezes the other network.

7. GAN as a special case of VDM

Statement

For $f(u)=u\log u-(u+1)\log\frac{u+1}{2}$ the conjugate is $f^*(t)=-\log(2-e^t)$ on $t\lt\log2$, and with $T=\log2+\log D$, $D=\sigma(V)$, the VDM objective becomes $J=\mathbb E_{P_X}[\log D]+\mathbb E_{P_\theta}[\log(1-D)]$ up to the constant $2\log2$. Class Aug 29 ("Deriving GAN as a special case of VDM", set as homework: "verify the algebra"), recap Sept 05. Used later: the GAN objective, its optimal critic (section 8), and the BCE implementation (section 9).

  1. The GAN generator function (a JS-like $f$): given in class $$f(u)=u\log u-(u+1)\log\frac{u+1}{2}$$ Compared with the JS $f$ of section 2 it lacks the factor $\tfrac12$: $f=2f_{JS}$, so $D_f=2\,\mathrm{JS}(P,Q)$.
  2. Derivative: the $(u+1)\cdot\frac1{u+1}$ term gives $1$ and cancels the $+1$ from $u\log u$. product rule $$f'(u)=\log u+1-\log\frac{u+1}{2}-1=\log\frac{2u}{u+1}$$
  3. Second derivative: convexity check $$f''(u)=\frac1u-\frac1{u+1}=\frac1{u(u+1)}>0,\qquad f(1)=1\cdot0-2\log1=0$$
  4. Conjugate: the maximiser solves $t=f'(u)$. section 3: $t^*=f'(u)$ $$e^t=\frac{2u}{u+1}\ \Longrightarrow\ u=\frac{e^t}{2-e^t},\qquad u>0\iff t\lt\log2$$ so $\mathrm{dom}f^*=(-\infty,\log2)$.
  5. Evaluate $f^*(t)=u\,t-f(u)=u\,f'(u)-f(u)$ at that $u$: insert $t=f'(u)$ $$f^*=u\log\frac{2u}{u+1}-u\log u+(u+1)\log\frac{u+1}2$$ $$\phantom{f^*}=u\log2-u\log(u+1)+(u+1)\log(u+1)-(u+1)\log2$$
  6. Collect terms: $-u\log(u+1)+(u+1)\log(u+1)=\log(u+1)$ $$f^*=\log(u+1)-\log2=\log\frac{u+1}{2}$$
  7. From step 4, $u+1=\dfrac{2}{2-e^t}$. add 1 to $u$ $$f^*(t)=\log\frac{1}{2-e^t}=-\log\big(2-e^t\big)$$
  8. Pick the activation so that $T$ lies in $\mathrm{dom}f^*$: $T=\log2+\log D$ with $D=\sigma(V)\in(0,1)$. Then $e^T=2D$ and $2-2D=2(1-D)$ $$f^*(T)=-\log\big(2-2D\big)=-\log2-\log(1-D)$$
  9. Plug into $J=\mathbb E_{P_X}[T]-\mathbb E_{P_\theta}[f^*(T)]$ (section 5/6): substitute $T$ and $f^*(T)$ $$J=\mathbb E_{P_X}\big[\log2+\log D\big]+\mathbb E_{P_\theta}\big[\log2+\log(1-D)\big]$$ $$\phantom{J}=\mathbb E_{P_X}[\log D]+\mathbb E_{P_\theta}[\log(1-D)]+2\log2$$
  10. Hence the bound reads $D_f(P_X\|P_\theta)\ge\mathcal J+\log4$ with $\mathcal J$ the familiar GAN objective; the additive constant $\log4$ changes neither $\arg\max_w$ nor $\arg\min_\theta$, so it is dropped. Writing $\hat x=G_\theta(z)$: $2\log2=\log4$, drop constant $$\mathcal J(\theta,w)=\mathbb E_{X\sim P_X}\big[\log D_w(x)\big]+\mathbb E_{Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)}\Big[\log\big(1-D_w(G_\theta(z))\big)\Big],\quad\min_\theta\max_w\mathcal J$$ GAN objective
▪

(ii) The Sept 05 recap writes $f(u)=u\log u-(u+1)\log(u+1)$ without the "/2": it differs from the Aug 29 form by $(u+1)\log2$, a linear term that shifts $D_f$ by $\int q\,(\tfrac pq+1)\log2=\log4$ only. 8. Optimal discriminator, the value −log 4 and the Jensen–Shannon divergence

Statement

For fixed generator, the maximiser of $\mathcal J(\theta,w)$ over all functions $D$ is $D^*(x)=\dfrac{p(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}$, and $\max_D\mathcal J=2\,\mathrm{JS}(P_X,P_\theta)-\log4\ge-\log4$ with equality iff $P_\theta=P_X$ (then $D^*\equiv\frac12$). Fills in what follows from the Aug 29 derivation (equality case $T^*=f'(p/q)$) and the Sept 05 "classifier fails iff distributions match" argument; the closed form was not written out in the notes. Used later: justifies the Turing-test reading and the stopping criterion $D\to\frac12$.

  1. For fixed $\theta$ write both expectations as integrals over $x$ (second via $\hat x=G_\theta(z)$, $\hat x\sim q$). section 6, step 3 $$\mathcal J(D)=\int\Big[p(x)\log D(x)+q(x)\log\big(1-D(x)\big)\Big]dx$$
  2. The integrand at each $x$ involves only the number $D(x)$, so maximise pointwise. With $a=p(x)$, $b=q(x)$ and $y=D(x)\in(0,1)$: pointwise maximisation (as in section 4) $$h(y)=a\log y+b\log(1-y)$$
  3. Stationary point: $h'(y)=0$ $$h'(y)=\frac ay-\frac b{1-y}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ a(1-y)=by\ \Longrightarrow\ y^*=\frac a{a+b}$$
  4. It is a maximum: $h''<0$ $$h''(y)=-\frac a{y^2}-\frac b{(1-y)^2}\lt0$$
  5. Therefore, for every $x$: steps 2–4 $$D^*(x)=\frac{p(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}=\sigma\!\Big(\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\Big)$$ This equals $e^{T^*}/2$ with $T^*=f'(p/q)=\log\frac{2p}{p+q}$ of section 7, as it must.
  6. Plug back (the value of the inner max): insert $D^*$, $1-D^*=\frac q{p+q}$ $$\max_D\mathcal J=\int\Big[p\log\frac p{p+q}+q\log\frac q{p+q}\Big]dx$$
  7. Introduce the mixture $m=\frac{p+q}2$, so $\frac p{p+q}=\frac{p}{2m}$ and $\log\frac{p}{p+q}=\log\frac pm-\log2$. $p+q=2m$ $$\max_D\mathcal J=\int p\log\frac pm+\int q\log\frac qm-\log2\!\int p-\log2\!\int q$$ $$\phantom{\max_D\mathcal J}=\mathrm{KL}(P\|M)+\mathrm{KL}(Q\|M)-2\log2$$
  8. Using $\mathrm{JS}=\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(P\|M)+\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(Q\|M)$: definition of JS $$\max_D\mathcal J=2\,\mathrm{JS}(P_X,P_\theta)-\log4$$ (consistent with section 7: $D_f=2\mathrm{JS}$ and bound $=\mathcal J+\log4$ tight at $D^*$.)
  9. JS is non-negative and zero iff $P=Q$ (sections 1–2, JS is an $f$-divergence). Hence the generator's minimum of $\max_D\mathcal J$ is $-\log4$, reached iff $P_\theta=P_X$ (section 1), where $D^*=\frac p{p+q}$ with $p=q$ $$p=q\ \Longrightarrow\ D^*(x)=\frac{p}{2p}=\frac12\ \ \text{for all }x,\qquad\mathcal J=\log\tfrac12+\log\tfrac12=-\log4$$
▪

(Whole section filled in — not written out in the notes. It also proves the Sept 05 claim: the best classifier can do no better than guessing exactly when $P_\theta=P_X$.)

Worked numeric checks

Gaussians $p=\mathcal N(0,1)$, $q=\mathcal N(1,1)$: $\log\frac pq=\frac{1-2x}{2}$, so $D^*(x)=\sigma\big(\tfrac{1-2x}2\big)$ — a logistic function, linear logit. $x=0$: $\sigma(0.5)=0.622$; $x=0.5$: $\sigma(0)=0.5$; $x=2$: $\sigma(-1.5)=0.182$.

Discrete $P=(0.7,0.3)$, $Q=(0.5,0.5)$: $D^*=(\tfrac{0.7}{1.2},\tfrac{0.3}{0.8})=(0.5833,\,0.375)$, $\mathcal J(D^*)=0.7\ln0.5833+0.3\ln0.375+0.5\ln0.4167+0.5\ln0.625=-1.3443$, and $2\mathrm{JS}-\ln4=0.0420-1.3863=-1.3443$ ✓. Any other critic is worse: $D=(0.6,0.4)\Rightarrow-1.3460$; $D\equiv\frac12\Rightarrow-1.3863$; $D=(0.9,0.1)\Rightarrow-1.9685$.

Remember for the exam
  • $D^*=\dfrac{p_{\text{data}}}{p_{\text{data}}+p_{\text{model}}}$ by pointwise maximisation of $a\log y+b\log(1-y)$.
  • Value at the optimum: $2\,\mathrm{JS}-\log4$; global minimum $-\log4$ at $P_\theta=P_X$, where $D^*=\tfrac12$ (the discriminator cannot tell real from fake).
Common mistake

Writing the minimum value as $0$ or $\log4$: the objective $\mathcal J$ lacks the $+\log4$ of section 7, so its minimum is $-\log4\approx-1.386$ (in nats).

9. The GAN loss is binary cross-entropy (and the non-saturating generator loss)

Statement

Maximising the GAN objective over the discriminator is the same as minimising BCE with label $1$ on real and $0$ on generated samples; and the "label-1" generator trick (non-saturating loss $-\log D(G(z))$) pushes in the same direction as $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ but with a gradient that does not vanish when the critic is winning. DL Tutorial 2 ("Loss function: BCE", "non-saturating loss"), linking to Aug 29/Sept 05. Used later: the PyTorch implementation BCEWithLogitsLoss.

  1. BCE for a label $y\in\{0,1\}$ and predicted probability $\hat y=D_w(x)$: definition $$\mathrm{BCE}(y,\hat y)=-\Big[y\log\hat y+(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)\Big]$$
  2. Real sample, $y=1$: only the first term survives, $\mathrm{BCE}=-\log D_w(x)$. Fake sample, $y=0$: only the second, $\mathrm{BCE}=-\log\big(1-D_w(\hat x)\big)$. $1-y=0$ or $y=0$
  3. Average on a real batch (all $y=1$) and a generated batch (all $y=0$) and add: sample-average (LLN) of the GAN terms $$L_D=-\Big[\tfrac1{B_1}\sum_{i}\log D_w(x_i)+\tfrac1{B_2}\sum_{j}\log\big(1-D_w(\hat x_j)\big)\Big]=-\widehat{\mathcal J}(\theta,w)$$
  4. Minimising $-\widehat{\mathcal J}$ over $w$ is maximising $\widehat{\mathcal J}$. $\arg\min(-g)=\arg\max g$ So the discriminator step "minimise BCE" is exactly the "ascent on $\mathcal J$" step of section 6.
  5. With a logit $V$ and $D=\sigma(V)$ (what BCEWithLogitsLoss computes stably): $\sigma(V)=\frac1{1+e^{-V}}$ $$-\log\sigma(V)=\log\big(1+e^{-V}\big),\qquad-\log\big(1-\sigma(V)\big)=\log\big(1+e^{V}\big)$$
  6. Generator, saturating (theory) loss $L_1=\log(1-D)$, $D=\sigma(V)$ on a fake. $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$ $$\frac{\partial L_1}{\partial V}=-\frac{\sigma'(V)}{1-\sigma(V)}=-\sigma(V)=-D$$
  7. Generator, non-saturating loss $L_2=-\log D$ (BCE with target $y=1$ on the fake). same derivative rule $$\frac{\partial L_2}{\partial V}=-\frac{\sigma'(V)}{\sigma(V)}=-\big(1-\sigma(V)\big)=-(1-D)$$
  8. Both derivatives are negative, so gradient descent on either raises $V$, i.e. raises $D$ on fakes — the same goal. But early in training $D\approx0$: $|\partial L_1/\partial V|=D\approx0$ (vanishing signal) while $|\partial L_2/\partial V|=1-D\approx1$. comparison
▪

(Steps 1–4 are from the tutorial; steps 5–7 re-derive the tutorial's $P$-space argument in logit space. $L_2$ is a heuristic replacement of the objective, not part of the min–max derivation of sections 5–8.)

Worked numeric check ($D=P=0.1$ on a fake)

Tutorial's derivative w.r.t. $P$: $\frac{dL_1}{dP}=-\frac1{1-P}=-1.11$ and $\frac{dL_2}{dP}=-\frac1P=-10$. Multiply by the sigmoid slope $P(1-P)=0.09$ to get the logit derivatives: $-1.11\times0.09=-0.10=-D$ ✓ and $-10\times0.09=-0.90=-(1-D)$ ✓. The non-saturating loss is nine times "pushier" here. For BCE values: $-\ln0.1=2.303$ (real image scored 0.1) vs $-\ln0.9=0.105$ (scored 0.9).

Remember for the exam
  • GAN discriminator loss = standard BCE; $y=1$ real, $y=0$ fake. Implemented with BCEWithLogitsLoss (sigmoid + BCE fused, numerically stable).
  • Generator step uses target 1 on fake images (non-saturating) — intentional, not a bug.
  • Detach the fakes in the discriminator step; do not detach in the generator step (gradients must reach $\theta$).
Common mistake

Thinking the label-1 trick means the discriminator step is wrong. In the discriminator step fakes get label $0$; only the generator step flips it to $1$.

10. The classifier-guided interpretation reaches the same objective

Statement

Training $D_w$ as the maximum-likelihood classifier of "real vs generated" and letting the generator do the opposite gives exactly $\min_\theta\max_w\mathcal J$; the classifier's ideal output is the Bayes posterior $\Pr(Y{=}1\mid x)=p/(p+q)$ and "classifier cannot discriminate" ($D\equiv\frac12$) happens iff $P_\theta=P_X$. Class Sept 05, "Classifier-guided generative sampler" and "Formalizing the classifier-guided interpretation" (the original 2014 GAN narrative). Used later: the standard way GANs are explained; the interpretation is valid only for this $f$ (range $[0,1]$).

  1. Build a labelled problem with equal class priors: $Y=1$ (real) with $X\mid Y{=}1\sim P_X$ and $Y=0$ (generated) with $X\mid Y{=}0\sim P_\theta$. $D_w(x)$ models $\Pr(Y{=}1\mid X{=}x)$ (range $[0,1]$ is allowed because of the sigmoid of section 7). set-up
  2. Likelihood of one labelled example under the Bernoulli model: Bernoulli pmf $$D_w(x)^{y}\big(1-D_w(x)\big)^{1-y}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{log-likelihood }\ y\log D_w(x)+(1-y)\log\big(1-D_w(x)\big)$$ which is the negative BCE of section 9.
  3. Average over the data-generating process (real with prob. $\frac12$, generated with prob. $\frac12$): law of total expectation $$\mathbb E\big[\text{log-lik}\big]=\tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{X\sim P_X}[\log D_w(x)]+\tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{X\sim P_\theta}[\log(1-D_w(x))]=\tfrac12\,\mathcal J(\theta,w)$$ The constant $\tfrac12$ does not change the argmax (the notes' $\mathcal J$ drops it).
  4. Classifier: maximum likelihood $\Rightarrow$ $w^*=\arg\max_w\mathcal J$. MLE for binary classification
  5. Generator: its role is to undo the classifier, so it minimises what the classifier maximises: adversary $$\theta^*=\arg\min_\theta\mathcal J(\theta,w)$$ This is the same $\mathcal J$ and the same min–max as in section 7: two stories, one algorithm.
  6. Ideal classifier: by Bayes' rule with equal priors Bayes' rule $$\Pr(Y{=}1\mid x)=\frac{\tfrac12p(x)}{\tfrac12p(x)+\tfrac12q(x)}=\frac{p(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}=D^*(x)$$ agreeing with the pointwise maximiser of section 8.
  7. Stopping criterion: $D^*(x)=\tfrac12$ for all $x$ iff $p=q$ (solve $\tfrac p{p+q}=\tfrac12$). cross-multiply So "no classifier can beat chance" $\Leftrightarrow$ $P_\theta=P_X$ — whereas defeating one fixed classifier is not enough (Figure 11).
▪

(The lecture presents steps 1–5 verbally and states the result; the likelihood and Bayes steps 2, 3, 6 are written out here — filled in. Historical note from the lecture: the 2014 GAN paper used this narrative; the f-GAN paper later showed it is the special case of VDM above.)

Worked numeric check

Equal priors on the two-point example $P=(0.7,0.3)$, $Q=(0.5,0.5)$: at $x_1$ the posterior is $\frac{0.7}{0.7+0.5}=0.583$, at $x_2$ it is $\frac{0.3}{0.3+0.5}=0.375$ — the same $D^*$ as section 8. If the generator matched the data, $Q=P$: posterior $=\frac p{2p}=0.5$ at both points and $\mathcal J=2\ln0.5=-1.386=-\ln4$.

Remember for the exam
  • Both narratives (f-divergence minimisation and classifier guidance) yield the same loss. The professor prefers: "the classifier is a consequence of constructing the lower bound, not the starting point."
  • Classifier reading holds only for the GAN-type $f$; least-squares GAN has a regressor instead. The f-divergence reading is always valid.
  • Alternate between classifier and generator because beating a single fixed classifier does not imply $P_\theta=P_X$.
Common mistake

Claiming "when the discriminator outputs $0.5$ on some samples the distributions match". The criterion is: no discriminator in the class (and in particular the optimal one) can do better than $\tfrac12$ everywhere.

11. Why a fixed discriminator is not enough (mode collapse remark)

Statement

Against a frozen discriminator $D$, the generator loss $\mathbb E_{Q}[\log(1-D(x))]$ is minimised by a distribution $Q$ that puts all its mass on the maximiser of $D$ — a single point. Class Sept 05, "Mode collapse and training-ratio heuristics" (the professor confirmed that oscillation of $P_{\theta_1}\leftrightarrow P_{\theta_2}$ is mode collapse); the proof below is added to make the remark rigorous. Used later: motivates keeping the discriminator weak/alternating and variants with several discriminators.

  1. Fix $D$ with values in $(0,1)$ and let $D_{\max}=\sup_xD(x)$. frozen critic
  2. $\log(1-y)$ is decreasing in $y$, and $D(x)\le D_{\max}$, so for every $x$: monotonicity $$\log\big(1-D(x)\big)\;\ge\;\log\big(1-D_{\max}\big)$$
  3. Take the expectation under any distribution $Q$ of generated samples: expectation is monotone $$\mathbb E_{Q}\big[\log(1-D(x))\big]\;\ge\;\log(1-D_{\max})$$
  4. Equality iff $\log(1-D(x))=\log(1-D_{\max})$ $Q$-almost surely, i.e. $Q$ is supported on $\arg\max D$. A point mass at an argmax achieves it. equality case $$\min_Q\mathbb E_Q\big[\log(1-D(x))\big]=\log(1-D_{\max}),\quad\text{attained by }Q=\delta_{x^*},\ x^*\in\arg\max D$$
  5. Consequence for alternating training: after the generator beats $D_1$ it may sit on a spike where $D_1$ is large; the retrained $D_2$ then scores that spike as fake and the generator jumps to $D_2$'s favourite point, and so on — the oscillation of Figure 11 ($P_{\theta_1}\to P_{\theta_2}\to\dots$) which the professor named mode collapse. best response to a frozen $D$
▪

(Added proof. In the lecture this was an intuition/heuristic: keep the discriminator comparatively weak (not over-trained) so the generator cannot just exploit one decision boundary, at the cost of slower convergence; a second remedy is several discriminators. Note: the Aug 29 notes mention updating the discriminator 5 times per generator update, while the Sept 05 notes describe training the generator more often (5:1) with the "keep the discriminator weak" rationale — the two note files disagree on the direction of the ratio; the proof above only supports the qualitative statement.)

Worked numeric check

Frozen $D(x_1)=0.9$, $D(x_2)=0.3$. A generator spread over both points ($Q=(\frac12,\frac12)$) has $\mathbb E_Q[\ln(1-D)]=\tfrac12\ln0.1+\tfrac12\ln0.7=-1.230$; the spike $Q=\delta_{x_1}$ has $\ln0.1=\mathbf{-2.303}$, lower (better for the generator), and equals $\ln(1-D_{\max})$ ✓. A data distribution with half its mass at $x_2$ is thus completely ignored.

Remember for the exam
  • Moving the generated samples to the "real side" of one classifier does not mean $P_\theta=P_X$; the generator and classifier must alternate, and success means no classifier can separate them.
  • Mode collapse = oscillation/concentration; remedies named in class: control the discriminator's strength/training ratio, use multiple discriminators.
Common mistake

Believing a stronger discriminator is always better. Against a very strong (or frozen) discriminator the generator can win by collapsing onto one point, so strength must be balanced against the generator.
