DDPM: closed forms and the training loss

Every derivation between the three-term DDPM loss and the final training and sampling algorithms: the one-shot forward marginal, the exact forward posterior, KL to regression, the noise-prediction loss, the four equivalent parameterisations, the sampling update, and the guidance score decomposition. Each proof has a picture, line-by-line algebra with the reason for every line, and a worked numeric check.

Where this page starts

The ELBO derivation itself (Bayes anchoring on $x_0$, telescoping) is on another page. We start from its result, the three-term loss $$\mathcal L=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q(x_1|x_0)}[\log p_\theta(x_0|x_1)]}_{\text{reconstruction}}-\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_T|x_0)\,\|\,p(x_T)\big)}_{\text{prior matching}}-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb E_{q(x_t|x_0)}\Big[\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t)\big)}_{\text{denoising matching}}\Big]$$ and make every ingredient of it computable. The professor listed four of these derivations as exam-style exercises in the Oct 3 lecture (marked below).

Notation (follows the lectures)
  • One forward step (Sept 19 and Oct 3 lectures): $x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t$, i.e. $q(x_t|x_{t-1})=\mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\,(1-\alpha_t)I\big)$. The Oct 10 tutorial writes the variance as $\beta_t=1-\alpha_t$; we use both, with $\beta_t:=1-\alpha_t$.
  • $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s$, and $\bar\alpha_0:=1$. Useful identity used constantly: $\bar\alpha_t=\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}$.
  • The lectures write $X_t$ (capital); we write $x_t$. The posterior $q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)=\mathcal N(\tilde\mu_t,\tilde\beta_tI)$: the lecture calls $\tilde\mu_t=\mu_q$ and $\tilde\beta_tI=\Sigma_q$.
  • Every Gaussian here has an isotropic covariance $cI$, so each coordinate (pixel) is independent and the algebra can be done on one coordinate; vectors behave identically with $\|\cdot\|^2$ in place of squares.

1. Closed-form forward marginal

Statement

Composing the $t$ one-step Gaussians gives, directly from $x_0$, $q(x_t|x_0)=\mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)I\big)$, equivalently $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. Source: Oct 3 lecture ("Closed-Form Forward Marginal"), assigned exercise 2. Used later: training builds any $x_t$ in one shot (Sections 3 to 5), and $\bar\alpha_T\approx0$ justifies starting generation from $\mathcal N(0,I)$ (Section 6).

  1. Claim to prove by induction on $t$: $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\bar\epsilon_t$ with $\bar\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ built from $\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_t$. induction hypothesis $P(t)$
  2. Base case $t=1$: $\bar\alpha_1=\alpha_1$, so the one-step rule already reads $$x_1=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_1}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_1}\,\epsilon_1.$$ definition of one step
  3. Inductive step. Assume $P(t-1)$: $x_{t-1}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\bar\epsilon_{t-1}$, where $\bar\epsilon_{t-1}$ depends only on $\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_{t-1}$, hence is independent of the fresh $\epsilon_t$. hypothesis plus independence of new noise
  4. Substitute into the one-step rule $x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t$: $$x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;x_0+\underbrace{\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}\;\bar\epsilon_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\;\epsilon_t}_{N}.$$ plug in $x_{t-1}$
  5. The signal coefficient: $\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}=\bar\alpha_t$, so the $x_0$ term is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0$. $\bar\alpha_t=\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}$
  6. $N$ is a sum of two independent zero-mean Gaussians, hence Gaussian with zero mean and the sum of the variances: $$\operatorname{Var}(N)=\big[\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})+(1-\alpha_t)\big]I.$$ sum of independent Gaussians: variances add
  7. Simplify the bracket: $$\alpha_t-\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}+1-\alpha_t=1-\bar\alpha_t.$$ the $\alpha_t$ terms cancel
  8. So $N\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\bar\epsilon_t$ for a single $\bar\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, which is $P(t)$. By induction the claim holds for all $t$, and therefore $$q(x_t|x_0)=\mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\;(1-\bar\alpha_t)I\big).$$ a Gaussian is fixed by mean and covariance
  9. Corollary (used for the prior): $\bar\alpha_T=\prod_s\alpha_s\to0$ for a long schedule, so $q(x_T|x_0)\to\mathcal N(0,I)$ whatever $x_0$ was. each $\alpha_s<1$, product shrinks
▪
Worked numeric check (running example)

Compose two steps by hand, $x_2=\sqrt{0.8}\,x_1+\sqrt{0.2}\,\epsilon_2$ and $x_1=\sqrt{0.9}\,x_0+\sqrt{0.1}\,\epsilon_1$. Signal coefficient: $\sqrt{0.8\cdot0.9}=\sqrt{0.72}=0.8485=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_2}$. Noise variance: $0.8\cdot0.1+0.2=0.28=1-0.72=1-\bar\alpha_2$. One more step to $t=3$: $0.7\cdot0.28+0.3=0.496=1-0.504=1-\bar\alpha_3$. All three agree with the one-shot formula (verified numerically).

Remember for the exam

Name the two facts used: (i) a linear map of a Gaussian is Gaussian, (ii) independent Gaussians add means and variances. State why one never simulates $x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}$ in training: any noise level costs one operation. The professor assigned this derivation (expand the recursion) as an exam-type exercise.

Common mistakes

(a) Writing the coefficient as $\bar\alpha_t$ instead of $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$: $\bar\alpha_t$ is the weight of the variance, its square root multiplies the signal. (b) Forgetting that the noise in the closed form is a new combined $\epsilon$, not $\epsilon_t$ of the last step. (c) Treating $\bar\alpha_t$ as a sum; it is a running product.

2. The exact forward posterior

Statement

$q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)=\mathcal N\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0),\,\tilde\beta_tI\big)$ with $\tilde\mu_t=\dfrac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\dfrac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t$ and $\tilde\beta_t=\dfrac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t$. Source: Oct 3 lecture ("Exact Forward Posterior"), assigned exercise 3; formulas restated in the Oct 10 tutorial. Used later: it is the "known denoising" target that $p_\theta$ must imitate (Sections 3 to 6).

  1. Bayes' rule with $x_0$ carried along (three-variable form from the lecture): $$q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)=\frac{q(x_t|x_{t-1},x_0)\;q(x_{t-1}|x_0)}{q(x_t|x_0)}.$$ Bayes rule, everything conditioned on $x_0$
  2. $q(x_t|x_{t-1},x_0)=q(x_t|x_{t-1})$. Markov: $x_t$ depends on the past only through $x_{t-1}$
  3. The denominator contains no $x_{t-1}$, so as a function of $x_{t-1}$ it is just a normalising constant: $$q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)\;\propto\;q(x_t|x_{t-1})\;q(x_{t-1}|x_0).$$ drop constants in $x_{t-1}$
  4. Both factors are Gaussian (one-step rule, and Section 1 at $t-1$). Writing out their exponents: $$q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)\propto\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\Big[\tfrac{\|x_t-\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}\|^2}{\beta_t}+\tfrac{\|x_{t-1}-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0\|^2}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\Big]\Big).$$ Gaussian density; normalisers do not involve $x_{t-1}$
  5. Expand both squares and collect powers of $x_{t-1}$. The bracket equals $$A\,\|x_{t-1}\|^2-2\,B^\top x_{t-1}+C,\qquad A=\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t}+\frac{1}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}},\quad B=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_t}{\beta_t}+\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}},$$ with $C$ free of $x_{t-1}$. expand squares; the cross term of the first square is $-2\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_t^\top x_{t-1}/\beta_t$
  6. Simplify $A$ over a common denominator: $$A=\frac{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})+\beta_t}{\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})},$$ because $\alpha_t-\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}+1-\alpha_t=1-\bar\alpha_t$. $\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}=\bar\alpha_t$
  7. Complete the square: $$A\|x_{t-1}\|^2-2B^\top x_{t-1}=A\Big\|x_{t-1}-\frac{B}{A}\Big\|^2-\frac{\|B\|^2}{A}.$$ complete the square; the last term is constant in $x_{t-1}$
  8. Hence the density is $\propto\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{A}{2}\|x_{t-1}-B/A\|^2\big)$, a Gaussian with covariance $A^{-1}I$ and mean $B/A$. recognise the Gaussian kernel; normalisation is automatic
  9. Variance: $$\tilde\beta_t=\frac1A=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t.$$ invert Step 6
  10. Mean: multiply $B$ by $1/A$: $$\tilde\mu_t=\frac{\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\Big[\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_t}{\beta_t}+\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\Big]=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t+\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0.$$ distribute; $\beta_t$ cancels in the $x_t$ term and $(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})$ in the $x_0$ term
▪
Reading the result

Precision-weighted average. Steps 5 to 10 say: precisions add ($A=1/\mathrm{Var}_B+1/\mathrm{Var}_A$ with $\alpha_t/\beta_t$ for B and $1/(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})$ for A), and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the two factor means $x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$ (B) and $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0$ (A). The lecture's intuition is the same: $\mu_q$ is a known linear combination of the clean $x_0$ and the noisy $x_t$, and the variance depends only on the schedule, not on the data.

Boundary check at $t=1$ (step filled in, not written out in class). With $\bar\alpha_0=1$ we get $\tilde\beta_1=0$ and $\tilde\mu_1=x_0$: given $x_1$ and $x_0$, the previous state is $x_0$ itself. This is why the $t=1$ term is handled as the reconstruction term, not as a KL.

Worked numeric check ($t=2$, $x_0=1$, $x_2=0.5$)

Precisions: $\alpha_2/\beta_2=0.8/0.2=4$ and $1/(1-\bar\alpha_1)=1/0.1=10$, so $A=14$ and $\tilde\beta_2=1/14=0.0714$. Formula check: $\frac{1-0.9}{1-0.72}\cdot0.2=\frac{0.1}{0.28}\cdot0.2=0.0714$. The mean from the closed formula: $\frac{\sqrt{0.9}\cdot0.2}{0.28}\cdot1+\frac{\sqrt{0.8}\cdot0.1}{0.28}\cdot0.5=0.6776+0.1597=0.8373$. From $B/A$: $B=\sqrt{0.8}\cdot0.5/0.2+\sqrt{0.9}\cdot1/0.1=2.2361+9.4868=11.7229$ and $B/A=0.8373$. Identical (verified numerically); this is the filled posterior curve of Figure 2.

Remember for the exam

Write the three-variable Bayes identity, say why $x_0$ is added (anchoring on the known training example gives a tractable target), name the Markov step, show the exponent with the two quadratics, and finish with "complete the square in $x_{t-1}$". Know both final formulas by heart (they are repeated in the tutorial) and that $\tilde\beta_t$ does not depend on $x_t$ or $x_0$.

Common mistakes

(a) Using $q(x_{t-1}|x_0)$ with variance $\bar\alpha_{t-1}$ instead of $1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}$. (b) Mixing up which coefficient goes with $x_0$ and which with $x_t$: the $x_0$ weight has $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t$ in the numerator, the $x_t$ weight has $\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})$. (c) Forgetting that the posterior is only tractable given $x_0$; without $x_0$ it would need the whole data distribution.

3. From KL divergence to regression

Statement

For two Gaussians with the same covariance $\Sigma$: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu_q,\Sigma)\,\|\,\mathcal N(\mu_\theta,\Sigma)\big)=\tfrac12(\mu_q-\mu_\theta)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_q-\mu_\theta)$. With $\Sigma=\sigma_t^2I$ each denoising term becomes $\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta(x_t,t)\|^2$. Source: Oct 3 lecture ("From KL Divergence to Regression"), assigned exercise 4; the Oct 10 tutorial writes $\mathcal L_{t-1}=\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta\|^2+C$. Used later: this is why DDPM training is plain mean-squared-error regression.

  1. Start from the definition (here $x\in\mathbb R^d$): $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)=\mathbb E_{x\sim q}\big[\log q(x)-\log p(x)\big].$$ definition of KL
  2. Write the Gaussian log-density: $$\log\mathcal N(x;\mu,\Sigma)=-\tfrac d2\log2\pi-\tfrac12\log|\Sigma|-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu).$$ Gaussian density
  3. Subtract the two log-densities. The first two terms are identical because the covariances are equal, so they cancel: $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{q}\Big[(x-\mu_\theta)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_\theta)-(x-\mu_q)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_q)\Big].$$ equal $\Sigma$: constants and log-determinants cancel
  4. Let $\Delta=\mu_q-\mu_\theta$ and write $x-\mu_\theta=(x-\mu_q)+\Delta$. Expanding the first quadratic form: $$(x-\mu_\theta)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_\theta)=(x-\mu_q)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_q)+2\Delta^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu_q)+\Delta^\top\Sigma^{-1}\Delta.$$ $(a+b)^\top M(a+b)$ with $M$ symmetric
  5. The first term cancels the subtracted quadratic. The cross term has expectation zero because $\mathbb E_q[x-\mu_q]=0$: $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\tfrac12\,\Delta^\top\Sigma^{-1}\Delta.$$ $x\sim q$ has mean $\mu_q$
  6. Specialise to $\Sigma=\sigma_t^2I$: $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\|\mu_q-\mu_\theta\|^2.$$ $\Sigma^{-1}=\sigma_t^{-2}I$
  7. Apply it to the denoising term. Both $q(x_{t-1}|x_t,x_0)$ (mean $\tilde\mu_t$, variance $\tilde\beta_tI$ from Section 2) and the model $p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t)=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\sigma_t^2I)$ with $\sigma_t^2$ set equal to $\tilde\beta_t$ (the lecture fixes $\Sigma_q$, not learned) have equal covariance for every $x_t$, so $$\mathcal L_{t-1}=\mathbb E_{q(x_t|x_0)}\Big[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\big\|\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)-\mu_\theta(x_t,t)\big\|^2\Big].$$ Step 6 applied inside the outer expectation over $x_t$
  8. The remaining pieces: the prior-matching term has no $\theta$, and the reconstruction term is also a squared error, $-\log p_\theta(x_0|x_1)=\frac{\|x_0-\mu_\theta(x_1,1)\|^2}{2\sigma_1^2}+\text{const}$ (consistent with $\tilde\mu_1=x_0$ from the Section 2 boundary check). So training the reverse model is regression of $\mu_\theta$ onto $\tilde\mu_t$ with a $t$-dependent weight. Gaussian log-likelihood is a squared error; the lecture notes the reconstruction term can be dropped in practice
▪
Worked numeric check

At $t=2$ take $\tilde\mu_2=0.8373$ (Section 2), a model mean $\mu_\theta=0.80$ and $\sigma_2^2=\tilde\beta_2=0.0714$. Then $\Delta=0.0373$ and $\mathrm{KL}=\frac{0.0373^2}{2\cdot0.0714}=0.00977$. Direct numerical integration of $\int q\log(q/p)\,dx$ gives $0.00977$ as well (verified).

Remember for the exam

The three words that carry the proof: equal covariance kills the log-det and the quadratic in $x-\mu_q$; the cross term vanishes by zero mean; what survives is a Mahalanobis distance of the means. The reason for fixing the variance (lecture): fewer parameters, cheaper, empirically effective, and it reduces the problem to mean regression.

Common mistake

Applying $\tfrac12\Delta^\top\Sigma^{-1}\Delta$ when the variances differ. If $\sigma_\theta^2$ were learned there would also be $\log(\sigma_\theta^2/\sigma_q^2)$ and variance-ratio terms (step filled in). Also, the weight $1/(2\sigma_t^2)$ changes with $t$; dropping it changes how much each noise level counts in the loss.

4. The noise-prediction form of the mean and the simplified loss

Statement

Substituting $x_0=(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ into $\tilde\mu_t$ gives $\tilde\mu_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon\Big)$. Parameterising $\mu_\theta$ the same way with a network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$ turns $\mathcal L_{t-1}$ into $\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\,\mathbb E\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta\|^2$, and dropping the weight gives $\mathcal L_{\text{simple}}=\mathbb E\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\|^2$. Source: Oct 3 lecture ("Equivalent DDPM Parameterizations", noise prediction) and Oct 10 tutorial §7 to §9; the algebra and the weight are written out here (step filled in, not worked out in class).

  1. Solve the closed form of Section 1 for the clean sample: $$x_0=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.$$ invert $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$
  2. Substitute into the posterior mean (Section 2): $$\tilde\mu_t=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\cdot\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t.$$ eliminate $x_0$
  3. Use $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}=1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$: $$\tilde\mu_t=\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\big(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon\big)+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t.$$ $\bar\alpha_t=\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}$
  4. Collect the coefficient of $x_t$ over the common denominator $\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_t)$: $$\frac{\beta_t+\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_t)}=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_t)}=\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}.$$ $\beta_t+\alpha_t-\bar\alpha_t=1-\bar\alpha_t$
  5. The coefficient of $\epsilon$ is $-\dfrac{\beta_t\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_t)}=-\dfrac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}$. Together $$\boxed{\;\tilde\mu_t=\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon\Big)\;}$$ $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}/(1-\bar\alpha_t)=1/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$
  6. Choose the model mean with the same functional form, with the network supplying the noise: $$\mu_\theta(x_t,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\Big).$$ parameterisation choice; $x_t$ is an input so it needs no prediction
  7. Subtract: the $x_t$ parts cancel, $$\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta=\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\big(\epsilon_\theta-\epsilon\big).$$ same $x_t$ in both
  8. Insert into the regression loss of Section 3: $$\mathcal L_{t-1}=\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\;\mathbb E_{x_0,\epsilon}\big\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\big\|^2,\qquad x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon.$$ square the norm; the expectation over $x_t\sim q(x_t|x_0)$ becomes one over $\epsilon$ by Section 1
  9. With $\sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t$ the weight simplifies to $\dfrac{\beta_t}{2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}$ (step filled in). substitute $\sigma_t^2$ and cancel $(1-\bar\alpha_t)\beta_t$
  10. The weights depend only on $t$. Dropping them gives the simplified objective, with $t$ drawn uniformly (the sum over $t$ is $T$ times an expectation over uniform $t$, and the constant $T$ is absorbed into the learning rate): $$\boxed{\;\mathcal L_{\text{simple}}=\mathbb E_{t,x_0,\epsilon}\Big\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\;t\big)\Big\|^2\;}$$ the tutorial: constants may be kept for exact ELBO weighting or omitted for a simple regression objective
▪
The resulting training algorithm (Oct 3 and Oct 10)
  1. Draw a clean example $x_0$, a time $t$ uniformly from $\{1,\dots,T\}$, and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$.
  2. Build $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$ directly (Section 1), with no simulation of earlier steps.
  3. Feed $(x_t,t)$ to the time-conditioned U-Net and take a gradient step on $\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\|^2$.

One network serves every $t$; the time embedding tells it the noise level. Pairing $x_{50}$ with the embedding of $t=45$ is a serious mismatch.

Worked numeric check ($t=2$, $x_0=1$, $x_2=0.5$)

The noise that produced $x_2$: $\epsilon=(x_2-\sqrt{0.72}\cdot1)/\sqrt{0.28}=(0.5-0.8485)/0.5292=-0.6587$. The $\epsilon$-form of the mean: $\tilde\mu_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{0.8}}\big(0.5-\frac{0.2}{0.5292}\cdot(-0.6587)\big)=\frac{0.5+0.2490}{0.8944}=0.8373$, equal to the $x_0$-form $0.8373$ of Section 2. Weight check: suppose the network is off by $0.3$, $\epsilon_\theta=\epsilon+0.3$. Then $\frac{\|\tilde\mu_2-\mu_\theta\|^2}{2\tilde\beta_2}=0.1125$ and $\frac{\beta_2}{2\alpha_2(1-\bar\alpha_1)}\cdot0.3^2=\frac{0.2}{2\cdot0.8\cdot0.1}\cdot0.09=1.25\cdot0.09=0.1125$ (verified).

Remember for the exam

Show the substitution of $x_0$ and the one piece of algebra that makes the $x_t$ coefficient collapse to $1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$ ($\beta_t+\alpha_t-\bar\alpha_t=1-\bar\alpha_t$). State that the target of the network is the noise actually added to build $x_t$, and that $\mathcal L_{\text{simple}}$ drops the schedule-dependent weight.

Common mistakes

(a) Sign error: it is $x_t$ minus the scaled noise. (b) Confusing $\epsilon$ (single combined noise of the closed form) with the per-step noise $\epsilon_t$ of the chain. (c) Believing $\mathcal L_{\text{simple}}$ is the exact ELBO; it is a reweighted version of it.

5. Equivalence of the four parameterisations

Statement

For fixed $(x_t,t)$ the quantities $\mu$ (reverse mean), $\hat x_0$ (clean sample), $\epsilon$ (noise) and $s$ (score) determine each other by explicit affine formulas, so a network may predict any of them; the native losses differ only by a schedule-dependent weight. Source: Oct 3 lecture ("Equivalent DDPM Parameterizations") and Oct 10 tutorial §7 and §8, which gives the conversions and the numerical consistency tests. Used later: sampling always converts the output back to $\mu_\theta$ (Section 6).

  1. $\epsilon\leftrightarrow\hat x_0$. Both directions are the rearrangement of the closed form $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$ (Section 4, Step 1): $$\hat x_0=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_\theta}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},\qquad \epsilon=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}.$$ solve the forward equation either way
  2. The conditional score. From $q(x_t|x_0)=\mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_t)I)$, $$\log q(x_t|x_0)=-\frac{\|x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0\|^2}{2(1-\bar\alpha_t)}+\text{const}.$$ Gaussian log-density (Section 1)
  3. Differentiate in $x_t$: $$\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t|x_0)=-\frac{x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t}.$$ gradient of a quadratic
  4. Replace $x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$: $$\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t|x_0)=-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}},\qquad\text{so}\quad s_\theta=-\frac{\epsilon_\theta}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}},\ \ \epsilon_\theta=-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;s_\theta.$$ forward equation; the network's score is its noise rescaled
  5. Score to clean sample. Insert $\epsilon_\theta=-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,s_\theta$ in Step 1: $$\hat x_0=\frac{x_t+(1-\bar\alpha_t)\,s_\theta}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.$$ $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\cdot\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}=1-\bar\alpha_t$
  6. Clean sample to mean. Replace $x_0$ by $\hat x_0$ in the posterior mean of Section 2: $$\mu_\theta=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat x_0+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t=c_0\,\hat x_0+c_t\,x_t.$$ the model mean has the form of $\tilde\mu_t$ with $\hat x_0$ in place of $x_0$
  7. Noise to mean is Section 4, Step 5. Score to mean: put $\epsilon_\theta=-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,s_\theta$ into it, so $\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta=-\beta_t s_\theta$ and $$\mu_\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(x_t+\beta_t\,s_\theta\big).$$ substitute the score for the noise
  8. Inverse maps (needed to see that nothing is lost): from $\mu_\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta)$ solve $$\epsilon_\theta=\frac{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\big(x_t-\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\mu_\theta\big)}{\beta_t},\qquad \hat x_0=\frac{\mu_\theta-c_t\,x_t}{c_0}.$$ $c_0\neq0$ for $t\ge2$, so the affine map is invertible
  9. Loss weights. Using $\mathcal L_{t-1}=\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta\|^2$ and Step 6, $\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta=c_0(x_0-\hat x_0)$, so the $x_0$-loss weight is $c_0^2/(2\sigma_t^2)$. With Section 4 Step 7 (noise) and $\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta=-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,(s-s_\theta)$ (score), and $\sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t$: $$w^{\mu}=\frac1{2\sigma_t^2},\quad w^{\hat x_0}=\frac{\bar\alpha_{t-1}\beta_t}{2(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})(1-\bar\alpha_t)},\quad w^{\epsilon}=\frac{\beta_t}{2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})},\quad w^{s}=w^{\epsilon}\,(1-\bar\alpha_t).$$ $c_0^2=\bar\alpha_{t-1}\beta_t^2/(1-\bar\alpha_t)^2$ divided by $2\tilde\beta_t$; the score residual brings a factor $(1-\bar\alpha_t)$
  10. Interpretation of $w^{\hat x_0}$ (step filled in): with $\mathrm{SNR}_t=\bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)$ we have $\mathrm{SNR}_{t-1}-\mathrm{SNR}_t=\frac{\bar\alpha_{t-1}(1-\bar\alpha_t)-\bar\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})(1-\bar\alpha_t)}=\frac{\bar\alpha_{t-1}\beta_t}{(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})(1-\bar\alpha_t)}$, hence $w^{\hat x_0}=\tfrac12(\mathrm{SNR}_{t-1}-\mathrm{SNR}_t)$. $\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\bar\alpha_t=\bar\alpha_{t-1}\beta_t$

Beyond what was derived in class

The $v$-parameterisation is not in the lecture notes, so it is not treated. One remark, flagged as filled in: the minimiser of each squared-error loss is the conditional mean given $x_t$ (for example $\mathbb E[\epsilon|x_t]$). Because the conversions above are affine, the optimal outputs of the four parameterisations are the same function written in different coordinates; only the training weights (which noise levels the optimiser cares about) differ. The tutorial's observation that the $\epsilon$-model looked strongest in its short run is an empirical statement about those weights, not about expressiveness.

The key relations: $s=-\epsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ and $\hat x_0=(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$. State that every path ends at $\mu_\theta$ because the reverse Gaussian needs a mean; clamping $\hat x_0$ to $[-1,1]$ is an implementation detail for normalised images.

Common mistakes

(a) Dropping the minus sign in $s=-\epsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$: the score points toward the clean signal, opposite to the noise. (b) Assuming equal prediction targets mean equal losses; the weights above differ by schedule. (c) Using the conditional score $\nabla\log q(x_t|x_0)$ and the marginal score $\nabla\log q(x_t)$ interchangeably: the network learns the average of the former given $x_t$, which is the latter (remark filled in, not derived in class).

6. The sampling update

Statement

Drawing $x_{t-1}\sim p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t)=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta,\sigma_t^2I)$ is the same as $x_{t-1}=\mu_\theta(x_t,t)+\sigma_tz$ with $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$; with the noise parameterisation, $x_{t-1}=\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\Big)+\sigma_tz$. Source: Oct 3 lecture ("DDPM Inference and Generation") and Oct 10 tutorial §10. Used later: this loop is the whole generator.

  1. The learned reverse transition is the Gaussian chosen in Section 3: $$p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t)=\mathcal N\big(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\;\sigma_t^2I\big).$$ modelling choice, variance fixed
  2. Reparameterisation lemma. If $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ then $\mu+\sigma z$ is Gaussian with mean $\mu+\sigma\,\mathbb E[z]=\mu$ and covariance $\sigma^2\operatorname{Cov}(z)=\sigma^2I$. So $$x_{t-1}=\mu_\theta(x_t,t)+\sigma_tz,\qquad z\sim\mathcal N(0,I),$$ is an exact sample from $p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t)$. a linear map of a Gaussian is Gaussian
  3. Insert the noise parameterisation of the mean (Section 4, Step 6): $$x_{t-1}=\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\Big)+\sigma_tz.$$ substitute $\mu_\theta$
  4. The variance: the lecture and the tutorial use the precomputed reverse variance $\sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t$ (the posterior variance $\Sigma_q$). A common alternative is $\sigma_t^2=\beta_t$; both are fixed numbers. Section 3, Step 7
  5. At the last step ($t=1$) no extra noise is added ($z=0$), so the output is not re-corrupted; with $\tilde\beta_1=0$ this is also consistent with Section 2. $\tilde\beta_1=0$
  6. Initialisation: $x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, justified because $q(x_T|x_0)\approx\mathcal N(0,I)$ for every $x_0$ (Section 1, Step 9 (corollary), since $\bar\alpha_T\approx0$). the forward process has erased the data
  7. If the network predicts $x_0$ or the score instead, first convert its output to $\mu_\theta$ with Figure 6, then apply Step 2 unchanged. Section 5
▪
Worked numeric check ($t=2$)

With a perfect network $\epsilon_\theta=\epsilon=-0.6587$ the mean is $\mu_\theta=0.8373$ (Section 4). With $\sigma_2^2=\tilde\beta_2=0.0714$, $\sigma_2=0.2673$, and a drawn $z=0.7$: $x_1=0.8373+0.2673\cdot0.7=1.0244$. (With the alternative $\sigma_2^2=\beta_2=0.2$ the noise scale would be $0.4472$ instead.)

Remember for the exam

The network output is not the sample; it parameterises one Gaussian transition from which we still draw. Training samples $x_t$ in one shot, inference walks all $T$ steps. The professor's reason for iterating (rather than mapping noise to data in one step): a single step is the VAE-decoder problem, hard from almost pure noise, while many small denoising corrections are easier. The same loop gives a novel sample because $x_T$ is fresh noise, not a corrupted training image.

Common mistakes

(a) Confusing $z$ (fresh noise for the sampler) with $\epsilon$ (the quantity the network predicts). (b) Adding noise at the last step. (c) Using a different schedule in training and sampling.

7. Classifier guidance: the score decomposition

Statement

$\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t|y)=\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)+\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y|x_t)$; with a guidance scale $w$ and the noise parameterisation, the guided noise is $\hat\epsilon=\epsilon_\theta-w\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(y|x_t,t)$. Source: Oct 3 lecture ("Guided and Conditional Diffusion"): the score sum is stated; the professor skipped its detailed derivation and the conversion to $\epsilon$ is filled in here. Used later: text-to-image and class-conditional generation.

  1. Bayes' rule on the noisy variable: $$p(x_t|y)=\frac{p(y|x_t)\,p(x_t)}{p(y)}.$$ Bayes rule
  2. Take logarithms: $$\log p(x_t|y)=\log p(x_t)+\log p(y|x_t)-\log p(y).$$ $\log$ of a product and a quotient
  3. Differentiate in $x_t$; $\log p(y)$ does not depend on $x_t$ and vanishes: $$\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t|y)=\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t)+\nabla_{x_t}\log p(y|x_t).$$ $p(y)$ is constant in $x_t$
  4. The first term is the diffusion model's score, the second is supplied by a classifier $p_\phi(y|x_t,t)$ trained on noisy images; scaling it by $w$: $$s_w(x_t,y)=s_\theta(x_t,t)+w\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(y|x_t,t).$$ guidance scale $w$ ($w=1$ is exact Bayes)
  5. Convert to noise using $s=-\epsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ (Section 5, Step 4), i.e. $\hat\epsilon=-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;s_w$: $$\hat\epsilon=\epsilon_\theta-w\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(y|x_t,t).$$ step filled in; then use $\hat\epsilon$ in the Section 6 update
▪
Worked numeric check ($t=2$)

Take the score $s=1.2447$ and $\epsilon_\theta=-0.6587$ (Section 5), and a hypothetical classifier gradient $g=0.8$ with $w=2$. Guided score: $1.2447+2\cdot0.8=2.8447$. Guided noise: $-0.6587-2\cdot0.5292\cdot0.8=-1.5054$. Consistency: $-(-1.5054)/0.5292=2.8447$, matching the guided score (verified).

Remember for the exam

This is a one-line Bayes-plus-gradient proof; know the intuition the professor gave: the auxiliary classifier tells the denoiser which direction in image space is more consistent with the description, steering the whole reverse trajectory. Training data for conditioning are image-label or image-caption pairs. No proof in class for latent diffusion: it is the same DDPM (same losses and sampler) run on the latent vectors of a trained autoencoder, then decoded; the gain is compute, not new mathematics.
