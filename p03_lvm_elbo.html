Latent variable models: ELBO and EM

Every derivation from the start of the latent-variable-model block: MLE equals minimising forward KL, why the marginal likelihood is intractable, the ELBO via Jensen, the exact gap to the posterior, the two forms of the ELBO, the E-step and M-step, why EM never decreases the likelihood, and the Gaussian-mixture updates. Each proof has a picture, numbered steps with justifications, and a numerical check.

Notation (professor's route)

Data $x$, latent $z$ (written $Z$ in class), model joint $p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$, model marginal $p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$ (a sum if $z$ is discrete). $q(z\mid x)$ is the arbitrary "variational posterior"; $p_\theta(z\mid x)$ is the true posterior of the model. The ELBO is called $J_\theta(Q)$ in the Sept 05 notes and $\mathcal L(\theta,Q)$ in the Sept 12 notes; here I write $J(\theta,q)$. Steps marked (step filled in — not written out in class) were stated or implied in lecture but their algebra was not shown.

1. MLE is minimising forward KL

Statement

$\arg\min_\theta D_{KL}(P_X\Vert P_\theta)=\arg\max_\theta \mathbb E_{x\sim P_X}[\log P_\theta(x)]$: maximum likelihood is forward-KL minimisation. Source: Sept 05 lecture (also recapped Sept 12). Used for every model that follows: VAEs, diffusion, and LLM training.

  1. Definition of the forward KL (data first, model second): $$D_{KL}(P_X\Vert P_\theta)=\int P_X(x)\,\log\frac{P_X(x)}{P_\theta(x)}\,dx$$definition
  2. Split the log of a quotient: $$D_{KL}(P_X\Vert P_\theta)=\int P_X(x)\log P_X(x)\,dx-\int P_X(x)\log P_\theta(x)\,dx$$$\log\frac ab=\log a-\log b$, linearity of $\int$
  3. The first integral does not contain $\theta$: $$\int P_X(x)\log P_X(x)\,dx=-H(P_X)=\text{const in }\theta$$nature fixes the data entropy
  4. Therefore $$D_{KL}(P_X\Vert P_\theta)=-H(P_X)-\mathbb E_{x\sim P_X}[\log P_\theta(x)]$$steps 2 and 3, rewrite $\int P_X(\cdot)$ as $\mathbb E_{P_X}$
  5. Minimising a function plus a constant is the same as minimising the function, and minimising $-f$ is maximising $f$: $$\theta^*=\arg\min_\theta D_{KL}(P_X\Vert P_\theta)=\arg\max_\theta\ \mathbb E_{x\sim P_X}[\log P_\theta(x)]$$constants do not move the argmin
  6. In practice $P_X$ is only known through samples $x_1,\dots,x_N$, so replace the expectation by the sample mean: $$\mathbb E_{P_X}[\log P_\theta(x)]\approx\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\log P_\theta(x_i)$$law of large numbers; this is the log-likelihood
▪
Worked numeric check

Data $P=(0.7,0.3)$ on two outcomes. Entropy term: $-H(P)=0.7\ln0.7+0.3\ln0.3=-0.611$. Model $(0.5,0.5)$: cross-entropy $=-\ln0.5=0.693$, so $KL=0.693-0.611=0.082$; directly, $0.7\ln\frac{0.7}{0.5}+0.3\ln\frac{0.3}{0.5}=0.2355-0.1532=0.082$. ✓. Model $(0.7,0.3)$: cross-entropy $0.611$, $KL=0$. Model $(0.9,0.1)$: cross-entropy $0.765$, $KL=0.154$. The model with the highest expected log-likelihood is the one with the smallest KL.

Remember for the exam
  • The professor: "maximising likelihood is better understood as minimising the KL divergence"; all LLMs are trained by maximum likelihood, i.e. forward KL $D_{KL}(P_X\Vert P_\theta)$.
  • The dropped term is the (negative) entropy of the data, independent of $\theta$.
  • Asked about reverse KL: the asymmetry is real (it motivated F-divergences earlier), but empirically, with good expressive models, forward KL alone works, and this is what all LLMs use.
Common mistake

Writing the KL the other way round, $D_{KL}(P_\theta\Vert P_X)$: then the expectation is under the model and the result is not the log-likelihood. MLE = data first in the KL.

2. Why the marginal likelihood is intractable

Statement

For a latent variable model, $\log p_\theta(x)=\log\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$ and its gradient cannot be computed directly: the integral has no closed form, and the gradient needs the very posterior $p_\theta(z\mid x)$ that requires $p_\theta(x)$. Source: Sept 05 ("The Core Challenge"), Sept 12 (Jensen section). The gradient identity is step filled in. This is the reason every LVM (VAE, diffusion, BERT) optimises a bound.

  1. The chain rule of probability gives the joint: $$p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$$product rule
  2. The model distribution on data is the marginal: $$p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)\,dz\quad\Big(\text{or }\sum_z\Big)$$definition of an LVM
  3. The objective needs the logarithm of this integral: $$L(\theta)=\log p_\theta(x)=\log\int p_\theta(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)\,dz$$log-likelihood; "log of a sum/integral"
  4. The log cannot be pushed inside the integral: $\log\int f\neq\int\log f$. If the pieces are exponentials, $\sum\log e^{a_k}=\sum a_k$ collapses, but $\log\sum e^{a_k}$ does not. The sum "stays stuck inside the log" (professor's remark), so no closed form and no sample-average form.
  5. Gradient identity (step filled in — not written out in class). Differentiate the log: $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)=\frac{1}{p_\theta(x)}\int\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$$\nabla\log u=\nabla u/u$; swap $\nabla$ and $\int$
  6. Use the log-derivative trick $\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x,z)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x,z)$: $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)=\int\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{p_\theta(x)}\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$$\nabla u=u\nabla\log u$
  7. The weight is the posterior by Bayes' rule: $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)=\mathbb E_{z\sim p_\theta(z\mid x)}\big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x,z)\big]$$$\frac{p(x,z)}{p(x)}=p(z\mid x)$
  8. The expectation is under $p_\theta(z\mid x)=p_\theta(x,z)/p_\theta(x)$, whose denominator is the intractable integral itself. The problem is circular: to learn $\theta$ we need the posterior, to get the posterior we need $p_\theta(x)$. This is why a separate distribution $q(z\mid x)$ is introduced.
  9. Brute-force alternatives fail. A discrete $z\in\{0,1\}^K$ needs $2^K$ terms ($K=30$: about $10^9$). A continuous $z$ with a neural-net decoder gives an integral with no closed form. Monte Carlo over the prior, $\log\frac1S\sum_s p(x\mid z_s)$ with $z_s\sim p(z)$, is a biased estimator of $\log p(x)$ (by Jensen it is a lower bound in expectation) and has huge variance because of Figure 2.1.
▪
Remember for the exam
  • "The integral cannot be solved directly, because we know nothing about $Z$; its distribution is what we are learning jointly."
  • Two jobs at once: learn $\theta$ and (implicitly) the latent space, since the data never shows a $z_i$.
  • Remedy in every LVM: introduce an auxiliary $q(z\mid x)$ and turn "log of an expectation" into "expectation of a log" (next proof).
Common mistake

Saying the difficulty is "not knowing the prior". The prior $p(z)$ and decoder $p(x\mid z)$ are model choices and are known. The difficulty is the integral over $z$ of a nonlinear function, inside a $\log$.

3. The ELBO via Jensen's inequality

Statement

For any $q(z\mid x)$: $\log p_\theta(x)\ \ge\ J(\theta,q)=\mathbb E_{z\sim q(z\mid x)}\big[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\big]$. Source: Sept 05 (full derivation, Steps 1-5); restated Sept 12. Used as the training objective of the VAE, diffusion models and BERT-style models.

Lemma (Jensen for $\log$, step filled in — only the statement was given in class). For a random variable $Y>0$ with mean $m=\mathbb E[Y]$: $\mathbb E[\log Y]\le\log\mathbb E[Y]$.

  1. A concave differentiable function lies below its tangent at $m$; for $\log$ the tangent at $m$ is $\log m+\frac{y-m}{m}$: $$\log y\ \le\ \log m+\frac{y-m}{m}\quad\text{for all }y>0$$concavity of $\log$
  2. Put $y=Y$ and take the expectation of both sides: $$\mathbb E[\log Y]\le\log m+\frac{\mathbb E[Y]-m}{m}$$monotonicity and linearity of $\mathbb E$
  3. Since $\mathbb E[Y]=m$ the last term vanishes: $\mathbb E[\log Y]\le\log\mathbb E[Y]$. Equality needs $\log Y$ to equal its tangent almost surely, which (strict concavity) happens iff $Y=m$ almost surely.
▪

Theorem: ELBO.

  1. Start from the log-evidence: $$L(\theta)=\log p_\theta(x)=\log\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$LVM definition
  2. Multiply and divide by $q(z\mid x)$ inside the integral (assume $q>0$ wherever $p_\theta(x,z)>0$): $$L(\theta)=\log\int q(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\,dz$$multiply and divide by $q$ (importance weighting)
  3. Recognise $\int q(z\mid x)\,g(z)\,dz$ as an expectation under $q$: $$L(\theta)=\log\mathbb E_{z\sim q(z\mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right]$$definition of expectation
  4. Apply Jensen with $Y=\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}$ (concave $\log$, so log of expectation is at least expectation of log): $$\log\mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right]\ \ge\ \mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right]$$Jensen: $\log$ concave
  5. Name the right-hand side the Evidence Lower BOund: $$\log p_\theta(x)\ \ge\ J(\theta,q)=\mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q(z\mid x)\big]$$definition; $\log$ of quotient
  6. Equality condition (step filled in): by the Lemma, equality iff $Y=\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}$ is constant in $z$. That constant must equal $\mathbb E_q[Y]=p_\theta(x)$, so $q(z\mid x)=p_\theta(x,z)/p_\theta(x)=p_\theta(z\mid x)$, the true posterior.
  7. For a dataset, sum over points: $\sum_i\log p_\theta(x_i)\ge\sum_iJ(\theta,q_i)$, each $x_i$ having its own $q_i(z\mid x_i)$.
▪
Worked numeric check: a 2-component mixture, one observation

Prior $p(z{=}1)=0.3,\ p(z{=}2)=0.7$; likelihoods $x\mid z{=}1\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, $x\mid z{=}2\sim\mathcal N(3,1)$; observed $x=2$. Joints: $p(x,1)=0.3\cdot0.05399=0.016197$, $p(x,2)=0.7\cdot0.24197=0.169379$. Evidence: $p(x)=0.185577$, so $\log p(x)=-1.6843$.

Take $q=(0.5,0.5)$. Then $Y_k=p(x,k)/q_k=(0.032395,\,0.338759)$; $\mathbb E_q[Y]=0.185577=p(x)$ ✓, $\log\mathbb E_q[Y]=-1.6843$, while $\mathbb E_q[\log Y]=\tfrac12(-3.4296-1.0828)=-2.2561$. So $J=-2.256\le-1.684$ as Jensen demands. The ELBO is computable with a sum over only two terms of $\log$, no log of a sum.

Remember for the exam
  • Steps in order: multiply and divide by $Q$, recognise an expectation, apply Jensen ($\log$ is concave), name the result the ELBO. Say "evidence" for $\log p_\theta(x)$.
  • Motivation to state: samples can approximate expectations of logs (sums of logs, tractable) but not logs of expectations.
  • The general LVM problem, "the holy grail": $\theta^*,Q^*=\arg\max_{\theta,Q}\mathbb E_{Q}\big[\log\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{Q(z\mid x)}\big]$.
  • VAEs, diffusion models and BERT-family models are all "ELBO optimisers".
Common mistake

Getting the direction wrong: for the concave $\log$, $\log\mathbb E[Y]\ge\mathbb E[\log Y]$. (For convex functions, such as $y^2$, the inequality flips.) Also: the bound holds for every $q$, not just a good one; $q$ is arbitrary but must cover the support of $p_\theta(x,z)$.

4. The exact gap: log-evidence = ELBO + KL to the posterior

Statement

$\log p_\theta(x)=J(\theta,q)+D_{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\Vert\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$. Hence $J\le\log p_\theta(x)$, with equality iff $q(z\mid x)=p_\theta(z\mid x)$. Source: the bound is Sept 05/12; the exact identity and the "equality iff posterior" statement are step filled in (implicit in the lecture's "the ELBO is tight when the approximation is good" and the E-step). Used in the VAE, in EM, and in variational inference generally.

  1. Bayes' rule for the model's own joint: $$p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(z\mid x)\,p_\theta(x)$$definition of conditional probability
  2. Insert into the ELBO: $$J(\theta,q)=\mathbb E_{q}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(z\mid x)\,p_\theta(x)}{q(z\mid x)}\Big]$$step 1 substituted
  3. Split the log of a product: $$J(\theta,q)=\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x)\big]+\mathbb E_{q}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(z\mid x)}{q(z\mid x)}\Big]$$$\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$, linearity of $\mathbb E$
  4. The first term does not depend on $z$, and $q$ integrates to 1: $$\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x)\big]=\log p_\theta(x)$$expectation of a constant
  5. The second term is minus a KL: $$\mathbb E_{q}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(z\mid x)}{q(z\mid x)}\Big]=-D_{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\Vert p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$definition of KL (flip the quotient, flip the sign)
  6. Combine and rearrange: $$\log p_\theta(x)=J(\theta,q)+D_{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\Vert p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)$$steps 3-5
  7. KL is non-negative (Gibbs' inequality). Proof by the Jensen Lemma of Proof 3: $$-D_{KL}(q\Vert p)=\mathbb E_q\Big[\log\frac pq\Big]\le\log\mathbb E_q\Big[\frac pq\Big]=\log\int p(z\mid x)\,dz=\log1=0$$Jensen; $p/q$ integrates against $q$ to $\int p=1$
  8. Equality in step 7 holds iff $p/q$ is constant, and the constant must be 1: so $D_{KL}=0\iff q=p_\theta(z\mid x)$.
  9. Conclude: $J(\theta,q)=\log p_\theta(x)-D_{KL}(\dots)\le\log p_\theta(x)$, with equality iff $q(z\mid x)=p_\theta(z\mid x)$. This also reproves the ELBO bound without a separate Jensen step on the joint.
▪

Remember for the exam
  • The ELBO is "tight" exactly when $Q$ equals the true latent posterior $P_\theta(Z\mid X)$; the lost amount is a KL.
  • Do not confuse the two distributions: $Q(Z\mid X)$ is the learned approximation; $P_\theta(Z\mid X)$ is the model's own (intractable) posterior. The professor: they are different objects.
  • Maximising the ELBO over $q$ is the same as minimising $KL(q\Vert p_\theta(z\mid x))$ because $\log p_\theta(x)$ does not depend on $q$.
Common mistake

Writing the gap as $D_{KL}(p_\theta(z\mid x)\Vert q)$. The correct direction is $q$ first (the expectation is under $q$, since we integrate against $q$ in the ELBO).

5. Two equivalent forms of the ELBO

Statement

$J(\theta,q)=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-D_{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\Vert p_\theta(z)\big)$ (reconstruction minus KL to the prior) and $J(\theta,q)=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x,z)]+H(q)$ (expected complete-data log-likelihood plus entropy). Source: Sept 12 lecture (the first form); the entropy form is step filled in and is the one used by the M-step. The first form is the VAE objective.

Form A: reconstruction minus KL.

  1. Start from the ELBO: $$J(\theta,q)=\mathbb E_{q}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\Big]$$definition (Proof 3)
  2. Factor the joint as likelihood times prior: $$p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p_\theta(z)$$chain rule
  3. Split the log of the product: $$J=\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big]+\mathbb E_{q}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(z)}{q(z\mid x)}\Big]$$$\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$; linearity of $\mathbb E$
  4. The second term is minus the KL of $q$ from the prior: $$J=\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big]-D_{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\Vert p_\theta(z)\big)$$definition of KL
▪

Form B: complete-data term plus entropy.

  1. Split the log of the quotient: $$J=\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q(z\mid x)\big]$$$\log\frac ab=\log a-\log b$
  2. Separate the two expectations: $$J=\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z)\big]-\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log q(z\mid x)\big]$$linearity of $\mathbb E$
  3. The second piece is the entropy of $q$: $$J=\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z)\big]+H(q),\qquad H(q)=-\mathbb E_q[\log q]$$definition of entropy; note $H(q)$ has no $\theta$
▪
Worked numeric check (mixture, $x=2$)

$q=(0.5,0.5)$: reconstruction $\mathbb E_q[\log p(x\mid z)]=0.5(\ln0.05399+\ln0.24197)=0.5(-2.9189-1.4189)=-2.1689$; $KL(q\Vert\text{prior}(0.3,0.7))=0.5\ln\frac{0.5}{0.3}+0.5\ln\frac{0.5}{0.7}=0.2554-0.1682=0.0872$. Form A: $-2.1689-0.0872=-2.2561$ ✓. Form B: $\mathbb E_q[\log p(x,z)]=0.5(-4.1228-1.7753)=-2.9491$, $H(q)=\ln2=0.6931$, sum $-2.2560$ ✓. At the posterior: reconstruction $-1.5499$, $KL$ to prior $0.1344$, ELBO $-1.6843$ ✓.

Remember for the exam
  • Reconstruction term "encourages the latent to explain the observed data"; KL term "keeps the variational posterior close to the prior over latents". This is the same pattern as the VAE loss.
  • A third form is Proof 4: $J=\log p_\theta(x)-D_{KL}(q\Vert p_\theta(z\mid x))$. Pick the form that makes the quantity you need explicit.
Common mistake

Using $\log p(x,z)$ (instead of $\log p(x\mid z)$) in the "reconstruction" term, or forgetting that the KL is to the prior $p_\theta(z)$, not to the posterior; the KL-to-posterior belongs to the gap formula of Proof 4.

6. Maximising the ELBO: E-step and M-step

Statement

(E) For fixed $\theta_t$, $J(\theta_t,q)$ is maximised over $q$ by $q^{t+1}(z\mid x)=p_{\theta_t}(z\mid x)$, with maximum value $\log p_{\theta_t}(x)$. (M) For fixed $q$, $\arg\max_\theta J(\theta,q)=\arg\max_\theta\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x,z)]$. Source: Sept 12 ("Optimization Strategy" and "The EM Algorithm", which names the steps but does not write out the maximisers; both derivations here are step filled in.)

E-step.

  1. Fix $\theta=\theta_t$. From Proof 4: $$J(\theta_t,q)=\log p_{\theta_t}(x)-D_{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\Vert p_{\theta_t}(z\mid x)\big)$$gap identity
  2. $\log p_{\theta_t}(x)$ is a constant with respect to $q$. Hence $$\arg\max_qJ(\theta_t,q)=\arg\min_q D_{KL}\big(q\Vert p_{\theta_t}(z\mid x)\big)$$constant drops out; maximising $-f$ is minimising $f$
  3. $KL\ge0$ with equality iff the arguments agree, so the unique minimiser is $$q^{t+1}(z\mid x)=p_{\theta_t}(z\mid x)=\frac{p_{\theta_t}(x,z)}{p_{\theta_t}(x)}$$Gibbs inequality, Proof 4 step 8
  4. The value attained is $J(\theta_t,q^{t+1})=\log p_{\theta_t}(x)$: the bound is tight at the current $\theta$.

M-step.

  1. Fix $q=q^{t+1}$ and use form B of Proof 5: $$J(\theta,q^{t+1})=\mathbb E_{q^{t+1}}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z)\big]+H(q^{t+1})$$entropy form
  2. $H(q^{t+1})$ does not depend on $\theta$, so $$\theta_{t+1}=\arg\max_\theta\ \underbrace{\mathbb E_{z\sim p_{\theta_t}(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x,z)\big]}_{\mathcal Q(\theta\mid\theta_t)}$$constant drops out of the argmax
  3. For a dataset: $\mathcal Q(\theta\mid\theta_t)=\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbb E_{z\sim p_{\theta_t}(z\mid x_i)}[\log p_\theta(x_i,z)]$. This is the expected complete-data log-likelihood: the latent variable is "filled in" by its posterior, then we do ordinary maximum likelihood on the completed data, which is usually easy because $\log p_\theta(x,z)$ has no log-of-sum.
▪
Worked numeric check

E-step on the single-point mixture ($x=2$): moving from $q=(0.5,0.5)$ to the posterior $(0.0873,0.9127)$ raises the ELBO from $-2.2561$ to $-1.6843=\log p(x)$ (the gap $0.5718$ is removed). M-step: see Proof 9, where the three-point dataset has $J(\theta_0,q)=-5.818$ before and $J(\theta_1,q)=-4.074$ after the M-step with $q$ held fixed.

Remember for the exam
  • Strategy: alternate. Fix $\theta$, optimise $Q$ (this is the Expectation step); fix $Q$, optimise $\theta$ (this is the Maximisation step). Repeat until convergence.
  • Optimal $Q$ at fixed $\theta$ is the exact posterior $P_\theta(Z\mid X)$. For GMM it is available in closed form; for a VAE it is not, so $Q$ is approximated by an encoder network.
Common mistake

The M-step maximises over $\theta$ only; the expectation is under the old posterior $p_{\theta_t}(z\mid x)$, which is held fixed even though $\theta$ changes inside $\log p_\theta(x,z)$.

7. EM never decreases the likelihood

Statement

The EM iterates satisfy $\log p_{\theta_{t+1}}(x)\ge\log p_{\theta_t}(x)$. Source: Sept 12 — the professor said "the proof of convergence is standard" without writing it; the whole argument is step filled in. Justifies using EM, and its VAE-era relatives, as a safe optimiser.

  1. E-step makes the bound tight at $\theta_t$ (Proof 6): $$J(\theta_t,q^{t+1})=\log p_{\theta_t}(x)$$$q^{t+1}=p_{\theta_t}(z\mid x)$ gives zero KL
  2. M-step maximises over $\theta$ with $q^{t+1}$ fixed, so it does at least as well as staying put: $$J(\theta_{t+1},q^{t+1})\ \ge\ J(\theta_t,q^{t+1})$$$\theta_{t+1}$ is an argmax (any $\theta$, in particular $\theta_t$, is no better)
  3. The ELBO is a lower bound for every $\theta$ and every $q$: $$\log p_{\theta_{t+1}}(x)\ \ge\ J(\theta_{t+1},q^{t+1})$$Proof 3 / Proof 4 with $\theta=\theta_{t+1}$
  4. Chain the three: $$\log p_{\theta_{t+1}}(x)\ \ge\ J(\theta_{t+1},q^{t+1})\ \ge\ J(\theta_t,q^{t+1})=\log p_{\theta_t}(x)$$steps 3, 2, 1
  5. Exact bookkeeping of the gain: $\log p_{\theta_{t+1}}(x)-\log p_{\theta_t}(x)=\big[J(\theta_{t+1},q^{t+1})-J(\theta_t,q^{t+1})\big]+D_{KL}\big(q^{t+1}\Vert p_{\theta_{t+1}}(z\mid x)\big)$, both brackets non-negative. Proof 4 at $\theta_{t+1}$, plus step 1
  6. Consequence: if the log-likelihood is bounded above, the non-decreasing sequence $\log p_{\theta_t}(x)$ converges. Fixed points are stationary points of $L$: the gap $L(\theta)-J(\theta,q^{t+1})=KL(q^{t+1}\Vert p_\theta(z\mid x))\ge0$ is zero at $\theta_t$, so it has zero gradient there and $\nabla_\theta L(\theta_t)=\nabla_\theta J(\theta_t,q^{t+1})$ (the bound is tangent to the curve in Figure 7.1). Convergence to a local, not necessarily global, optimum.
  7. For a dataset, sum steps 1-4 over $i$ (each $q_i$ is the posterior of $x_i$); monotonicity holds for the total log-likelihood.
▪
Worked numeric check (3-point GMM iteration of Proof 9: $x=\{0,1,5\}$)

Start $\theta_0$: $\alpha=(0.5,0.5)$, $\mu=(0,4)$, $\sigma^2=(1,1)$. E-step: $J(\theta_0,q^1)=-5.8178=\log p_{\theta_0}(X)$ (tight ✓). After the M-step with $q^1$ fixed: $J(\theta_1,q^1)=-4.0741$. True log-likelihood at $\theta_1$: $-3.6630$. Chain: $-5.818\le-4.074\le-3.663$ ✓. The extra $0.411$ above the bound equals $KL(q^1\Vert p_{\theta_1}(z\mid x))$ summed over the three points.

Remember for the exam
  • The 3-inequality sandwich: tight at old $\theta$, M-step improves the bound, bound is below the true log-likelihood at new $\theta$.
  • EM guarantees monotone increase and convergence to a local optimum/stationary point; not global optimality.
Common mistake

Claiming the M-step maximises the log-likelihood itself. It maximises the bound built at $\theta_t$; only the E-step makes the bound touch the likelihood. Also: the M-step only needs to increase $J$ for monotonicity (Generalised EM).

8. Gaussian mixture: the E-step (responsibilities)

Statement

For a GMM, $P(z{=}j\mid x_i)=\gamma_{ij}=\dfrac{\alpha_j\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}{\sum_{l=1}^M\alpha_l\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_l,\Sigma_l)}$, and this is the exact optimal $Q$. Source: Sept 12 (GMM setup: discrete latent, mixing weights, Gaussian components); the posterior formula is step filled in. Used in clustering and as the bridge to VAEs.

  1. The GMM as a latent variable model: $z\in\{1,\dots,M\}$ with $$P(z{=}j)=\alpha_j,\ \ \alpha_j\ge0,\ \ \sum_{j=1}^M\alpha_j=1,\qquad p(x\mid z{=}j)=\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$$model definition (Sept 12)
  2. Joint by the chain rule: $$p_\theta(x,z{=}j)=\alpha_j\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$$$p(z)\,p(x\mid z)$
  3. Marginal (the integral is a finite sum, so here the LVM is tractable): $$p_\theta(x)=\sum_{l=1}^M\alpha_l\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_l,\Sigma_l)$$sum rule over the discrete $z$
  4. The E-step optimum is the posterior (Proof 6); by Bayes' rule $$\gamma_{ij}\doteq q(z{=}j\mid x_i)=p_\theta(z{=}j\mid x_i)=\frac{p_\theta(x_i,z{=}j)}{p_\theta(x_i)}=\frac{\alpha_j\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}{\sum_{l}\alpha_l\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_l,\Sigma_l)}$$Bayes' rule; steps 2 and 3
  5. The $\gamma_{ij}$ are non-negative and sum to one over components for each point: $$\sum_{j=1}^M\gamma_{ij}=1$$normalisation by the denominator
  6. With the $D$-dimensional Gaussian $$\mathcal N(x\mid\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\Big)$$definition; keep $|\Sigma_j|$ in the ratio, it differs per component
▪

Remember for the exam
  • Responsibility = posterior probability that component $j$ generated $x_i$; a soft cluster assignment. In GMM the E-step is exact because $z$ is discrete and the sum is finite.
  • The GMM is "one of the early and classical latent variable models"; the latent variable picks the Gaussian that generated the point, $\alpha_j$ are the cluster priors.
  • Learned: $\{\alpha_j,\mu_j,\Sigma_j\}_{j=1}^M$.
Common mistake

Forgetting the weight $\alpha_j$ (using only the likelihood $\mathcal N$): a component with a high density but tiny prior would be over-credited. Normalise over $j$ (components), not over $i$ (data points).

9. Gaussian mixture: the M-step (means, covariances, weights)

Statement

With $N_j=\sum_i\gamma_{ij}$: $\ \mu_j=\frac1{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}x_i,\ \ \Sigma_j=\frac1{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}(x_i-\mu_j)(x_i-\mu_j)^\top,\ \ \alpha_j=\frac{N_j}{N}$. Source: Sept 12 only sets up the parameters $\{\alpha,\mu,\Sigma\}$; the maximisation is step filled in. This is the classical result the professor pointed to (NPTEL classical ML) and the template for every M-step.

  1. For the dataset $x_1,\dots,x_N$, with $q_i(z{=}j)=\gamma_{ij}$ from the E-step, the ELBO is $$J=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^M\gamma_{ij}\,\log\frac{\alpha_j\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}{\gamma_{ij}}$$ELBO per point, $z$ discrete so $\mathbb E_q$ is a finite sum
  2. Only the part containing $\theta$ matters (the $-\gamma\log\gamma$ part is the entropy $H(q)$, independent of $\theta$): $$\mathcal Q(\theta)=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\gamma_{ij}\Big[\log\alpha_j+\log\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)\Big]$$M-step objective, Proof 6
  3. Expand the Gaussian log-density: $$\log\mathcal N(x\mid\mu,\Sigma)=-\tfrac D2\log2\pi-\tfrac12\log|\Sigma|-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)$$take $\log$ of the density
  4. Means. Differentiate $\mathcal Q$ in $\mu_j$ (only component $j$'s terms depend on it): $$\frac{\partial\mathcal Q}{\partial\mu_j}=\sum_i\gamma_{ij}\,\Sigma_j^{-1}(x_i-\mu_j)=0$$$\nabla_\mu\big[-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\big]=\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)$
  5. Multiply by $\Sigma_j$ and solve: $$\mu_j=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ij}\,x_i}{\sum_i\gamma_{ij}}=\frac1{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}\,x_i,\qquad N_j\doteq\sum_i\gamma_{ij}$$$\Sigma_j$ invertible; $\mathcal Q$ is concave in $\mu_j$, so this stationary point is the maximum
  6. Covariances. Write $\Lambda_j=\Sigma_j^{-1}$ so $\log|\Sigma_j|=-\log|\Lambda_j|$. The part of $\mathcal Q$ that depends on $\Lambda_j$ is $$\sum_i\gamma_{ij}\Big[\tfrac12\log|\Lambda_j|-\tfrac12(x_i-\mu_j)^\top\Lambda_j(x_i-\mu_j)\Big]$$step 3 with $\Sigma^{-1}=\Lambda$
  7. Differentiate in $\Lambda_j$ using $\frac{\partial\log|\Lambda|}{\partial\Lambda}=\Lambda^{-1}=\Sigma$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda}\,a^\top\Lambda a=aa^\top$: $$\tfrac12N_j\,\Sigma_j-\tfrac12\sum_i\gamma_{ij}(x_i-\mu_j)(x_i-\mu_j)^\top=0$$matrix calculus (symmetric $\Lambda$)
  8. Solve for $\Sigma_j$ (with the new $\mu_j$ of step 5): $$\Sigma_j=\frac1{N_j}\sum_i\gamma_{ij}\,(x_i-\mu_j)(x_i-\mu_j)^\top$$divide by $N_j/2$
  9. Weights. The $\alpha$-part is $\sum_jN_j\log\alpha_j$ subject to $\sum_j\alpha_j=1$. Lagrangian: $$\mathcal F=\sum_jN_j\log\alpha_j+\lambda\Big(1-\sum_j\alpha_j\Big)$$constrained optimisation, $\alpha$ is a probability vector
  10. Set $\partial\mathcal F/\partial\alpha_j=N_j/\alpha_j-\lambda=0$, so $\alpha_j=N_j/\lambda$.
  11. Impose the constraint: $\sum_j\alpha_j=\frac1\lambda\sum_jN_j=1$. And $\sum_jN_j=\sum_i\sum_j\gamma_{ij}=\sum_i1=N$, so $\lambda=N$: $$\alpha_j=\frac{N_j}{N}$$$\gamma_{i\cdot}$ sums to 1 (Proof 8 step 5); $\log$ is concave so this is the maximum
▪
Worked numeric check: full iteration (data $x=\{0,1,5\}$, $\theta_0$: $\alpha=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $\mu=(0,4)$, $\sigma^2=(1,1)$)

Responsibilities from the table of Proof 8. Effective counts: $N_1=0.99966+0.98201+0.000006=1.98168$, $N_2=0.00034+0.01799+0.999994=1.01832$ (sum $=3=N$ ✓).

Means: $\mu_1=\frac{0.98201\cdot1+0.000006\cdot5}{1.98168}=0.4956$; $\mu_2=\frac{0.01799\cdot1+0.999994\cdot5}{1.01832}=4.9277$.

Variances (1-D): $\sigma_1^2=\frac{0.99966(0.4956)^2+0.98201(0.5044)^2+0.000006(4.50)^2}{1.98168}=0.2500$; $\sigma_2^2=\frac{0.00034(4.93)^2+0.01799(3.93)^2+0.999994(0.0723)^2}{1.01832}=0.2856$.

Weights: $\alpha=(1.98168/3,\ 1.01832/3)=(0.6606,\ 0.3394)$.

Log-likelihood: before $=-5.8178$, after $=-3.6630$: it increased, as Proof 7 guarantees. The learned model now describes the data as a tight cluster near $0.5$ (two points, weight $\approx2/3$) and one near $4.9$ (one point, weight $\approx1/3$).

Remember for the exam
  • GMM M-step = weighted maximum-likelihood for a Gaussian: weights are the responsibilities. $N_j$ is the "effective number of points" in cluster $j$.
  • Alternate: E-step computes $\gamma_{ij}$ (posterior), M-step re-estimates $\alpha_j,\mu_j,\Sigma_j$; repeat to convergence. Hard assignments ($\gamma\in\{0,1\}$) would give K-means.
  • GMM learns $\alpha,\mu,\Sigma$ exactly because the posterior is tractable; its limitation (many parameters, rigid cluster shapes, fixed discrete latent) motivates VAEs with continuous $z$ and an approximate $Q$.
Common mistake

Using the old means inside the covariance update; the update uses the freshly computed $\mu_j$. Also: EM for GMM can collapse a component onto one point ($\sigma^2\to0$, likelihood $\to\infty$) and finds only a local optimum, so initialisation matters.
