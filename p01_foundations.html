Foundations: change of variables, sampling, MLE = KL

The mathematics that every later model rests on: why pushing a simple random variable through a function gives a sampler, how the density changes under that function (1-D and multi-D Jacobian), the information-theory identities behind KL divergence, why $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\ge 0$ and every f-divergence is non-negative, why sample averages can replace expectations, and why maximum likelihood is the same thing as minimising a KL divergence. Sources: classes of 15 Aug, 22 Aug, 29 Aug and 5 Sep.

1. The sampling principle: push a simple distribution through a function

Statement (class of 22 Aug)

If $Z$ is a random variable with a known, easy-to-sample density $p_Z$ and $G$ is a deterministic function, then $X=G(Z)$ is again a random variable whose law depends only on $p_Z$ and $G$; drawing $z\sim p_Z$ and returning $x=G(z)$ produces samples of $X$. Flipping the question (fix the target $p_X$, tune $G_\theta$) is the engine of GANs, VAE decoders, flows and diffusion samplers.

  1. $X=G(Z)$ with $Z:\Omega\to\mathbb R^K$ and $G:\mathbb R^K\to\mathbb R^D$ deterministic, so $X=G\circ Z:\Omega\to\mathbb R^D$ is itself a function on the sample space. composition of functions = a random variable
  2. For any event $A\subseteq\mathbb R^D$: $\;P(X\in A)=P\big(\{\omega: G(Z(\omega))\in A\}\big)$. definition of $X$
  3. $\;=P\big(Z\in G^{-1}(A)\big)$, where $G^{-1}(A)=\{z: G(z)\in A\}$ is the pre-image of $A$ (no inverse function needed). pre-image
  4. $$P(X\in A)=\int_{G^{-1}(A)}p_Z(z)\,dz$$ definition of the density of $Z$
  5. The right-hand side contains only $p_Z$ and $G$, so these two objects fix the law of $X$ completely (answer to the professor's question "what does $p_X$ depend on?"). step 4
  6. Sampling: if $z_1,\dots,z_N\sim p_Z$ are i.i.d. and $x_i=G(z_i)$, then each $x_i$ has law $P_X$ by steps 2–4, and the $x_i$ are independent because they are the same function of independent inputs. functions of independent variables are independent
  7. Flip the question: $p_X$ is a fixed unknown target and we can sample $Z$; the only free object is $G$. Parametrise $G=G_\theta$ (a neural network) so each $\theta$ gives a different $p_\theta$, and solve $$\theta^*=\arg\min_\theta D\big(p_X\,\|\,p_\theta\big).$$ "$G$ is the only lever"
▪
Worked check 1: a line

Let $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and $G(z)=mz+c$ with $m=2$, $c=1$. A draw $z=0.5$ gives $x=2(0.5)+1=2$. Over many draws $x$ has mean $c=1$ and variance $m^2=4$, i.e. $X\sim\mathcal N(1,4)$ (derived rigorously in section 2). Different $(m,c)$ give different distributions: the slope and intercept are the "parameters $\theta$".

Worked check 2: any 1-D target from a uniform seed (not done in class, standard)

Let $U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and let $F$ be a strictly increasing CDF. Take $G=F^{-1}$. Then $P(G(U)\le x)=P(U\le F(x))=F(x)$, because $P(U\le a)=a$ on $[0,1]$. So $G(U)$ has CDF $F$. For $F(x)=1-e^{-x}$ (exponential): $G(u)=-\ln(1-u)$ and $u=0.5$ gives $x=0.693$, the median. This is the 1-D case of "there is a function that turns the seed into the target"; deep generative models learn this function in high dimensions where $F^{-1}$ is unavailable.

Remember for the exam
  • A deterministic function of a random variable is a random variable; its law depends only on $p_Z$ and $G$.
  • You cannot create randomness from nothing: the seed $Z$ is the source of randomness (Gaussian, because it has infinite support); $G$ converts one random variable into another.
  • Training = estimating the distribution (once); inference = sampling by $z\mapsto G_{\theta^*}(z)$ (repeatedly). The density $p_\theta$ is only implicit: we get samples, not a formula.
Common mistake

Thinking $G_\theta(Z)$ has the same distribution as $Z$. Passing a random variable through a function changes its distribution; that is exactly the lever being exploited. Also: $p_\theta$ is not known in closed form, so $D(p_X\|p_\theta)$ cannot be evaluated pointwise; this is the central difficulty of the course.

2. Change of variables in one dimension

Statement (class of 22 Aug names the problem; formula filled in)

If $X=G(Z)$ with $G$ strictly monotone and differentiable, then $p_X(x)=p_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big)\,\big|\tfrac{d}{dx}G^{-1}(x)\big|$. In class the professor only stated that $p_X$ depends on $p_Z$ and $G$ and that the exact relation (Jacobian) returns in flow-based models; the derivation below is filled in — not written out in class. Used in: normalising flows, and in every "reparameterisation" argument.

  1. $F_X(x)=P(X\le x)=P\big(G(Z)\le x\big)$. definition of CDF
  2. Case $G$ increasing: $G(Z)\le x\iff Z\le G^{-1}(x)$, hence $$F_X(x)=F_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big).$$ monotone map keeps the order
  3. Differentiate in $x$: $$p_X(x)=\frac{d}{dx}F_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big)=p_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big)\,\frac{dG^{-1}(x)}{dx}.$$ chain rule, $F_Z'=p_Z$
  4. Case $G$ decreasing: $G(Z)\le x\iff Z\ge G^{-1}(x)$, so $F_X(x)=1-F_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big)$ and $$p_X(x)=-\,p_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big)\,\frac{dG^{-1}(x)}{dx}.$$ order is reversed
  5. For decreasing $G$ the derivative $\frac{dG^{-1}}{dx}$ is negative, so the minus sign makes the density positive. Both cases are the single formula $$p_X(x)=p_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big)\,\Big|\frac{dG^{-1}(x)}{dx}\Big|.$$ absolute value absorbs the sign
  6. With $z=G^{-1}(x)$ and $\frac{dG^{-1}}{dx}=\frac{1}{G'(z)}$: $$p_X(x)=\frac{p_Z(z)}{|G'(z)|}.$$ inverse function theorem
▪
$$p_X(x)\,|dx|=p_Z(z)\,|dz|\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad p_X(x)=p_Z(z)\Big|\frac{dz}{dx}\Big|$$
Probability mass is conserved(mnemonic for the formula above)
Worked numeric check: $x=2z+1$, $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$

$G^{-1}(x)=(x-1)/2$ and $\big|\frac{dG^{-1}}{dx}\big|=\tfrac12$, so $$p_X(x)=\tfrac12\,p_Z\!\Big(\tfrac{x-1}{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x-1)^2/8},$$ which is the $\mathcal N(1,4)$ density. At $x=3$: $z=1$, $p_Z(1)=0.2420$, so $p_X(3)=0.1210$. Directly from $\mathcal N(1,4)$: $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-4/8}=0.1995\times0.6065=0.1210$. ✓

Non-linear example: $x=e^{z}$, so $z=\ln x$ and $|dz/dx|=1/x$. Then $p_X(x)=p_Z(\ln x)/x$ (log-normal). At $x=e$: $p_Z(1)/e=0.2420/2.718=0.0890$. Check that it integrates to 1: substituting back $z=\ln x$ gives $\int p_Z(z)dz=1$.

Remember for the exam
  • $p_X$ depends on two things: the density of $Z$ and the function $G$.
  • Formula = $p_Z$ evaluated at the pre-image, times the absolute derivative of the inverse (equivalently divided by $|G'|$).
  • Derive via the CDF: $F_X(x)=F_Z(G^{-1}(x))$ then differentiate.
Common mistake

Writing $p_X(x)=p_Z(G^{-1}(x))$ with no stretch factor. A density is not a function that is simply re-labelled; it is probability per unit volume, so it must be rescaled. Also forgetting the absolute value for decreasing $G$, and using $G'$ where $(G^{-1})'$ is needed (they are reciprocals).

3. Change of variables in many dimensions (Jacobian)

Statement (mentioned in class of 22 Aug; derivation filled in)

If $G:\mathbb R^D\to\mathbb R^D$ is invertible and differentiable, $X=G(Z)$ and $z=G^{-1}(x)$, then $p_X(x)=p_Z(z)\,/\,|\det J_G(z)|$. The professor said the exact vector relation "involves the Jacobian of $G$ (seen in flow-based models)"; we derive it here — step filled in, not written out in class. Used in: normalising flows, where $G$ is built so that $\det J_G$ is cheap.

  1. Define the Jacobian matrix $[J_G(z)]_{ij}=\dfrac{\partial G_i}{\partial z_j}(z)$; it is the best linear approximation of $G$ near $z$. definition
  2. For any region $A$: $\;P(X\in A)=\displaystyle\int_A p_X(x)\,dx$. definition of density
  3. Also $P(X\in A)=P\big(Z\in G^{-1}(A)\big)=\displaystyle\int_{G^{-1}(A)}p_Z(z)\,dz$. section 1, step 4
  4. Substitute $z=G^{-1}(x)$ in this integral; the volume element transforms as $dz=\big|\det J_{G^{-1}}(x)\big|\,dx$: $$P(X\in A)=\int_A p_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big)\,\big|\det J_{G^{-1}}(x)\big|\,dx.$$ multivariable change of variables for integrals
  5. Steps 2 and 4 give two integrals over $A$ equal for every region $A$; two integrands with equal integrals on every region agree (almost everywhere): $$p_X(x)=p_Z\big(G^{-1}(x)\big)\,\big|\det J_{G^{-1}}(x)\big|.$$ arbitrary $A$ shrinks to a point
  6. Since $G\circ G^{-1}=\mathrm{id}$, the chain rule gives $J_{G^{-1}}(x)=\big[J_G(z)\big]^{-1}$, and $\det(M^{-1})=1/\det M$: $$p_X(x)=\frac{p_Z(z)}{|\det J_G(z)|},\qquad z=G^{-1}(x).$$ inverse function theorem, det of inverse
  7. Take logs (this is the form used in flows): $$\log p_X(x)=\log p_Z(z)-\log\big|\det J_G(z)\big|.$$ $\log$ of a quotient
▪
Worked numeric check: linear map in 2-D

Let $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_2)$ and $x=Az+b$, with $A$ having rows $(2,1)$ and $(0,3)$. Then $\det A=2\cdot3-1\cdot0=6$, and $J_G=A$ everywhere. At $x=b$ we have $z=0$, so $$p_X(b)=\frac{p_Z(0)}{6}=\frac{1/(2\pi)}{6}=\frac{1}{12\pi}=0.0265.$$ Cross-check: $X\sim\mathcal N(b,AA^{\top})$ with $AA^{\top}$ having rows $(5,3)$ and $(3,9)$, whose determinant is $45-9=36$. The Gaussian density at its mean is $\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{36}}=\dfrac{1}{12\pi}$. ✓

Sanity check in 1-D: $\det$ reduces to the scalar slope, and the formula becomes section 2 ($x=2z+1$: divide by $|2|$).

Remember for the exam
  • $p_X$ is the $p_Z$ value at the pre-image divided by $|\det J_G|$ (or multiplied by $|\det J_{G^{-1}}|$). The Jacobian is the local volume-stretch factor.
  • This needs $G$ invertible with $K=D$ (same dimension); that is the price flow-based models pay for an exact density.
  • Flow models keep $\det J_G$ tractable; GANs/VAEs do not, which is why they are "implicit" or need approximations.
Common mistake

Using $|\det J_G|$ when you hold $x$ and want $p_X(x)$ via the inverse map: the factor is $|\det J_{G^{-1}}(x)|$, i.e. $1/|\det J_G(z)|$. Check the direction with the picture: stretching ($|\det|>1$) must lower the density.

4. Surprisal, entropy, cross-entropy and KL = H(P,Q) − H(P)

Statement (class of 22 Aug, homework 2)

(a) The information $I(A)=-\log P(A)$ is additive for independent events. (b) With $H(P)=-\sum p_i\log p_i$ and $H(P,Q)=-\sum p_i\log q_i$ we have $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\sum_i p_i\log\frac{p_i}{q_i}=H(P,Q)-H(P)$: the KL divergence is the extra code length paid for using $Q$ when the truth is $P$. Used in: every KL-based model (VAEs, autoregressive models, LLM cross-entropy training).

  1. Define surprisal $I(A)=-\log P(A)=\log\frac{1}{P(A)}$; since $P(A)\in(0,1]$ it is $\ge0$, small for likely events and large for rare ones. definition
  2. Let $A_1,A_2$ be independent: $P(A_1\cap A_2)=P(A_1)P(A_2)$. independence
  3. $$I(A_1\cap A_2)=-\log\big(P(A_1)P(A_2)\big)=-\log P(A_1)-\log P(A_2)=I(A_1)+I(A_2).$$ $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$ This is why a log is used, and the minus sign makes it non-negative.
  4. Entropy = expected surprisal: $$H(P)=\sum_i p_i\log\frac{1}{p_i}=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i.$$ definition of expectation
  5. Cross-entropy = surprisal under $Q$, averaged with the probabilities of $P$: $$H(P,Q)=-\sum_i p_i\log q_i.$$ definition
  6. $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\sum_i p_i\log\frac{p_i}{q_i}=\sum_i p_i\big(\log p_i-\log q_i\big).$$ $\log$ of a quotient
  7. Split the sum: $$=\sum_i p_i\log p_i-\sum_i p_i\log q_i.$$ linearity of the sum
  8. Recognise the two terms: $$=-H(P)+H(P,Q)=H(P,Q)-H(P).\qquad\text{Hence }H(P,Q)\ge H(P)\iff D_{\mathrm{KL}}\ge0.$$ definitions in steps 4–5
▪
Worked numeric check (bits, $\log_2$)

Additivity: $P(A_1)=\tfrac12$, $P(A_2)=\tfrac18$ independent. $I=1+3=4$ bits, and $-\log_2(\tfrac1{16})=4$. ✓

$P=(0.5,0.5)$, $Q=(0.9,0.1)$:

  • $H(P)=-2(0.5)\log_2 0.5=1$ bit.
  • $H(P,Q)=-0.5\log_2 0.9-0.5\log_2 0.1=0.5(0.152)+0.5(3.322)=1.737$ bits.
  • $H(P,Q)-H(P)=0.737$ bits.
  • Directly: $0.5\log_2\frac{0.5}{0.9}+0.5\log_2\frac{0.5}{0.1}=0.5(-0.848)+0.5(2.322)=0.737$. ✓
Remember for the exam
  • Rare events carry more information; additivity for independent events forces the $\log$.
  • Cross-entropy $\ge$ entropy; KL = cross-entropy minus entropy = extra information in $Q$ not in $P$.
  • KL is non-negative, zero iff $P=Q$, not symmetric, no triangle inequality: a divergence, not a distance (sections 5 and 7).
Common mistake

Taking the expectation under the wrong distribution: both the cross-entropy and the KL average with the probabilities of the first argument $P$ (the data), while the log involves $Q$.

5. KL divergence is non-negative (and zero only if P = Q)

Statement (class of 22 Aug, homework 1; Jensen step filled in)

$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\ge0$ with equality iff $P=Q$. The professor set this as homework, equivalent to "cross-entropy $\ge$ entropy"; the Jensen route below is filled in — not derived in class. Used in: justifying KL as a training loss; the ELBO argument in later lectures.

  1. Work over the support $S=\{i:p_i>0\}$. Negate the KL: $$-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\sum_{i\in S}p_i\log\frac{q_i}{p_i}.$$ $-\log(a/b)=\log(b/a)$
  2. The weights $p_i$ are positive and sum to 1, and $\log$ is concave, so with $t_i=q_i/p_i$: $$\sum_{i\in S}p_i\log t_i\;\le\;\log\Big(\sum_{i\in S}p_i\,t_i\Big).$$ Jensen: $\log$ is concave
  3. $$\sum_{i\in S}p_i\,\frac{q_i}{p_i}=\sum_{i\in S}q_i\;\le\;1.$$ $q$ sums to 1 over all $i$
  4. $\log$ is increasing, so $-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\le\log\sum_{S}q_i\le\log 1=0$, i.e. $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\ge0$. monotonicity of $\log$
  5. Equality: $\log$ is strictly concave, so Jensen is an equality iff all $t_i$ are equal on $S$, say $t_i=c$, and we also need $\sum_S q_i=1$. Then $q_i=c\,p_i$ on $S$ and $q_i=0$ outside $S$, so $1=\sum q_i=c\sum p_i=c$, hence $Q=P$. Conversely $P=Q$ gives $\log 1=0$ in every term. equality case of Jensen
▪
Second proof in one line

Use $\ln t\le t-1$ (tangent line at $t=1$ lies above the concave $\ln$). Then $\sum_S p_i\ln\frac{q_i}{p_i}\le\sum_S p_i\big(\frac{q_i}{p_i}-1\big)=\sum_S q_i-\sum_S p_i\le 1-1=0$. Same conclusion, equality iff $q_i/p_i=1$. For densities replace sums by integrals: the proof is word-for-word the same.

Worked numeric checks

Using $P=(0.5,0.5)$, $Q=(0.9,0.1)$, $t=(1.8,\,0.2)$: $\sum p_i\log_2 t_i=0.5(0.848)+0.5(-2.322)=-0.737\le\log_2(\sum p_it_i)=\log_2 1=0$. ✓ ($-0.737=-D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ from section 4.)

Not symmetric: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P)=0.9\log_2\frac{0.9}{0.5}+0.1\log_2\frac{0.1}{0.5}=0.9(0.848)+0.1(-2.322)=0.531$ bits, while $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=0.737$ bits.

No triangle inequality (homework 3), Bernoulli parameters $0.5,\,0.9,\,0.99$ in bits: $D(0.5\|0.99)=2.329$, but $D(0.5\|0.9)+D(0.9\|0.99)=0.737+0.208=0.945<2.329$. The "direct" route is longer than the detour, so KL is a divergence, not a metric.

Remember for the exam
  • The two-line proof: negate KL, apply Jensen with the concave $\log$ and weights $p_i$, the inside sums to $\le1$, so $-\mathrm{KL}\le\log1=0$.
  • State the equality case: strict concavity forces $q_i/p_i$ constant, hence $P=Q$.
  • Zero iff equal is exactly what makes KL usable as the $D$ in $\arg\min_\theta D(p_X\|p_\theta)$.
Common mistake

Applying Jensen in the wrong direction (the inequality flips for convex $-\log$ versus concave $\log$), or forgetting that a single pointwise term $p_i\log\frac{p_i}{q_i}$ can be negative; only the sum is guaranteed $\ge0$.

6. Every f-divergence is non-negative; KL is the case f(u) = u log u

Statement (class of 22 Aug, homework 4 and 5; Jensen step filled in)

For $f$ convex with $f(1)=0$, $D_f(p_X\|p_\theta)=\int p_\theta(x)\,f\!\big(\tfrac{p_X(x)}{p_\theta(x)}\big)dx\ge0$, with equality iff $p_X=p_\theta$ (for strictly convex $f$ at 1). Choosing $f(u)=u\log u$ recovers KL. Used in: the f-GAN / variational divergence minimisation of the next lectures.

  1. Let $u(x)=\dfrac{p_X(x)}{p_\theta(x)}\ge0$ (likelihood ratio; assume $p_\theta>0$ wherever $p_X>0$). Then $$D_f=\int p_\theta(x)\,f\big(u(x)\big)\,dx=\mathbb E_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[f(u(x))\big].$$ integral against a density = expectation
  2. $$\mathbb E_{p_\theta}\big[f(u)\big]\;\ge\;f\Big(\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[u]\Big).$$ Jensen: $f$ convex
  3. $$\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[u]=\int p_\theta\,\frac{p_X}{p_\theta}\,dx=\int p_X\,dx=1.$$ $p_\theta$ cancels, $p_X$ integrates to 1
  4. Therefore $D_f\ge f(1)=0$. $f(1)=0$ by assumption
  5. Equality: if $p_X=p_\theta$ then $u\equiv1$ and $D_f=\int p_\theta f(1)=0$. Conversely, for $f$ strictly convex Jensen is an equality only when $u$ is constant; that constant is $1$ by step 3, so $p_X=p_\theta$. equality case of Jensen
  6. Special case KL: with $f(u)=u\log u$, $$p_\theta\,f\Big(\frac{p_X}{p_\theta}\Big)=p_\theta\,\frac{p_X}{p_\theta}\log\frac{p_X}{p_\theta}=p_X\log\frac{p_X}{p_\theta},$$ so $D_f=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_X\|p_\theta)$; and $f(1)=1\cdot\log1=0$, $f''(u)=1/u>0$ so the conditions hold. cancel $p_\theta$
▪
Worked numeric check

Discrete $p_X=(0.5,0.5)$, $p_\theta=(0.9,0.1)$, so $u=(0.5556,\;5)$ and $\sum p_\theta u=0.9(0.5556)+0.1(5)=1$.

  • KL ($f=u\ln u$, nats): $0.9(0.5556\ln0.5556)+0.1(5\ln5)=0.9(-0.3266)+0.1(8.047)=-0.294+0.805=0.511$ nats $=0.737$ bits. ✓ (same as section 4)
  • Total variation ($f=\tfrac12|u-1|$): $0.9(0.222)+0.1(2)=0.2+0.2=0.40$, and indeed $\tfrac12\sum|p_X-p_\theta|=\tfrac12(0.4+0.4)=0.40$. ✓

Other choices from class: Jensen–Shannon uses $f(u)=\tfrac12\big[u\log u-(u+1)\log\tfrac{u+1}{2}\big]$. The definition is a template: any convex $f$ with $f(1)=0$ gives a valid divergence.

Remember for the exam
  • Recipe: write $D_f$ as $\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[f(u)]$, apply Jensen, note $\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[u]=1$, conclude $\ge f(1)=0$.
  • Two conditions on $f$: convex and $f(1)=0$.
  • KL: $f(u)=u\log u$. This family is the setting for the variational (GAN) derivation.
Common mistake

Taking the expectation under $p_X$ instead of $p_\theta$: the weight in $D_f$ is $p_\theta$, and it is exactly under $p_\theta$ that the ratio $u$ has mean 1. Also, the argument of $f$ is $p_X/p_\theta$ (data over model), not the reverse.

7. Forward versus reverse KL: which error does each punish?

Statement (class of 22 Aug, section 13)

A good divergence must be large wherever model and data disagree. Forward KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_X\|p_\theta)$ is large when the data has mass the model misses (missing modes) but nearly blind to model mass where there is no data (junk). Reverse KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta\|p_X)$ is the opposite. This is the argument that motivated moving to the f-divergence family. (An argument by pointwise comparison rather than a theorem; the professor first stated the two cases the wrong way round and corrected himself after a student's remark. The table below is the corrected version.)

  1. Forward-KL integrand at a point $x$: $\;p_X(x)\log\dfrac{p_X(x)}{p_\theta(x)}$; reverse-KL integrand: $\;p_\theta(x)\log\dfrac{p_\theta(x)}{p_X(x)}$. definitions
  2. Case A, a real mode the model misses: $p_X(x')=0.5$, $p_\theta(x')=0.01$. Forward: $$0.5\ln\frac{0.5}{0.01}=0.5\ln50=1.96\ \text{(large)}.$$ big ratio times big weight $p_X$
  3. Case A, reverse: $$0.01\ln\frac{0.01}{0.5}=0.01\ln0.02=-0.039\ \text{(tiny)}.$$ weight $p_\theta=0.01$ is tiny
  4. Case B, junk mass: $p_X(x'')=0.01$, $p_\theta(x'')=0.5$. Forward: $$0.01\ln\frac{0.01}{0.5}=-0.039\ \text{(negligible)}.$$ weight $p_X$ is tiny
  5. Case B, reverse: $$0.5\ln\frac{0.5}{0.01}=1.96\ \text{(large)}.$$ big weight $p_\theta$, big ratio
  6. Conclusion: forward KL penalises missing modes (A) but not hallucinations (B); reverse KL does the opposite. Each handles only one error, so we want a divergence that can be tuned, which leads to the f-divergence family. steps 2–5

Remember for the exam
  • Forward KL (data first): weighted by $p_X$, punishes missing modes; blind to hallucinations.
  • Reverse KL: weighted by $p_\theta$, punishes hallucinated / junk regions; blind to missing modes.
  • On 5 Sep the professor added: empirically, for sufficiently expressive models, forward KL alone works and all LLMs are trained with it.
Common mistake

Swapping A and B (the lecture's own slip). Test yourself: the weight in front of the log is the first argument, so the first argument decides which region of space is "watched".

8. Law of large numbers: expectations become sample averages

Statement (classes of 22 Aug and 29 Aug)

$\displaystyle\int h(x)\,p_X(x)\,dx=\mathbb E_{X\sim p_X}[h(X)]\approx\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n h(x_i)$ for i.i.d. $x_i\sim p_X$, with the approximation exact as $n\to\infty$. The right-hand side needs samples from $p_X$, never the density itself. The professor called it the most important idea driving modern ML: "whatever we want to compute must be an expectation with respect to a distribution we can sample from". The class states the LLN; the proof below (Chebyshev, weak form) is filled in — not written out in class. Used in: f-GAN objectives, ELBO training, MLE on a dataset.

  1. Let $\mu=\int h(x)\,p_X(x)\,dx=\mathbb E[h(X)]$ and $\sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}(h(X))<\infty$. Define the sample average $S_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n h(x_i)$. definitions; $\int h\,p=\mathbb E[h]$
  2. $$\mathbb E[S_n]=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E[h(x_i)]=\frac1n\,n\mu=\mu.$$ linearity of $\mathbb E$; identically distributed So $S_n$ is unbiased.
  3. $$\mathrm{Var}(S_n)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathrm{Var}(h(x_i))=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.$$ independence: variances add; $\mathrm{Var}(aY)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(Y)$
  4. For any $\varepsilon>0$: $$P\big(|S_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon\big)\;\le\;\frac{\mathrm{Var}(S_n)}{\varepsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\,\varepsilon^2}.$$ Chebyshev's inequality
  5. The bound tends to 0 as $n\to\infty$, i.e. $S_n\to\mu$ in probability. The typical error is of size $\sigma/\sqrt n$: to halve the error you need four times the data. $\sigma^2/n\varepsilon^2\to0$
  6. Consequence used all course long: $$\int h(x)\,p_X(x)\,dx\;\approx\;\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n h(x_i),\qquad x_i\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}p_X,$$ so an objective written as expectations under $p_X$ and $p_\theta$ can be evaluated from the dataset and from generated samples $G_\theta(z_i)$, without knowing either density. step 5

Worked numeric check

$h(x)=x^2$, $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$: $\mu=\mathbb E[X^2]=1$, $\mathbb E[X^4]=3$, so $\sigma^2=3-1=2$. For $n=100$ the standard error is $\sqrt{2/100}=0.14$, and Chebyshev gives $P(|S_{100}-1|\ge0.5)\le\frac{2}{100(0.25)}=0.08$. For $n=10{,}000$ the standard error is $0.014$.

Remember for the exam
  • Integral against a density = expectation = limit of a sample average; unknown density, known samples is enough.
  • It only becomes tight as $n\to\infty$; "data is the new oil" plus GPUs made large $n$ real. But $n$ is still finite: with all the data a lookup table would suffice, and the point of a generative model is to generalise.
  • Use it to turn $D_f$, the GAN objective and the ELBO into trainable sample averages.
Common mistake

The samples must come from the distribution in the subscript of $\mathbb E$. Also, you cannot move a $\log$ outside an expectation (a log of an expectation cannot be estimated by a plain sample average of logs: $\log\mathbb E[Y]\ne\mathbb E[\log Y]$); this is the obstacle that later motivates the ELBO.

9. Maximum likelihood = minimising the forward KL

Statement (class of 5 Sep; LLN link from 29 Aug)

$\arg\min_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_X\|P_\theta)=\arg\max_\theta\mathbb E_{X\sim P_X}[\log P_\theta(x)]$, and with the LLN the right-hand side is the maximum-likelihood objective $\arg\max_\theta\frac1n\sum_i\log P_\theta(x_i)$. The professor called this "an extremely important result": "maximising likelihood is better understood as minimising the KL divergence". All LLMs are trained this way. Steps 6–7 (connection to the product likelihood) were only indicated, filled in here.

  1. $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_X\|P_\theta)=\int P_X(x)\log\frac{P_X(x)}{P_\theta(x)}\,dx.$$ definition
  2. $$=\int P_X(x)\log P_X(x)\,dx-\int P_X(x)\log P_\theta(x)\,dx.$$ $\log(a/b)=\log a-\log b$
  3. The first term equals $-H(P_X)$: it does not contain $\theta$, so it is a constant (we cannot change the entropy of the true data by tweaking the model). no $\theta$ appears
  4. $$\theta^*=\arg\min_\theta\Big\{-\int P_X(x)\log P_\theta(x)\,dx\Big\}.$$ adding a constant does not move the $\arg\min$
  5. $$\theta^*=\arg\max_\theta\int P_X(x)\log P_\theta(x)\,dx=\arg\max_\theta\ \mathbb E_{X\sim P_X}\big[\log P_\theta(x)\big].$$ $\min(-g)=\max g$; integral = expectation
  6. Replace the expectation by a sample average of the data $x_1,\dots,x_n\sim P_X$ with $h=\log P_\theta$: $$\theta^*\approx\arg\max_\theta\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log P_\theta(x_i).$$ law of large numbers (section 8)
  7. For i.i.d. data the joint likelihood is $\prod_iP_\theta(x_i)$, so $$\frac1n\sum_i\log P_\theta(x_i)=\frac1n\log\prod_iP_\theta(x_i).$$ $\log$ of a product is a sum Since $\log$ is increasing and $\frac1n>0$ this has the same maximiser as the likelihood: this is exactly maximum likelihood estimation, with $\log P_\theta(x)$ the log-likelihood.

Remember for the exam
  • Expand the KL, drop the $\theta$-free entropy term, flip $\min$ to $\max$: MLE falls out. The only trick is the constant.
  • MLE = minimising the forward KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_X\|P_\theta)$ (data first), which is why LLMs trained by maximum likelihood minimise forward KL.
  • The LLN supplies the last step: expectation over the unknown $P_X$ becomes an average over the training set.
Common mistake

Doing the same with the reverse KL: $\int P_\theta\log\frac{P_\theta}{P_X}$ contains $\log P_X$ inside a $\theta$-dependent expectation, which needs the unknown density, so it does not reduce to a likelihood. Also, maximum likelihood needs $P_\theta(x_i)$ to be evaluable; implicit models (GAN generators) have no such density, which is why they use other divergences.
