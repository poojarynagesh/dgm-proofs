Flow matching

Every derivation from the two flow-matching lectures (10 Oct and 17 Oct) plus the 17 Oct tutorial, one section per result: density change under a flow, the continuity equation, the marginal path, the marginalisation theorem, the conditional-flow-matching theorem, the Gaussian conditional velocity, and Euler/RK4 sampling. Each proof has a picture, numbered steps with the reason for each line, and a worked numeric check.

One-paragraph summary

Flow matching moves noise $x_0\sim p_{\text{init}}=\mathcal N(0,I)$ to data by the ODE $\tfrac{d}{dt}x_t=u_t(x_t)$. A probability path $p_t$ is generated by a field $u_t$ exactly when they satisfy the continuity equation $\partial_t p_t+\nabla\!\cdot(p_tu_t)=0$. We cannot write the field for the whole dataset, but for one data point $z$ a Gaussian path has an explicit field $u_t(x\mid z)$. The marginalisation theorem says the posterior-weighted average of those conditional fields generates the marginal path to the data; the CFM theorem says regressing on the (easy) conditional field has the same gradient as regressing on the (intractable) marginal field. Training is therefore a plain MSE on $x_1-x_0$, and sampling is an ODE solve.

Notation (follows the lectures)

$z$ = one data point, $p_{\text{data}}(z)$ the data law; $x$ = the current state, $x_t$ its value at time $t\in[0,1]$; $p_t(x\mid z)$ conditional path, $p_t(x)$ marginal path; $u_t(x\mid z)$ conditional field, $u_t(x)$ marginal field; $u_\theta(t,x)$ the network. Gaussian path: $p_t(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(\alpha_tz,\beta_t^2I)$ with $\alpha_0=0,\beta_0=1,\alpha_1=1,\beta_1=0$. Tutorial notation for the linear schedule: $x_0$ = noise, $x_1$ = data, so $x_1$ plays the role of $z$ and $x_0$ the role of $\epsilon$ (note: opposite to DDPM, where $x_0$ is data).

1. Flow ODE, push-forward and the density change

Statement

If $x_t=\phi_t(x_0)$ solves $\dot x_t=u_t(x_t)$ with $x_0\sim p_0$, then $p_t=(\phi_t)_\#p_0$ and along a trajectory $\tfrac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t)=-\nabla\!\cdot u_t(x_t)$. Source: 10 Oct lecture §4–§6 (push-forward, vector field, ODE, flow map $\psi_t$); the Jacobian/divergence formula is (step filled in — not written out in class). Used later: it is the reason a vector field defines a distribution path, and it leads straight to the continuity equation (§2).

  1. A vector field $u:[0,1]\times\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$ defines the ODE; its solution map from initial value $x_0$ is the flow $\phi_t$: $$\frac{d}{dt}\phi_t(x_0)=u_t(\phi_t(x_0)),\qquad \phi_0(x_0)=x_0 .$$definition of flow; unique solution for Lipschitz $u$
  2. If $x_0\sim p_0$ then $X_t=\phi_t(X_0)$ has law $p_t:=(\phi_t)_\#p_0$ (the push-forward, as for a GAN generator $x=G(z)$, but obtained by solving an ODE). definition of push-forward
  3. Each $\phi_t$ is a smooth invertible map (uniqueness lets us run the ODE backwards), so the change-of-variables formula gives $$p_t(\phi_t(x_0))\,\big|\det D\phi_t(x_0)\big|=p_0(x_0).$$change of variables, density of an invertible map
  4. Take the logarithm and differentiate in $t$ along the trajectory: $$\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(\phi_t(x_0))=-\frac{d}{dt}\log\big|\det D\phi_t(x_0)\big| .$$$p_0(x_0)$ does not depend on $t$
  5. Write $A_t=D\phi_t(x_0)$ (the Jacobian matrix). Differentiating step 1 in $x_0$ gives $\dot A_t=Du_t(\phi_t(x_0))\,A_t$. chain rule, swap $\partial_t$ and $D$
  6. Jacobi's formula for the derivative of a determinant: $\frac{d}{dt}\log|\det A_t|=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(A_t^{-1}\dot A_t\big)$. Substituting step 5, $$\frac{d}{dt}\log|\det A_t|=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(A_t^{-1}Du_t\,A_t\big)=\operatorname{tr}\big(Du_t(\phi_t(x_0))\big)=\nabla\!\cdot u_t(\phi_t(x_0)).$$Jacobi's formula; trace is cyclic; $\operatorname{tr}Du=\operatorname{div}u$
  7. Combine steps 4 and 6: $$\boxed{\;\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t)=-\nabla\!\cdot u_t(x_t)\;}\qquad x_t=\phi_t(x_0).$$divergence = rate of volume change
▪
Worked numeric check (1-D linear field, verified in Python)

Take $u_t(x)=x$ and $p_0=\mathcal N(0,1)$. The flow is $\phi_t(x_0)=x_0e^{t}$, so $p_t=\mathcal N(0,e^{2t})$. Here $\nabla\!\cdot u=1$, so the formula predicts $\log p_t(x_t)-\log p_0(x_0)=-t$. At $x_0=1$, $t=0.5$: $x_t=e^{0.5}=1.6487$ and $\log\mathcal N(1.6487;0,e^{1})-\log\mathcal N(1;0,1)=-0.5$ exactly. ✓

Remember for the exam
  • Time convention: $t\in[0,1]$, $t=0$ is the prior $p_{\text{init}}$ (Gaussian), $t=1$ is the data. The field depends on both $x$ and $t$; $\mathbb R^d\times[0,1]$ is a Cartesian product (domain), not a cross product.
  • Existence and uniqueness of the ODE solution needs a Lipschitz field with bounded derivatives; that is what makes generation well defined.
  • A flow is a deterministic push-forward of the prior. Diversity comes only from the random initial draw. It is not one step: one still integrates the ODE numerically.
  • The ODE path is not defined as a Markov chain; Markovianity is not needed.
Common mistake

Forgetting the minus sign: positive divergence (spreading) lowers density. Also, $\partial_tp_t(x)$ at a fixed point is not $\frac{d}{dt}p_t(x_t)$ along a moving trajectory; the difference is the transport term $u\cdot\nabla p_t$ (see §2, Part B step 8).

2. The continuity equation

Statement

If $x_t$ moves with velocity $u_t$ and has density $p_t$, then $\;\partial_tp_t(x)=-\nabla_x\!\cdot\big(p_t(x)\,u_t(x)\big)$, i.e. probability is conserved. Conversely a field $u_t$ generates the path $p_t$ iff the equation holds. Source: 17 Oct lecture §4.1–4.2 (stated as a physics result; the 10 Oct lecture said its proof was postponed). The derivation below is (step filled in — not written out in class). Used later: the marginalisation theorem (§4) is proved by checking exactly this equation.

Part A — local intuition (1-D).

  1. The mass in $[x,x+dx]$ is $p_t(x)\,dx$.density times length
  2. Mass crosses a point at rate flux $F=p\,u$ (density $\times$ velocity).mass per unit time = density $\times$ speed
  3. Conservation: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(p_t(x)dx\big)=F_t(x)-F_t(x+dx)$.inflow minus outflow
  4. Divide by $dx$ and let $dx\to0$: $\partial_tp_t=-\partial_xF_t=-\partial_x(p_tu_t)$.definition of derivative

Part B — rigorous derivation in $\mathbb R^d$ with a test function. Let $f:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R$ be smooth with compact support.

  1. Differentiate the expectation of $f(X_t)$: $$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[f(X_t)]=\frac{d}{dt}\int f(x)\,p_t(x)\,dx=\int f(x)\,\partial_tp_t(x)\,dx .$$swap $d/dt$ and $\int$
  2. Differentiate inside the expectation instead, along the trajectory: $$\frac{d}{dt}f(X_t)=\nabla f(X_t)\cdot\dot X_t=\nabla f(X_t)\cdot u_t(X_t).$$chain rule and the ODE $\dot X_t=u_t(X_t)$
  3. Take the expectation under $X_t\sim p_t$: $$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[f(X_t)]=\int \nabla f(x)\cdot u_t(x)\,p_t(x)\,dx .$$expectation of a function of $X_t$ is an integral against $p_t$
  4. Product rule for divergence: $\nabla\!\cdot\big(f\,p_tu_t\big)=\nabla f\cdot(p_tu_t)+f\,\nabla\!\cdot(p_tu_t)$. Integrate over $\mathbb R^d$; the left side integrates to zero: $$\int\nabla\!\cdot\big(f\,p_tu_t\big)\,dx=0 .$$divergence theorem; $f$ has compact support so no boundary term
  5. Hence $$\int\nabla f\cdot(p_tu_t)\,dx=-\int f\,\nabla\!\cdot(p_tu_t)\,dx .$$rearrange step 4 (integration by parts)
  6. Equate step 1 with step 3 and use step 5: $$\int f(x)\,\partial_tp_t(x)\,dx=-\int f(x)\,\nabla\!\cdot\big(p_tu_t\big)(x)\,dx\qquad\text{for every test function }f .$$both equal $\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E f(X_t)$
  7. A continuous function that integrates to zero against every test function is zero, so $$\boxed{\;\partial_tp_t(x)+\nabla_x\!\cdot\big(p_t(x)\,u_t(x)\big)=0\;}$$fundamental lemma of calculus of variations
  8. Cross-check with §1. Expand $\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t)=\partial_t\log p_t+u_t\cdot\nabla\log p_t$ (total derivative along the path). Setting this equal to $-\nabla\!\cdot u_t$ from §1 and multiplying by $p_t$ gives $\partial_tp_t+u_t\cdot\nabla p_t+p_t\nabla\!\cdot u_t=\partial_tp_t+\nabla\!\cdot(p_tu_t)=0$, the same equation.product rule for divergence
▪

Converse (stated in class, not proved): under the usual regularity assumptions, if $p_t$ and $u_t$ satisfy the equation with $p_0$ given, then $p_t$ is the law of the ODE flow of $u_t$ started from $p_0$ (solutions of the continuity equation are unique). This is why checking the equation proves that a field generates a path (used in §4).

Worked numeric check (verified)

$u_t(x)=x$, $p_0=\mathcal N(0,1)$, so at $t=0$: $p(x)=\phi(x)$ with $\phi'(x)=-x\phi(x)$. Take $x=2$: $p=0.05399$. Left side: $p_t=\mathcal N(0,e^{2t})$, $\partial_tp_t(x)\big|_{t=0}=p\,(x^2-1)=3p=0.16197$. Right side: $-\partial_x(p\,u)=-(p+x\,p')=-(p-x^2p)=p(x^2-1)=3p=0.16197$. ✓

Remember for the exam
  • Write it as $\partial_tp_t=-\nabla\!\cdot(p_tu_t)$ and name the pieces: $p_tu_t$ = probability flux, $\nabla\!\cdot$ = scalar divergence $\sum_k\partial v_k/\partial x_k$.
  • Fluid analogy used in class: probability is a conserved fluid; the minus sign makes density fall under net outflow and rise under net inflow.
  • Main use: a path and a field are compatible iff they satisfy this equation. The marginalisation proof is just this equation checked.
Common mistake

Dropping $p_t$ inside the divergence, writing $-p_t\nabla\!\cdot u_t$ or $-\nabla\!\cdot u_t$. The divergence acts on the product $p_tu_t$, and this is what lets us pull the integral over $z$ inside in §4.

3. Marginal probability path and its endpoints

Statement

If the conditional paths satisfy $p_0(x\mid z)=p_{\text{init}}(x)$ and $p_1(x\mid z)=\delta_z(x)$, then the marginal $p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid z)p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz$ satisfies $p_0=p_{\text{init}}$ and $p_1=p_{\text{data}}$; the Gaussian path $\mathcal N(\alpha_tz,\beta_t^2I)$ meets these endpoint conditions. Source: 10 Oct lecture §7.2–7.3 and §8 (students were assigned to verify the endpoints), 17 Oct lecture §2.3 and §6.1. Used later: it tells us that generating the marginal path produces data, so we only need to design cheap per-sample paths.

  1. The joint of state and target is $p_t(x,z)=p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)$.chain rule of probability
  2. Integrating out $z$ defines the marginal path: $$p_t(x)=\int p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz .$$law of total probability
  3. At $t=0$ every conditional equals the prior, which does not depend on $z$: $$p_0(x)=\int p_{\text{init}}(x)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz=p_{\text{init}}(x)\underbrace{\int p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz}_{=1}=p_{\text{init}}(x).$$pull constant out; densities integrate to 1
  4. At $t=1$ every conditional is a point mass at its own $z$: $$p_1(x)=\int\delta_z(x)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz=\int\delta(x-z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz=p_{\text{data}}(x).$$sifting property of the Dirac delta
  5. Gaussian path, $t=0$: $p_0(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(x;\alpha_0z,\beta_0^2I)=\mathcal N(x;0,I)=p_{\text{init}}$ because $\alpha_0=0,\ \beta_0=1$.plug in boundary values
  6. Gaussian path, $t=1$: $\mathcal N(x;\alpha_1z,\beta_1^2I)=\mathcal N(x;z,0)$. Its characteristic function is $e^{i\omega^{\!\top}\alpha_tz-\beta_t^2\|\omega\|^2/2}\to e^{i\omega^{\!\top}z}$ as $t\to1$, the characteristic function of $\delta_z$.zero-variance Gaussian = Dirac delta
  7. Sampling the path: with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, $x_t=\alpha_tz+\beta_t\epsilon$ has mean $\alpha_tz$ and covariance $\beta_t^2I$.affine map of a Gaussian
▪
Worked numeric check (two data points, $\alpha_t=t,\ \beta_t=1-t$)

Data $z\in\{-1,3\}$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each: $p_t(x)=\tfrac12\mathcal N(x;-t,(1-t)^2)+\tfrac12\mathcal N(x;3t,(1-t)^2)$. At $t=0$ both components are $\mathcal N(0,1)$, so the mixture is $\mathcal N(0,1)$. At $t=0.9$ the components are $\mathcal N(-0.9,0.1^2)$ and $\mathcal N(2.7,0.1^2)$: two narrow bumps; as $t\to1$ they become $\tfrac12\delta_{-1}+\tfrac12\delta_3=p_{\text{data}}$.

Remember for the exam
  • $z$ is one particular data point, not the dataset. $p_t(x\mid z)$ is the law of the state at time $t$ given target $z$; $x$ there is the time-$t$ state $x_t$.
  • Conditional path: $p_0(\cdot\mid z)=p_{\text{init}}$, $p_1(\cdot\mid z)=\delta_z$. Marginal path: average over $p_{\text{data}}$. For a finite dataset it is $\frac1N\sum_ip_t(x\mid z_i)$.
  • Gaussian path requires $\alpha_0=0,\beta_0=1,\alpha_1=1,\beta_1=0$, differentiable monotone; different valid schedules give different paths.
Common mistake (asked in class)

“Each conditional path ends at one training point, so this just memorises the data.” No: a single conditional path is tied to one $z$, but the marginal path (the mixture) is what the learned field transports; at generation we start from a fresh $\mathcal N(0,I)$ sample and follow the marginal field. Novelty is an empirical property, not a theorem.

4. The marginalisation theorem

Statement

Suppose for every $z$ the conditional field $u_t(x\mid z)$ generates $p_t(x\mid z)$. Then $$u_t(x)=\frac{\int u_t(x\mid z)\,p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz}{p_t(x)}=\mathbb E\big[u_t(x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x\big]$$ generates the marginal path $p_t(x)$; in particular its ODE carries $p_{\text{init}}$ to $p_{\text{data}}$. Source: 10 Oct lecture §9.1 (statement), 17 Oct lecture §3.2 and §4.3 “Marginalisation proof, written out” (the proof below follows it line by line; the Bayes-rule form and the final conclusion step are expanded). Used later: it is the ingredient in the CFM theorem (§5).

Proof. Assume $\partial_tp_t(x\mid z)=-\nabla_x\!\cdot[p_t(x\mid z)u_t(x\mid z)]$ for every $z$ (§2).

  1. Start from the marginal path: $$\partial_tp_t(x)=\partial_t\int p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz .$$definition of marginal path (§3)
  2. Move the time derivative inside: $$\partial_tp_t(x)=\int \partial_tp_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz .$$swap $\partial_t$ and $\int$ (regularity assumed in class)
  3. Insert the conditional continuity equation for each $z$: $$\partial_tp_t(x)=-\int\nabla_x\!\cdot\big[p_t(x\mid z)\,u_t(x\mid z)\big]\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz .$$each conditional field generates its conditional path
  4. Pull the divergence outside the integral over $z$: $$\partial_tp_t(x)=-\nabla_x\!\cdot\Big[\int p_t(x\mid z)\,u_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz\Big] .$$$\nabla_x$ does not act on $z$ or $p_{\text{data}}(z)$; swap $\nabla_x\!\cdot$ and $\int$
  5. Multiply the definition of $u_t$ by $p_t(x)$ (assume $p_t(x)>0$): $$p_t(x)\,u_t(x)=\int u_t(x\mid z)\,p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz .$$definition of the marginal field
  6. Substitute step 5 into the bracket of step 4: $$\partial_tp_t(x)=-\nabla_x\!\cdot\big[p_t(x)\,u_t(x)\big].$$this is the marginal continuity equation
  7. Conclusion (filled in). $p_t,u_t$ satisfy the continuity equation, $p_0=p_{\text{init}}$ (§3 step 3), so by the converse in §2 the ODE $\dot x=u_t(x)$ started from $p_{\text{init}}$ has law $p_t$ at every $t$; at $t=1$, $p_1=p_{\text{data}}$ (§3 step 4).continuity equation characterises generation
  8. Conditional-expectation form (filled in). By Bayes' rule $p_t(z\mid x)=\dfrac{p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)}{p_t(x)}$, so $$u_t(x)=\int u_t(x\mid z)\,\underbrace{\frac{p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)}{p_t(x)}}_{p_t(z\mid x)}\,dz=\mathbb E\big[u_t(x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x\big].$$Bayes rule

Worked numeric check (verified in Python)

Data $z\in\{-1,3\}$ (prob. $\tfrac12$ each), $\alpha_t=t,\beta_t=1-t$, evaluate at $t=0.5$, $x=1$. Conditionals: $p_t(x\mid -1)=\mathcal N(1;-0.5,0.5^2)=0.7979\,e^{-4.5}=0.008864$ and $p_t(x\mid 3)=\mathcal N(1;1.5,0.5^2)=0.7979\,e^{-0.5}=0.48394$. So $p_t(x)=\tfrac12(0.008864+0.48394)=0.24640$ and the posterior is $(0.01799,\ 0.98201)$. Conditional fields (using $u_t(x\mid z)=(z-x)/(1-t)$, §6): $(-1-1)/0.5=-4$ and $(3-1)/0.5=+4$. Marginal field $u_t(1)=0.01799(-4)+0.98201(4)=\mathbf{3.856}$. Continuity check by finite differences: $\partial_tp_t(1)=-1.5493$ equals $-\partial_x[p_tu_t](1)=-1.5493$. ✓

Remember for the exam
  • Marginal field = conditional velocities averaged with weights $p_t(x\mid z)p_{\text{data}}(z)/p_t(x)$, i.e. $\mathbb E[u_t(x\mid Z)\mid X_t=x]$.
  • Proof in one line each: swap $\partial_t$ and $\int$; use conditional continuity eq.; swap $\nabla\!\cdot$ and $\int$; recognise $p_tu_t$ from the definition.
  • It is a theoretical formula: it needs $p_{\text{data}}$ and all path densities, so it cannot be evaluated; that is why we need the CFM theorem.
Common mistake

Thinking the marginal field equals the conditional field for a chosen $z$ (a student asked this in class). The theorem relates their losses (§5), not the fields pointwise; the marginal field is the posterior average. Also the weights use the posterior over $z$ given the current $x$, not the prior $p_{\text{data}}(z)$.

5. The conditional-flow-matching theorem

Statement

$\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}(\theta)=\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}(\theta)+C$ where $C$ does not depend on $\theta$; hence $\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}$ and both have the same minimiser, the marginal field. Source: 10 Oct lecture §9.2–9.4 (6-step sketch), 17 Oct lecture §5.1–5.4 (proof assigned as an exercise; the algebra below is filled in — not written out in class). Used later: it justifies the training algorithm of §6: regress on $x_1-x_0$ and still learn the right marginal field.

Fix a time distribution $t\sim q(t)$ (uniform on $[0,1]$ in class). Write $u_\theta$ for $u_\theta(t,x)$ and use the marginalisation theorem of §4 for $u_t(x)$.

  1. Expand the marginal loss, taking $x\sim p_t$: $$\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}=\mathbb E_{t,x\sim p_t}\Big[\|u_\theta\|^2-2\,u_\theta\!\cdot u_t(x)+\|u_t(x)\|^2\Big].$$expand the square $\|a-b\|^2=\|a\|^2-2a\!\cdot\!b+\|b\|^2$
  2. Expand the conditional loss, taking $z\sim p_{\text{data}},\ x\sim p_t(\cdot\mid z)$: $$\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}=\mathbb E_{t,z,x\sim p_t(\cdot|z)}\Big[\|u_\theta\|^2-2\,u_\theta\!\cdot u_t(x\mid z)+\|u_t(x\mid z)\|^2\Big].$$same expansion
  3. Term 1 agrees. The integrand has no $z$-dependence, so $$\mathbb E_{z}\mathbb E_{x\sim p_t(\cdot|z)}\|u_\theta\|^2=\iint\|u_\theta(t,x)\|^2\,p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz\,dx=\int\|u_\theta\|^2\,p_t(x)\,dx=\mathbb E_{x\sim p_t}\|u_\theta\|^2 .$$Fubini; marginal path definition
  4. Cross term agrees. Since $u_\theta$ does not depend on $z$, integrate $z$ first: $$\iint u_\theta\!\cdot u_t(x\mid z)\,p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz\,dx=\int u_\theta(t,x)\cdot\Big[\int u_t(x\mid z)\,p_t(x\mid z)\,p_{\text{data}}(z)\,dz\Big]dx .$$Fubini; $u_\theta$ independent of $z$
  5. The bracket is $p_t(x)\,u_t(x)$, so the cross term equals $$\int u_\theta(t,x)\cdot u_t(x)\,p_t(x)\,dx=\mathbb E_{x\sim p_t}\big[u_\theta\!\cdot u_t(x)\big],$$ the cross term of $\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}$.marginalisation theorem: $\int u_t(x|z)p_t(x|z)p_{\text{data}}(z)dz=p_t(x)u_t(x)$
  6. Subtract: the two $\theta$-dependent terms cancel and $$\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}-\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{t,z,x\sim p_t(\cdot|z)}\|u_t(x\mid z)\|^2-\mathbb E_{t,x\sim p_t}\|u_t(x)\|^2}_{=:C}.$$term independent of $\theta$
  7. $C$ involves no network at all, so $\nabla_\theta C=0$ and $$\boxed{\;\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}(\theta)=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}(\theta)\;}$$gradient of a constant is zero
  8. Sign and size of $C$ (filled in). By Bayes (§4 step 8), $\mathbb E[\|u(x|Z)\|^2\mid X_t=x]-\|\mathbb E[u(x|Z)\mid X_t=x]\|^2=\operatorname{Var}[u(x|Z)\mid X_t=x]\ge0$, so $C=\mathbb E_{t,x}\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}[u_t(x|Z)\mid X_t=x]\ge0$ and $\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}\ge\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}$.variance identity $E\|U\|^2=\|EU\|^2+\operatorname{Var}$
  9. Why the minimiser is the marginal field (the “intuition via conditional expectation” of the 17 Oct lecture). For fixed $(t,x)$ and any vector $v$, with $U=u_t(x\mid Z)$ and $\bar u=\mathbb E[U\mid x]$: $$\mathbb E\big[\|v-U\|^2\mid x\big]=\|v-\bar u\|^2+\mathbb E\big[\|U-\bar u\|^2\mid x\big],$$ the cross term $2(v-\bar u)\cdot\mathbb E[\bar u-U\mid x]$ being zero. The first term is minimised at $v=\bar u=u_t(x)$.add and subtract the conditional mean

Remember for the exam
  • Expand the square; the model-squared term matches by the marginal-path definition, the cross term matches by the marginalisation identity, the target-squared term is $\theta$-free. Say these three things.
  • The losses have different values; the fields $u_t(x)$ and $u_t(x|z)$ are not equal; only the gradients and minimisers coincide.
  • Convention warning: the 10 Oct notes write $\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}=\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}+C$, the 17 Oct notes write $\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}=\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}+C$; the constant is the conditional variance, so strictly $\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}=\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}+C$ with $C\ge0$. Either way: $C$ independent of $\theta$.
  • Practical meaning: no need to compute the integral over all $z$ at each update; sample one $z$ (and noise, time) and regress.
Common mistake

Claiming the cross terms match “by definition”. They match only after taking the expectation over $z$ with $u_\theta$ held fixed, which is exactly where the marginal field enters. Another: pulling $u_\theta$ out of the $x$-integral; it depends on $x$.

6. Conditional velocity of a Gaussian path (and the straight-line case)

Statement

For $p_t(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(\alpha_tz,\beta_t^2I)$ the conditional field is $u_t(x\mid z)=\dot\alpha_tz+\frac{\dot\beta_t}{\beta_t}(x-\alpha_tz)$. For $\alpha_t=t,\ \beta_t=1-t$ the training target is $z-\epsilon$ (tutorial: $x_1-x_0$), constant in time. More generally with $p_t(x|z)=\mathcal N(\mu_t(z),\sigma_t^2I)$: $u_t(x\mid z)=\frac{\dot\sigma_t}{\sigma_t}(x-\mu_t(z))+\dot\mu_t(z)$. Source: 17 Oct lecture §6.1–6.4 (this was deferred from 10 Oct), 17 Oct tutorial §2–3. Used later: it gives the regression target of the training algorithm.

  1. Reparameterise a sample of the path: $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and $$x_t=\alpha_tz+\beta_t\epsilon\ \sim\ \mathcal N(\alpha_tz,\beta_t^2I)=p_t(x\mid z).$$affine map of a Gaussian (§3 step 7)
  2. Fix $(z,\epsilon)$ and differentiate in time; $z$ and $\epsilon$ are constants: $$\frac{dx_t}{dt}=\dot\alpha_t\,z+\dot\beta_t\,\epsilon .$$differentiate a time-weighted sum
  3. For $\beta_t>0$ invert the reparameterisation to express $\epsilon$ by the position $x=x_t$: $$\epsilon=\frac{x-\alpha_tz}{\beta_t}.$$solve $x=\alpha_tz+\beta_t\epsilon$ for $\epsilon$
  4. Substitute into step 2; the velocity as a function of position is $$\boxed{\;u_t(x\mid z)=\dot\alpha_t\,z+\frac{\dot\beta_t}{\beta_t}\,(x-\alpha_tz)\;}$$velocity at position $x$ = $dx_t/dt$ for the trajectory passing through $x$
  5. Equivalent linear-in-$x$ form: $$u_t(x\mid z)=\Big(\dot\alpha_t-\frac{\dot\beta_t}{\beta_t}\alpha_t\Big)z+\frac{\dot\beta_t}{\beta_t}\,x .$$collect terms in $z$ and $x$
  6. This field really generates $p_t(x\mid z)$ (filled in). The map $\psi_t(\epsilon)=\alpha_tz+\beta_t\epsilon$ satisfies $\frac{d}{dt}\psi_t(\epsilon)=u_t(\psi_t(\epsilon)\mid z)$ by steps 2–4 and $\psi_0(\epsilon)=\epsilon$; by uniqueness of ODE solutions it is the flow of $u_t(\cdot\mid z)$, and it pushes $\mathcal N(0,I)$ to $\mathcal N(\alpha_tz,\beta_t^2I)$.uniqueness of the ODE solution; push-forward of Gaussian
  7. General $\mu_t(z),\sigma_t$ (the form in the task description). Repeat with $x_t=\mu_t(z)+\sigma_t\epsilon$: $\dot x_t=\dot\mu_t+\dot\sigma_t\epsilon$ and $\epsilon=(x-\mu_t)/\sigma_t$, giving $u_t(x\mid z)=\dot\mu_t(z)+\frac{\dot\sigma_t}{\sigma_t}(x-\mu_t(z))$. The class case is $\mu_t=\alpha_tz$, $\sigma_t=\beta_t$.same computation, renamed
  8. Linear (optimal-transport) schedule $\alpha_t=t,\ \beta_t=1-t$: $\dot\alpha_t=1,\ \dot\beta_t=-1$ and $x_t=tz+(1-t)\epsilon$. At the point $x=x_t$, $$u_t(x_t\mid z)=z-\frac{x_t-tz}{1-t}=z-\epsilon .$$$(x_t-tz)/(1-t)=\epsilon$ by step 3
  9. Directly, the sign of the $\epsilon$ term comes from the derivative of $(1-t)$: $$\frac{d}{dt}\big[tz+(1-t)\epsilon\big]=z-\epsilon .$$derivative of $(1-t)\epsilon$ is $-\epsilon$
  10. In the tutorial's notation ($x_0$ noise, $x_1$ data): $x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1$ and $\dfrac{d}{dt}x_t=-x_0+x_1$, so $$u_t(x_t\mid x_1)=x_1-x_0,\qquad \mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}=\mathbb E\,\|v_\theta(x_t,t)-(x_1-x_0)\|^2 .$$same as step 8 with $x_0\leftrightarrow\epsilon$, $x_1\leftrightarrow z$
  11. Position-only form: substituting $\epsilon$ as in step 3, $u_t(x\mid z)=\dfrac{z-x}{1-t}$: “point at $z$ with the speed that arrives at $t=1$”.$z-\frac{x-tz}{1-t}=\frac{(1-t)z-x+tz}{1-t}$

Worked numeric checks (verified in Python)
  1. Tutorial pair $x_0=-1,\ x_1=3$: $x_t=-1+4t$, so velocity $=x_1-x_0=4$ for every $t$. At $t=0.25$: $x_t=0$ and the position form $(z-x)/(1-t)=(3-0)/0.75=4$. ✓
  2. Lecture example $z=4,\ \epsilon=-2,\ t=0.25$: $x_t=0.25(4)+0.75(-2)=-0.5$; target $z-\epsilon=6$; position form $(4+0.5)/0.75=6$. ✓
  3. General formula, linear schedule at $z=2,\ t=0.25,\ x=1$: $\alpha z=0.5$; $u=1\cdot2+\frac{-1}{0.75}(1-0.5)=2-0.6667=1.3333$; position form $(2-1)/0.75=1.3333$. ✓
  4. Non-linear schedule $\alpha_t=t^2,\ \beta_t=1-t$ at $z=2,\ t=0.5,\ x=1.2$: $\dot\alpha=2t=1$, $\alpha z=0.5$, $\beta=0.5$, $\dot\beta=-1$: $u=1\cdot2+\frac{-1}{0.5}(1.2-0.5)=2-1.4=0.6$; via $\epsilon=(x-\alpha z)/\beta=1.4$: $\dot\alpha z+\dot\beta\epsilon=2-1.4=0.6$. ✓
Remember for the exam
  • Method: reparameterise ($x_t=\alpha_tz+\beta_t\epsilon$) $\to$ differentiate in $t$ with $z,\epsilon$ fixed $\to$ eliminate $\epsilon$ in favour of $x$. State the result $u_t(x|z)=\dot\alpha_tz+\frac{\dot\beta_t}{\beta_t}(x-\alpha_tz)$.
  • With $\alpha_t=t,\beta_t=1-t$: target $=z-\epsilon$, the minus on $\epsilon$ comes from $\frac{d}{dt}(1-t)=-1$ (asked in class).
  • Training step: sample $z$, $t\sim U[0,1]$, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$; $x_t=tz+(1-t)\epsilon$; loss $\|u_\theta(t,x_t)-(z-\epsilon)\|^2$. The network output is a velocity, not an image. Sample $t$ independently per example and broadcast it over pixels.
Common mistake

(i) Using DDPM's convention in the tutorial formula: here $x_0$ is noise and $x_1$ is data, so the target is $x_1-x_0$, not $x_0-x_1$. (ii) The coefficient $\dot\beta_t/\beta_t=-1/(1-t)$ blows up as $t\to1$, but $x-\alpha_tz\to0$ at the same rate, so the product stays finite ($=-\epsilon$); never evaluate the position form at $x\ne\alpha_tz$ exactly at $t=1$.

7. Euler (and RK4) sampling from the ODE

Statement

Generation solves $\dot x_t=u_\theta(t,x_t)$, $x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, numerically. The explicit Euler step is $x_{k+1}=x_k+h\,u_\theta(t_k,x_k)$ with $h=1/N$, first-order accurate; classical RK4 uses four velocity evaluations per step and is fourth-order. For a straight-line (constant-velocity) path Euler is exact. Source: 10 Oct lecture §5.5, 17 Oct lecture §7.2–7.3, 17 Oct tutorial §5–6 (the error analysis and the RK4 check are filled in — not written out in class). Used later: every generated sample in the course comes from this loop.

  1. The ODE in integral form: $$x_{t+h}=x_t+\int_t^{t+h}u_s(x_s)\,ds .$$fundamental theorem of calculus
  2. Freeze the integrand at its left-end value (rectangle rule): $$\int_t^{t+h}u_s(x_s)\,ds\approx h\,u_t(x_t).$$first-order Taylor: $x_{t+h}=x_t+h\dot x_t+O(h^2)$ with $\dot x_t=u_t(x_t)$
  3. On the uniform grid $t_k=kh$, $h=1/N$, starting from $x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$: $$\boxed{\;x_{k+1}=x_k+h\,u_\theta(t_k,x_k),\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1\;}$$apply step 2 repeatedly; each step reuses the previous state
  4. Error (filled in). The Taylor remainder is $\tfrac{h^2}{2}\ddot x(\xi)$: local error $O(h^2)$. Over $N=1/h$ steps these add up to a global error $O(h)$, so Euler is first order.$N\cdot O(h^2)=O(h)$ for a Lipschitz field
  5. Exactness for straight paths (filled in). If the velocity is constant along the path, as for a conditional linear path ($u=z-\epsilon$), then $\ddot x=0$ and $\sum_kh(z-\epsilon)=z-\epsilon$ from $x_0=\epsilon$ reaches $x_1=z$ exactly. The marginal field does vary with $(t,x)$, so a finite $N$ is needed; the closer the learned paths are to straight, the fewer steps are needed.zero second derivative kills the remainder
  6. RK4. Using four slope estimates per step of size $h$ (tutorial §6): $$k_1=u(x_n,t_n),\quad k_2=u\big(x_n+\tfrac h2k_1,\,t_n+\tfrac h2\big),\quad k_3=u\big(x_n+\tfrac h2k_2,\,t_n+\tfrac h2\big),\quad k_4=u(x_n+hk_3,\,t_n+h)$$ $$x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac h6\big(k_1+2k_2+2k_3+k_4\big).$$Simpson-type weighting of slopes at start, midpoint (twice), end
  7. Why RK4 is fourth order (check on the linear test ODE $\dot x=\lambda x$, filled in). With $s=\lambda h$: $k_1=\lambda x$, $k_2=\lambda x(1+\tfrac s2)$, $k_3=\lambda x(1+\tfrac s2+\tfrac{s^2}{4})$, $k_4=\lambda x(1+s+\tfrac{s^2}{2}+\tfrac{s^3}{4})$, so $$x_{n+1}=x_n\Big(1+s+\frac{s^2}{2}+\frac{s^3}{6}+\frac{s^4}{24}\Big),$$ which matches $e^{s}$ through $s^4$ (local error $O(h^5)$), whereas Euler's factor $1+s$ matches only to $s^1$.sum the four slopes and compare with the Taylor series of $e^s$
  8. Cost: Euler uses 1 network evaluation per step, RK4 uses 4; RK4 is usually much more accurate for the same $N$.count evaluations

Worked numeric check (verified)

$\dot x=x$, $x(0)=1$, exact $x(1)=e=2.71828$. Euler $x_{k+1}=(1+h)x_k$: $N=2$ ($h=0.5$): $x_1=1.5$, $x_2=2.25$; $N=10$: $1.1^{10}=2.5937$. Error falls roughly in proportion to $h$ ($0.468\to0.125$ for $h$ from $0.5\to0.1$). RK4, one step $h=1$: $k_1=1,\ k_2=1.5,\ k_3=1.75,\ k_4=2.75$, $x_1=1+\frac16(1+3+3.5+2.75)=2.70833$, within $0.01$ of $e$ with 4 evaluations. Straight path: $z=3,\ \epsilon=-1$, $u\equiv4$: $N=4$ Euler steps from $-1$ give $-1+4\cdot0.25\cdot4=3=z$ exactly. ✓

Remember for the exam
  • Euler: $x_{t+\Delta t}=x_t+\Delta t\,u_t(x_t)$; with $N$ steps $\Delta t=1/N$; each step uses the previous state, not the initial noise.
  • It is deterministic given $x_0$ and the field; randomness enters only through the initial $\mathcal N(0,I)$ draw. Not a one-step jump; the class quoted roughly 20–50 solver steps for flow matching versus 1,000–2,000 for DDPM (empirical, not universal).
  • RK4 = 4 evaluations per step, weights $\frac16(1,2,2,1)$, midpoint evaluated twice. EMA weights are used for sampling in the tutorial.
Common mistake

Restarting every step from $x_0$ (asked in class), or equating “fewer steps” with “fewer parameters”: the network size is comparable to diffusion; only the number of ODE steps drops.
