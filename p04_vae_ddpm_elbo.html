VAE, VQ-VAE and the DDPM ELBO

Every derivation from the 19 Sept and 3 Oct classes (plus the matching tutorials), written as numbered steps you can reproduce from memory: the VAE ELBO, the closed-form Gaussian KL, the reparameterisation trick, why prior samples are decodable, VQ-VAE quantisation with straight-through gradients, and then the whole DDPM ELBO as one long staged proof with a picture per stage.

One-paragraph summary

A VAE maximises $\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z))$; the KL has a closed form for Gaussians and the expectation is made differentiable by writing $z=\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon$. The KL term is also what makes $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ a legitimate input for the decoder. VQ-VAE swaps the Gaussian latent for a nearest-neighbour lookup in a learned codebook, using a straight-through copy of the gradient. A DDPM is a hierarchical VAE whose encoder is a fixed Markov chain; its ELBO is built by (i) chain-rule + Markov factorisations, (ii) keeping only the variables each term depends on, (iii) a three-variable Bayes rule that anchors every forward step on $X_0$, (iv) a telescoping cancellation, which together leave reconstruction $-$ prior matching $-$ sum of denoising-matching KLs.

1. The VAE ELBO and its gap

Statement

For any encoder $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ with full support, $\log p_\theta(x)=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))}_{\text{ELBO}}+D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p_\theta(z\mid x))\ \ge\ \text{ELBO}$. Source: recap at the start of the 19 Sept class (derived in the earlier VAE lectures; re-done in the 19 Sept and 3 Oct tutorials). Used later: identical template for the DDPM ELBO (stage 1 below), and the loss every VAE tutorial trains.

  1. Marginalise the joint: $$\log p_\theta(x)=\log\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$$ definition of a latent-variable model
  2. Multiply and divide by the encoder (where $q_\phi(z\mid x)>0$): $$\log p_\theta(x)=\log\int q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\,dz$$ multiply-and-divide trick
  3. Recognise an expectation: $$\log p_\theta(x)=\log\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]$$ definition of expectation
  4. Move the log inside: $$\log p_\theta(x)\ \ge\ \mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]=:\text{ELBO}$$ Jensen: $\log$ is concave, so $\log\mathbb E\ge\mathbb E\log$
  5. Factorise the joint: $$p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)$$ chain rule of probability; prior $p(z)$
  6. Split the log: $$\text{ELBO}=\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]+\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log\frac{p(z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]$$ $\log(ab/c)=\log a+\log(b/c)$
  7. The second term is a KL with the opposite sign: $$\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log\frac{p(z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]=-\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log\frac{q_\phi(z\mid x)}{p(z)}\right]=-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z))$$ definition of KL
  8. Hence $$\text{ELBO}=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]}_{\text{reconstruction}}-\underbrace{D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z))}_{\text{KL term}}$$ steps 6–7
  9. (step filled in — not written out in class) Size of the gap. Use the other factorisation $p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(z\mid x)\,p_\theta(x)$ in step 4: $$\text{ELBO}=\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x)+\log\frac{p_\theta(z\mid x)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]=\log p_\theta(x)-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x))$$ $\log p_\theta(x)$ does not depend on $z$, so it leaves the expectation
  10. Since $D_{KL}\ge0$, $\text{ELBO}\le\log p_\theta(x)$ with equality iff $q_\phi(z\mid x)=p_\theta(z\mid x)$. Maximising the ELBO therefore raises the likelihood and shrinks the approximation gap at the same time. Gibbs inequality
  11. (step filled in) From ELBO to loss. Training minimises $-\text{ELBO}$. With a Gaussian decoder of fixed variance, $\log p_\theta(x\mid z)=-\dfrac{\|x-\hat x_\theta(z)\|^2}{2\sigma_x^2}-\text{const}$, so the reconstruction loss is MSE up to scale; with Bernoulli pixels it is the binary cross-entropy $-\sum_j[x_j\log\hat x_j+(1-x_j)\log(1-\hat x_j)]$. plug in the likelihood the decoder is assumed to have

Remember for the exam
  • Write the ELBO as reconstruction $-$ KL$(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))$; the loss is its negative. The KL term is subtracted in the ELBO, added in the loss.
  • Professor's recap of training: encoder outputs $\mu_\phi(x),\sigma_\phi(x)$; sample $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$; $z=\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon$; decoder output is compared with $x$; $\theta$ and $\phi$ are updated by gradient descent.
  • The encoder is needed during training because it sits in two places: the KL and the expectation over $z$. At generation time only the decoder is used.
  • The reconstruction loss is dictated by the decoder's likelihood: MSE is not automatic (Bernoulli gives BCE).
Common mistake

Getting the Jensen direction wrong. $\log$ is concave, so $\log\mathbb E[\cdot]\ge\mathbb E[\log\cdot]$ — and that is exactly why the expectation of the log is a lower bound.

2. Closed-form KL: diagonal Gaussian vs standard normal

Statement

For $q=\mathcal N(\mu,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$ and $p=\mathcal N(0,I)$: $D_{KL}(q\|p)=\tfrac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2)$. Source: formula stated in the 19 Sept class ("closed form because both are Gaussian") and in the Sept 19 / Oct 3 tutorials; the derivation was not written out (step filled in). Used later: the KL term of every VAE loss; the same computation gives the DDPM consistency term.

  1. Both densities factorise over the $d_z$ latent coordinates (diagonal covariance, identity prior): $$\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_z}\log\frac{q_j(z_j)}{p_j(z_j)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad D_{KL}(q\|p)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_z}D_{KL}(q_j\|p_j)$$ independence of coordinates + linearity of expectation
  2. Work in one coordinate, $q_j=\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $p_j=\mathcal N(0,1)$: $$D_{KL}(q_j\|p_j)=\mathbb E_{z\sim q_j}\big[\log q_j(z)-\log p_j(z)\big]$$ definition of KL
  3. Write both log-densities: $$\log q_j(z)=-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2},\qquad \log p_j(z)=-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\frac{z^2}{2}$$ Gaussian density
  4. Subtract (the $\log 2\pi$ cancels): $$\log q_j-\log p_j=-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{z^2}{2}$$ algebra
  5. Take the expectation under $q_j$ using $\mathbb E[(z-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2$ and $\mathbb E[z^2]=\sigma^2+\mu^2$: $$D_{KL}(q_j\|p_j)=-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{\sigma^2+\mu^2}{2}$$ variance definition; $\mathbb E[z^2]=\operatorname{Var}+\text{mean}^2$
  6. Collect terms: $$D_{KL}(q_j\|p_j)=\tfrac12\big(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2\big)$$ $-\tfrac12=-\tfrac12\cdot1$
  7. Sum over coordinates: $$D_{KL}(q\|p)=\tfrac12\sum_{j}\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\big)$$ step 1
  8. Network form: the encoder outputs $\text{logvar}=\log\sigma^2$ (unconstrained, and $\sigma^2=e^{\text{logvar}}>0$ automatically): $$D_{KL}=-\tfrac12\sum_j\big(1+\text{logvar}_j-\mu_j^2-e^{\text{logvar}_j}\big)$$ substitute $\sigma_j^2=e^{\text{logvar}_j}$
  9. Non-negativity check: $f(s)=s-1-\log s$ has $f'(s)=1-1/s$, minimum at $s=1$ where $f=0$; and $\mu^2\ge0$. So $D_{KL}\ge0$ with equality iff $\mu=0,\sigma=1$. calculus

Remember for the exam
  • Both $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ and $p(z)$ are Gaussian, so the KL term is deterministic and differentiable in $\phi$ — no sampling needed for it (the professor's recap).
  • Memorise $\tfrac12\sum(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2)$ and the three-line derivation: expectation of log-ratio, $\mathbb E[(z-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2$, $\mathbb E[z^2]=\sigma^2+\mu^2$.
  • Diagonal covariance (not "diagonally dominant"): $d_z$ variances, not a $d_z\times d_z$ matrix.
Common mistake

Confusing $\sigma$ with $\sigma^2$ and $\log\sigma^2$: the formula uses the variance; with log-variance output, $\sigma=\exp(\tfrac12\,\text{logvar})$ (the $\tfrac12$ is needed to get the standard deviation).

3. Reparameterisation trick: why gradients flow

Statement

With $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and $z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon$: (a) $z\sim\mathcal N(\mu_\phi,\operatorname{diag}\sigma_\phi^2)$, and (b) $\nabla_\phi\,\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[f(z)]=\mathbb E_{\epsilon}[\nabla_\phi f(\mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\epsilon)]$, so the gradient can be taken through the sample. Source: 19 Sept class (training recap and Q&A "isn't there a subtlety in sampling from $\mu,\sigma$?") and the 19 Sept / 3 Oct tutorials. Used later: every VAE; the DDPM forward step $X_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}X_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\epsilon_t$ is the same device.

  1. (a) Law of $z$. For a scalar, $\mathbb E[\mu+\sigma\epsilon]=\mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)=\sigma^2\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)=\sigma^2$; an affine map of a Gaussian is Gaussian, so $z\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. Coordinatewise, $z\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\operatorname{diag}\sigma^2)$. shift-and-scale of a Gaussian
  2. Therefore any expectation over $z$ can be computed over $\epsilon$ instead: $$\mathbb E_{z\sim q_\phi(z\mid x)}[f(z)]=\mathbb E_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)}\big[f(\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon)\big]$$ change of variables (law of the unconscious statistician)
  3. The density of $\epsilon$, $\mathcal N(\epsilon;0,I)$, contains no $\phi$. All the dependence on $\phi$ now sits inside the integrand. this is the whole point
  4. (b) Differentiate: $$\nabla_\phi\,\mathbb E_{\epsilon}\big[f(\mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\epsilon)\big]=\mathbb E_{\epsilon}\big[\nabla_\phi f(\mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\epsilon)\big]$$ $\nabla$ and $\mathbb E$ commute because the sampling measure does not depend on $\phi$ (regularity conditions assumed)
  5. Apply the chain rule through the deterministic map $z=\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon$: $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu}=f'(z)\cdot1,\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial\sigma}=f'(z)\cdot\epsilon$$ chain rule; $\mu,\sigma$ are outputs of the encoder, so backprop continues into $\phi$
  6. Monte-Carlo estimate with a few draws $\epsilon^{(l)}$: $$\nabla_\phi\,\mathbb E_q[f]\approx\frac1L\sum_{l=1}^{L}\nabla_\phi f\big(\mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\epsilon^{(l)}\big)$$ sample mean of an unbiased integrand (in training, $L=1$ per data point)
  7. Contrast: without the trick the parameter is inside the density, $\nabla_\phi\int q_\phi(z\mid x)f(z)\,dz=\int\nabla_\phi q_\phi\,f\,dz$, which is not the expectation of a gradient of anything you can evaluate at a sampled $z$; and a sampled $z$ itself has no derivative with respect to $\phi$. why the lecture says the expectation "cannot be directly differentiated"
▪
Worked numeric check

Take $\mu=1$, $\sigma=0.5$, $f(z)=(z-2)^2$, one draw $\epsilon=0.8$. Then $z=1+0.5\cdot0.8=1.4$, $f'(z)=2(1.4-2)=-1.2$, so $\partial f/\partial\mu=-1.2$ and $\partial f/\partial\sigma=-1.2\cdot0.8=-0.96$.

Unbiasedness: $\mathbb E_\epsilon[2(\mu+\sigma\epsilon-2)]=2(\mu-2)=-2$ and $\mathbb E_\epsilon[2(\mu+\sigma\epsilon-2)\epsilon]=2\sigma=1$. These match the exact derivatives of $\mathbb E[(z-2)^2]=(\mu-2)^2+\sigma^2$, namely $2(\mu-2)=-2$ and $2\sigma=1$. A single draw $(-1.2,-0.96)$ is a noisy but unbiased estimate of $(-2,\,1)$.

Remember for the exam
  • $\epsilon$ is parameter-free; the transformation from $(\mu,\sigma)$ to $z$ is differentiable. "Randomness is isolated in $\epsilon$."
  • Implementation: $\sigma=\exp(0.5\,\text{logvar})$, $\epsilon=\texttt{randn\_like}(\sigma)$, $z=\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon$.
  • The professor's point: the trick is what lets the expectation over $Z$ in the ELBO be differentiated during training, which is why the encoder outputs $\mu,\sigma$ rather than the model using raw draws.
Common mistake

Saying "$z$ is random, so it has no gradient". The sample is random but, given $\epsilon$, it is a deterministic differentiable function of $\mu,\sigma$; the gradient flows along that function and treats $\epsilon$ as a constant input.

4. Why sampling from the prior works (role of the KL term)

Statement

The decoder was trained on $z\sim q_\phi(z\mid x)$, yet generation feeds it $z\sim p(z)=\mathcal N(0,I)$. This is justified because maximising the ELBO minimises the KL term, driving $q_\phi(z\mid x)\to p(z)$. Source: 19 Sept class, "Why sampling from the prior works: the role of the KL term". The aggregate-posterior identity in steps 4–7 is a step filled in — not written out in class. Used later: the generation recipe of every latent-variable model (VAE, DDPM, flows): sample the prior, then decode.

  1. The objective is maximised and the KL enters with a minus sign: $$\text{ELBO}=\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))$$ proof 1
  2. So maximising the ELBO minimises $D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))$ (against the reconstruction term, which pulls the other way). sign of the term
  3. If the KL reaches $0$ then $q_\phi(z\mid x)=p(z)$ for that $x$. $D_{KL}=0$ iff the distributions are equal Hence, for a well-trained VAE, $q_\phi(Z\mid X)\approx\mathcal N(0,I)$ for essentially every $X$, so drawing $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ is (approximately) drawing from $q_\phi(Z\mid X)$ — the input distribution the decoder was fit to. This is the argument given in class.
  4. (step filled in) Averaged version. Define the aggregate posterior $q(z)=\mathbb E_{x\sim\text{data}}\,q_\phi(z\mid x)$ — the distribution of latent codes the decoder actually sees in training. Average the KL over the data: $$\mathbb E_x\,D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))=\mathbb E_x\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log\frac{q_\phi(z\mid x)}{p(z)}\right]$$ definition
  5. Insert $q(z)$ by multiply-and-divide inside the log: $$=\mathbb E_x\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log\frac{q_\phi(z\mid x)}{q(z)}+\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right]$$ $\log\frac ab=\log\frac ac+\log\frac cb$
  6. For the second piece, $\mathbb E_x\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[g(z)]=\mathbb E_{q(z)}[g(z)]$, so it equals $D_{KL}(q(z)\|p(z))$; the first piece is the mutual information $I_q(X;Z)$. Hence $$\mathbb E_x\,D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))=I_q(X;Z)+D_{KL}(q(z)\|p(z))$$ definition of aggregate posterior and of mutual information
  7. Both pieces are $\ge0$, so $D_{KL}(q(z)\|p(z))\le\mathbb E_x D_{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))$: a small KL term guarantees that the codes seen in training are distributed like the prior. non-negativity of KL and of mutual information

Worked numeric check

One latent dimension, prior $\mathcal N(0,1)$ (density at $0$: $0.3989$), two data clusters with equally likely posteriors $\mathcal N(\pm m,s^2)$.

Weak KL: $m=2$, $s^2=0.25$. KL per posterior $=\tfrac12(4+0.25-1-\ln0.25)=2.318$. Aggregate density at $z=0$: $\mathcal N(0;2,0.25)=e^{-8}/\sqrt{2\pi\cdot0.25}=0.000268$ — about $1/1490$ of the prior density, so a prior sample at $0$ falls in a hole the decoder never saw.

Strong KL: $m=0.5$, $s^2=0.5625$. KL per posterior $=\tfrac12(0.25+0.5625-1-\ln0.5625)=0.194$. Aggregate density at $0$: $\mathcal N(0;0.5,0.5625)=0.426$, ratio $1.07$ to the prior. A cheap KL buys good coverage.

Remember for the exam
  • Generation recipe for any latent-variable model $P_\theta(X)=\int P_\theta(X,Z)dZ$: (1) $z_{\text{new}}\sim p(Z)$, (2) $x\sim p_\theta(X\mid Z=z_{\text{new}})$. For a VAE, option A: use the decoder output $\hat x_\theta(z)$ (the mean); option B: sample from the Gaussian centred on it.
  • Rule stated explicitly: encoder for embedding extraction, decoder for generation. Embeddings: option A use $\mu_{\phi^*}(x_{\text{test}})$ (one fixed embedding); option B sample from $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma)$ (no fixed embedding).
  • Why is the encoder still needed if generation only uses $\mathcal N(0,1)$? Because it is in the KL and the reconstruction expectation during training.
Common mistake

Reading "KL $\to0$" literally for every $x$. Exact zero means $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ ignores $x$ (posterior collapse, mutual information $0$) and the decoder cannot reconstruct. The trained VAE balances the two terms; the prior-sampling argument is approximate.

5. VQ-VAE: nearest-neighbour quantisation, straight-through gradient, codebook and commitment loss

Statement

With deterministic encoder/decoder and a codebook $\{z_1,\dots,z_M\}$: $z_q(x)=z_{j^*}$ with $j^*=\arg\min_j\|z_e(x)-z_j\|$; the gradient of the reconstruction loss is passed from $z_q$ to $z_e$ unchanged (straight-through); the codebook is learned by gradient descent on the latent-matching loss, and the full loss is reconstruction + codebook + commitment. Source: 19 Sept class (quantisation, two objectives, dictionary learning). The explicit straight-through trick and stop-gradient split of the latent-matching loss (with $\beta$) are from the VQ-VAE paper, a step filled in — not written out in class. Used later: pretrained VQ-VAE decoders sit under modern generators; "what is generated is a latent, then decoded".

  1. Encode, then quantise to the closest codeword: $$z_e=E_\phi(x_i),\qquad j^*=\arg\min_{j\in\{1,\dots,M\}}\|z_e-z_j\|,\qquad z_q=z_{j^*}$$ definition of vector quantisation (nearest neighbour)
  2. Decode the quantised vector: $$\hat x=D_\theta(z_q)$$ the decoder input is $z_q$, not $z_e$
  3. (step filled in) Why plain backprop fails. $z_q(z_e)$ is piecewise constant: it equals one fixed codeword on each Voronoi cell, so $\partial z_q/\partial z_e=0$ almost everywhere (and is undefined on cell boundaries). Hence $\nabla_\phi\|x-\hat x\|^2=0$ and the encoder would get no reconstruction signal. derivative of a piecewise-constant function
  4. (step filled in) Straight-through estimator. Define $z_q^{\text{ST}}=z_e+\operatorname{sg}[z_q-z_e]$, where $\operatorname{sg}$ (stop-gradient) is the identity in the forward pass and has zero derivative in the backward pass. Forward value: $z_e+(z_q-z_e)=z_q$, so the decoder is unchanged. Backward: $$\frac{\partial z_q^{\text{ST}}}{\partial z_e}=I\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\partial L_{\text{rec}}}{\partial z_e}:=\frac{\partial L_{\text{rec}}}{\partial z_q}$$ identity added to a constant: gradient copied across the quantiser
  5. The decoder, which receives $z_q$ as an ordinary input, is trained by the usual gradient of the reconstruction loss $\|x_i-\hat x\|^2$. same as a VAE reconstruction objective
  6. Second objective, latent matching (the lecture's term): $\|z_e(x_i)-z_q(x_i)\|^2$. It is the only place the dictionary appears once the reconstruction path is cut by step 3–4. For $j=j^*$: $$\frac{\partial}{\partial z_j}\|z_e-z_j\|^2=2(z_j-z_e),\qquad\text{and }0\text{ for }j\neq j^*$$ gradient of a squared distance; $z_j$ are learned parameters, random at initialisation
  7. Gradient descent with step $\eta$ on that loss moves the winning codeword toward the encoder output that selected it: $$z_{j^*}\leftarrow z_{j^*}-\eta\cdot2(z_{j^*}-z_e)=(1-2\eta)\,z_{j^*}+2\eta\,z_e$$ dictionary learned jointly with $\phi,\theta$
  8. (step filled in) The same squared distance also pulls $z_e$ toward the codeword; to control the two effects separately the paper splits it with stop-gradients and weights the encoder side by $\beta$: $$L=\underbrace{\|x-\hat x\|^2}_{\text{reconstruction}}+\underbrace{\|\operatorname{sg}[z_e]-z_q\|^2}_{\text{codebook loss (updates }z_j)}+\underbrace{\beta\,\|z_e-\operatorname{sg}[z_q]\|^2}_{\text{commitment loss (updates }\phi)}$$ $\operatorname{sg}$ routes each term's gradient to one side only; $\beta\approx0.25$ in the paper

Worked numeric check

$M=3$ codewords $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$, encoder output $z_e=(0.8,0.3)$. Distances: $\sqrt{0.64+0.09}=0.854$, $\sqrt{0.04+0.09}=0.361$, $\sqrt{0.64+0.49}=1.063$, so $j^*=2$, $z_q=(1,0)$.

Straight-through: if the decoder reports $\partial L_{\text{rec}}/\partial z_q=(-0.2,\,0.4)$, the encoder receives $\partial L_{\text{rec}}/\partial z_e=(-0.2,\,0.4)$ (the true derivative through $\arg\min$ would be $0$).

Remember for the exam
  • Two changes versus a VAE: deterministic encoder/decoder (no $\mu,\sigma$, no reparameterisation) and a discrete latent from an $M$-vector dictionary. The decoder input $z_q$ differs from the encoder output $z_e$ by the quantisation step.
  • Two objectives: reconstruction, and latent matching between $z_e$ and $z_q$ — "very similar to the VAE objective: reconstruction plus a regulariser on $Z$".
  • Dictionary vectors are learned parameters (random init, gradient descent jointly with $\phi,\theta$), not fixed constants.
  • Why VQ-VAE beats a plain VAE (professor): discretisation, and no Gaussian/reparameterisation restriction on the latent. Generation = produce a latent, then run the pretrained VQ-VAE decoder.
Common mistake

Claiming the gradient flows through the $\arg\min$. It does not; the encoder trains through the straight-through copy (reconstruction) and through the commitment term.

6. DDPM forward step is Gaussian, Markov, and keeps N(0,I) fixed

Statement

From $X_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}X_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t$, $\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$: (a) $q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})=\mathcal N(X_t;\sqrt{\alpha_t}X_{t-1},(1-\alpha_t)I)$ with no learnable parameter; (b) $X_t\perp\{X_0,\dots,X_{t-2}\}\mid X_{t-1}$; (c) if $X_{t-1}\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ then $X_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. Source: 19 Sept class ("The Forward Process Is a First-Order Markov Chain", "Deriving $q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})$"), recap on 3 Oct. Part (c) is a step filled in — the class argued stationarity through the unrolled mean (a student's observation) but did not write this one-line check. Used later: the Markov factorisation of $q$ in the next proof. (The closed-form marginal $q(X_t\mid X_0)$ and the posterior are on the next page of this site.)

  1. Fix $X_{t-1}$ (condition on it). Then $\sqrt{\alpha_t}X_{t-1}$ is a constant vector $c$ and $X_t=c+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_t$, with $\epsilon_t$ independent of $X_{t-1}$. $\epsilon_t$ is freshly sampled
  2. Mean and covariance: $$\mathbb E[X_t\mid X_{t-1}]=\sqrt{\alpha_t}X_{t-1},\qquad\operatorname{Cov}[X_t\mid X_{t-1}]=(1-\alpha_t)\,\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_t)=(1-\alpha_t)I$$ $\operatorname{Var}(a\epsilon)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)$ with $a=\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}$
  3. A constant plus a scaled Gaussian is Gaussian: $$q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})=\mathcal N\big(X_t;\ \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,X_{t-1},\ (1-\alpha_t)I\big)$$ this is a reparameterisation (proof 3) read backwards; (a) done. $\alpha_t\in(0,1)$ are schedule constants, so no $\phi$ appears
  4. (b) Markov property. The only inputs to $X_t$ are $X_{t-1}$ and $\epsilon_t$, and $\epsilon_t$ is independent of $X_0,\dots,X_{t-1}$. So $$p(X_t\mid X_{t-1},X_{t-2},\dots,X_0)=p(X_t\mid X_{t-1})$$ first-order Markov chain by construction
  5. (c) Fixed point (step filled in). Suppose $X_{t-1}\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, independent of $\epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. A sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian with mean $0$ and covariance $$\alpha_t I+(1-\alpha_t)I=I$$ variances add for independent terms
  6. So $\mathcal N(0,I)$ is preserved by every step: this is why the $\sqrt{\alpha_t}$ scaling is there. Adding noise without shrinking the signal would give variance $1+(1-\alpha_t)>1$ and the chain would blow up instead of settling. With scaling, the mean of $X_T$ carries the factor $\prod_t\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,X_0\to0$ (each $\alpha_t<1$) and the variance tends to $1$, so $X_T\approx\mathcal N(0,I)$ regardless of $X_0$. the professor's answer to "why scale at all?"

  • DDPM = hierarchical VAE with three constraints: $T$ latents, every latent has the dimensionality of the data, and a fixed (non-learned) encoding process. Only the decoder has parameters $\theta$.

  • Every equation is written per data point; batching only matters in implementation.

Reading $q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})$ as having learned parameters ("$q_\phi$"). The encoding distribution carries no $\phi$ subscript; in the ELBO only $p_\theta$ is learned.

7. The two joint factorisations

Statement

$q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})$ (derived: chain rule + Markov) and $p_\theta(X_{0:T})=p(X_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)$ (defined: the model), with Gaussian reverse conditionals $\mathcal N(X_{t-1};\mu_\theta(X_t),\Sigma_\theta(X_t))$. Source: 19 Sept class ("Chain Rule Expansion of the Joint Encoding Distribution", "Defining the Model: the Reverse Process"); restated in the 3 Oct recap and 3 Oct tutorial. Used later: numerator and denominator of the DDPM ELBO.

  1. Order the latents $X_1,\dots,X_T$ and apply the chain rule of probability, conditioning everything on $X_0$: $$q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(X_t\mid X_{t-1},X_{t-2},\dots,X_1,X_0)$$ chain rule of probability
  2. Written out for $T=3$: $$q(X_1\mid X_0)\;q(X_2\mid X_1,X_0)\;q(X_3\mid X_2,X_1,X_0)$$ same chain rule, explicit
  3. Each conditional depends only on the immediately preceding variable (proof 6b): $$q(X_t\mid X_{t-1},\dots,X_0)=q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})$$ first-order Markov property of the forward process
  4. Substitute (for $T=3$: $q(X_1\mid X_0)\,q(X_2\mid X_1)\,q(X_3\mid X_2)$): $$q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})$$ steps 1 and 3
  5. Model joint (a definition, not a derivation). The decoder is chosen as the mirror-image chain started from the prior: $$p_\theta(X_{0:T})=p(X_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t),\qquad p(X_T)=\mathcal N(0,I)$$ modelling choice: reverse chain is also first-order Markov
  6. Each reverse step is a Gaussian with learned mean and variance, one per step $t$ (in practice produced by one network conditioned on $t$): $$p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)=\mathcal N\big(X_{t-1};\ \mu_\theta(X_t,t),\ \Sigma_\theta(X_t,t)\big)$$ the only learnable objects in the whole model
  7. (step filled in) Sanity check that $p_\theta(X_{0:T})$ is a normalised density: integrate out $X_0$ first, $\int p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)dX_0=1$; then $X_1$, $\int p_\theta(X_1\mid X_2)dX_1=1$; and so on down to $\int p(X_T)dX_T=1$. each conditional integrates to 1 over its left variable

  • First-order Markov: $X_t\perp\{X_0,\dots,X_{t-2}\}\mid X_{t-1}$.

Using the same Markov argument to shorten $q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)$. The forward chain gives independence in the forward direction only; see proof 10.

8. DDPM ELBO, stage 1: Jensen and expanding the logs

Statement

$\log p_\theta(X_0)\ge J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}\Big[\log p(X_T)+\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)-\log q(X_1\mid X_0)+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})\Big]$. Source: 19 Sept class, "Starting the ELBO Construction for DDPM"; 3 Oct class, "Constructing the DDPM ELBO". Proof 1 with $x\to X_0$, $z\to X_{1:T}$. Used later: start of the five-stage proof; stages 2–5 below finish it.

  1. Marginalise out the latents: $$\log p_\theta(X_0)=\log\int p_\theta(X_{0:T})\,dX_{1:T}$$ latent-variable model, $Z=X_{1:T}$
  2. Multiply and divide by the (fixed) forward joint: $$=\log\int q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)\frac{p_\theta(X_{0:T})}{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}\,dX_{1:T}=\log\mathbb E_{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(X_{0:T})}{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}\right]$$ multiply-and-divide; definition of expectation
  3. $$\log p_\theta(X_0)\ \ge\ J(\theta):=\mathbb E_{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(X_{0:T})}{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}\right]$$ Jensen (note: no $\phi$ anywhere, since the encoder is fixed)
  4. Insert both factorisations (proof 7): $$J(\theta)=\mathbb E_q\!\left[\log\frac{p(X_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})}\right]$$ numerator = reverse chain, denominator = forward chain
  5. Logs turn products into sums: $$J(\theta)=\mathbb E_q\Big[\log p(X_T)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})\Big]$$ $\log\frac{ab}{c}=\log a+\log b-\log c$
  6. Peel the $t=1$ term off each sum: $$J(\theta)=\mathbb E_q\Big[\log p(X_T)+\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)-\log q(X_1\mid X_0)\Big]$$ $$\qquad+\ \mathbb E_q\Big[\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})\Big]$$ splitting a sum; these are exactly the five groups of terms in the statement

Remember for the exam
  • Notation flags the professor gave: the encoder has no $\phi$, the decoder keeps $\theta$ — all learning is in the decoding process.
  • The professor previewed the destination: after "a lot of algebra" the loss becomes "a simple regression problem" (that last reduction is the next page).
  • Same ELBO template as the VAE with $Z\to X_{1:T}$, $X\to X_0$.

Dropping the $t=1$ term of the forward denominator or the $t=1$ term of the reverse product when splitting sums. Both are needed: the first becomes the reconstruction term, the second cancels in stage 4.

9. DDPM ELBO, stage 2: which variables survive each expectation

Statement

If $g$ depends only on a subset of the variables, an expectation over the full joint equals an expectation over that subset's marginal: $\mathbb E_{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}[g]=\mathbb E_{q(X_a,X_b\mid X_0)}[g(X_a,X_b)]$; and for pairs, $\mathbb E_{q(X_{t-1},X_t\mid X_0)}[f]=\mathbb E_{q(X_t\mid X_0)}\big[\mathbb E_{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}[f]\big]$. Source: 19 Sept class "Simplifying the ELBO: Which Variables Survive Each Expectation" (the nesting identity is Homework 11); 3 Oct class "Expectation Reduction"; 3 Oct tutorial "Why nested expectations are required". Used later: stage 5.

  1. Let $g$ depend only on $X_a,X_b$ (two of the latents). Write the expectation as an integral over all latents: $$\mathbb E_{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}[g]=\int q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)\,g(X_a,X_b)\,dX_{1:T}$$ definition of expectation
  2. Separate the variables the integrand does not use: $$=\int g(X_a,X_b)\Big[\int q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)\,dX_{\text{others}}\Big]dX_a\,dX_b$$ Fubini; $g$ is constant in $X_{\text{others}}$
  3. The bracket is the marginal of $(X_a,X_b)$ given $X_0$: $$\int q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)\,dX_{\text{others}}=q(X_a,X_b\mid X_0)$$ definition of a marginal (integrating other variables out)
  4. Therefore $\mathbb E_{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}[g]=\mathbb E_{q(X_a,X_b\mid X_0)}[g]$. Apply it to the three groups of stage 1: $$\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)\ \to\ q(X_1\mid X_0),\qquad\log p(X_T)\ \to\ q(X_T\mid X_0)$$ $$\text{terms in }(X_{t-1},X_t)\ \to\ q(X_{t-1},X_t\mid X_0)$$ each term's argument list
  5. Nesting identity. Product rule for the pair: $$q(X_{t-1},X_t\mid X_0)=q(X_t\mid X_0)\;q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)$$ product rule of probability (conditioned on $X_0$)
  6. Insert into the pair expectation of any $f(X_{t-1},X_t)$: $$\mathbb E_{q(X_{t-1},X_t\mid X_0)}[f]=\int q(X_t\mid X_0)\Big[\int q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\,f\,dX_{t-1}\Big]dX_t$$ $$=\mathbb E_{q(X_t\mid X_0)}\Big[\mathbb E_{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}[f]\Big]$$ tower property / law of iterated expectation (Homework 11)
  7. The inner expectation is over exactly the distribution that will sit in the numerator of the log-ratio — this is what lets us recognise a KL in stage 5. Without the nesting, both $X_{t-1}$ and $X_t$ would be random with no KL in sight. motivation for the nested form

10. DDPM ELBO, stage 3: the three-variable Bayes-rule trick

Statement

For $t\ge2$: $q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})=q(X_t\mid X_{t-1},X_0)=\dfrac{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\,q(X_t\mid X_0)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_0)}$. Source: 19 Sept class, "The Three-Variable Bayes-Rule Trick: Anchoring the Forward Process on $X_0$" ("the most important trick; everything else is just algebra"); 3 Oct class "Bayes-Rule Reparameterization" (same derivation, equivalent form). Used later: produces the known denoising distribution $q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)$ that appears in the last term of the loss.

  1. Insert $X_0$ for free. By the Markov property of the forward chain ($t\ge2$, so $X_0$ is a genuine earlier variable): $$q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})=q(X_t\mid X_{t-1},X_0)$$ $X_t\perp X_0\mid X_{t-1}$ (proof 6b)
  2. Write the joint of $(X_t,X_{t-1})$ given $X_0$ in one order: $$q(X_t,X_{t-1}\mid X_0)=q(X_t\mid X_{t-1},X_0)\;q(X_{t-1}\mid X_0)$$ product rule
  3. And in the other order: $$q(X_t,X_{t-1}\mid X_0)=q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\;q(X_t\mid X_0)$$ product rule, variables swapped
  4. Equate steps 2 and 3 and divide by $q(X_{t-1}\mid X_0)>0$: $$q(X_t\mid X_{t-1},X_0)=\frac{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\,q(X_t\mid X_0)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_0)}$$ Bayes' rule $P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}$ with $X_0$ as a common conditioning variable (the "three-variable" version)
  5. Combine with step 1: $$q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})=\frac{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\,q(X_t\mid X_0)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_0)}$$ steps 1 and 4
  6. Equivalent form used by the 3 Oct class and tutorial: $$q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)=\frac{q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})\,q(X_{t-1}\mid X_0)}{q(X_t\mid X_0)}$$ rearranging step 5
  7. Why $X_0$ cannot be dropped from the posterior. The Markov property holds only forward: $X_t\perp X_0\mid X_{t-1}$. It does not imply $X_{t-1}\perp X_0\mid X_t$: many different images can end in the same noisy $X_t$, and knowing which $X_0$ was used still changes which $X_{t-1}$ was visited. class Q&A: why $q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\neq q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)$
▪
Worked numeric check

With $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s=(0.9,\,0.72,\,0.504)$ the marginals are $q(X_t\mid X_0)=\mathcal N(X_t;\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}X_0,1-\bar\alpha_t)$ (closed forms from the next page): $q(X_1|X_0)=0.9260$, $q(X_2|X_0)=0.6069$, $q(X_3|X_0)=0.4358$. Remember for the exam
  • Two moves in order: (1) insert $X_0$ (free by Markov); (2) Bayes' rule with three variables. Say why: it anchors the process on the real data point, because the goal is to reverse the chain and land on an actual image.
  • Name each piece: $q(X_t\mid X_0)$ = "how noisy is $X_t$ starting from $X_0$"; $q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)$ = the new known denoising distribution, computable in closed form from the fixed forward process; it is Gaussian.
  • Many noise paths lead from $X_0$ to the same $X_t$; conditioning on $X_0$ resolves which $X_{t-1}$ is likely.
Common mistake

Simplifying $q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)$ to $q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)$ "by Markov". The Markov chain removes $X_0$ from $q(X_t\mid X_{t-1},X_0)$, not from the reversed conditional.

11. DDPM ELBO, stage 4: telescoping cancellation

Statement

$\prod_{t=2}^{T}\dfrac{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_0)}{q(X_t\mid X_0)}=\dfrac{q(X_1\mid X_0)}{q(X_T\mid X_0)}$; substituting stage 3 into the stage 1 integrand therefore gives $\log\dfrac{p(X_T)\,p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T}p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{q(X_T\mid X_0)\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}$ — the forward conditionals are gone and the $q(X_1\mid X_0)$ from the first link cancels. Source: 19 Sept class "The Telescoping Cancellation and the Final Three-Term Loss"; 3 Oct class "Telescoping Cancellation". Used later: stage 5.

  1. Start from the integrand of stage 1, written as one ratio with the $t=1$ links separated: $$\frac{p(X_T)\;p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T}p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{q(X_1\mid X_0)\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})}$$ stage 1, step 6, products instead of sums
  2. Replace every forward conditional in the denominator using stage 3: $$\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(X_t\mid X_{t-1})=\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\cdot\prod_{t=2}^{T}\frac{q(X_t\mid X_0)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_0)}$$ three-variable Bayes rule applied to each $t$, then split the product
  3. Expand the last product, writing $q_t:=q(X_t\mid X_0)$: $$\prod_{t=2}^{T}\frac{q_t}{q_{t-1}}=\frac{q_2}{q_1}\cdot\frac{q_3}{q_2}\cdots\frac{q_{T-1}}{q_{T-2}}\cdot\frac{q_T}{q_{T-1}}$$ writing out $t=2,3,\dots,T$
  4. Every intermediate $q_2,\dots,q_{T-1}$ appears once above and once below the fraction bar and cancels: $$\prod_{t=2}^{T}\frac{q_t}{q_{t-1}}=\frac{q_T}{q_1}$$ telescoping product; (the lecture writes it inverted, $\prod\frac{q_{t-1}}{q_t}=\frac{q_1}{q_T}$, when it sits in the numerator)
  5. Substitute back into the denominator of step 1: $$q(X_1\mid X_0)\cdot\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\cdot\frac{q(X_T\mid X_0)}{q(X_1\mid X_0)}=q(X_T\mid X_0)\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)$$ the first-link $q(X_1\mid X_0)$ cancels the surviving $q_1$ in the denominator of the telescoped ratio
  6. Hence the integrand is $$\frac{p(X_T)\;p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T}p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{q(X_T\mid X_0)\;\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}$$ step 5 in the denominator of step 1
  7. Take logs and regroup into three kinds of pieces: $$\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)\;+\;\log\frac{p(X_T)}{q(X_T\mid X_0)}\;+\;\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}$$ $\log$ of a product is a sum; pair numerator with denominator terms

12. DDPM ELBO, stage 5: assembling the three-term loss

Statement

$J(\theta)=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q(X_1\mid X_0)}[\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)]}_{\text{(A) reconstruction}}-\underbrace{D_{KL}(q(X_T\mid X_0)\|p(X_T))}_{\text{(B) prior matching}}-\underbrace{\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb E_{q(X_t\mid X_0)}\big[D_{KL}(q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\|p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t))\big]}_{\text{(C) denoising matching}}$. Source: 19 Sept class "Step 5: The final DDPM ELBO" and "Interpreting the Three Terms: Known vs. Learned Denoising"; 3 Oct class "Expectation Reduction and ELBO Decomposition", "Interpretation of the Three ELBO Terms". Used later: next page reduces (C) to a regression on the noise; training and sampling algorithms.

  1. Take $\mathbb E_{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}$ of the three-part integrand of stage 4, using linearity: $$J(\theta)=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)]+\mathbb E_q\!\left[\log\frac{p(X_T)}{q(X_T\mid X_0)}\right]+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb E_q\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}\right]$$ linearity of expectation
  2. (A) The first expectation involves only $X_1$: $$\mathbb E_{q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)}[\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)]=\mathbb E_{q(X_1\mid X_0)}[\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)]$$ survival of variables (stage 2)
  3. (B) The second involves only $X_T$ and is minus a KL: $$\mathbb E_{q(X_T\mid X_0)}\!\left[\log\frac{p(X_T)}{q(X_T\mid X_0)}\right]=-\mathbb E_{q(X_T\mid X_0)}\!\left[\log\frac{q(X_T\mid X_0)}{p(X_T)}\right]=-D_{KL}\big(q(X_T\mid X_0)\,\|\,p(X_T)\big)$$ survival; definition of KL
  4. (C) Each remaining term involves the pair $(X_{t-1},X_t)$; nest the expectation: $$\mathbb E_{q(X_{t-1},X_t\mid X_0)}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}\right]=\mathbb E_{q(X_t\mid X_0)}\Big[\mathbb E_{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}\Big]\Big]$$ tower property (stage 2, step 6)
  5. The inner expectation is taken under the very distribution in the denominator, with the ratio flipped, so it is minus a KL: $$\mathbb E_{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}\!\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)}{q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)}\right]=-D_{KL}\big(q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)\big)$$ definition $D_{KL}(q\|p)=\mathbb E_q[\log\frac qp]$ and $\log\frac pq=-\log\frac qp$
  6. Collect (A), (B), (C): $$J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{q(X_1\mid X_0)}[\log p_\theta(X_0\mid X_1)]-D_{KL}\big(q(X_T\mid X_0)\|p(X_T)\big)-\sum_{t=2}^{T}\mathbb E_{q(X_t\mid X_0)}\Big[D_{KL}\big(q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)\big)\Big]$$ steps 1–5
  7. $\theta$ appears in (A) and (C) only. (B) contains the fixed forward process and the fixed prior $p(X_T)=\mathcal N(0,I)$, so it is a constant offset and is dropped from the optimisation. Maximising $J$ is equivalent to maximising (A) while minimising every KL in (C). no $\theta$ means no gradient

Worked numeric check

One consistency term at $t=2$ in the running example. Known denoising (from proofs 10–11): $q(X_1\mid X_2,X_0)=\mathcal N(0.8374,\,0.0714)$. Suppose the model's step is $p_\theta(X_1\mid X_2)=\mathcal N(0.7,\,0.0714)$ (same variance, mean off by $0.1374$).

For equal variances $\sigma^2$: $D_{KL}(\mathcal N(\mu_1,\sigma^2)\|\mathcal N(\mu_2,\sigma^2))=\mathbb E[(z-\mu_2)^2-(z-\mu_1)^2]/(2\sigma^2)=(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ (the $z$-dependent cross terms combine to $(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2$ in expectation).

$D_{KL}=\dfrac{0.1374^2}{2\times0.0714}=\dfrac{0.01887}{0.1429}=0.132$ nats. A model that matches the known mean exactly gives $0$; the term is zero only if learned and known denoising agree.

Remember for the exam
  • Three terms: (A) reconstruction, structurally identical to a VAE's with $X_0$ as data and $X_1$ as the first latent; (B) prior matching, the analogue of the VAE's single KL, independent of $\theta$; (C) denoising matching ("consistency"), "the most important term", sum from $t=2$ to $T$.
  • Known vs learned: $q(X_{t-1}\mid X_t,X_0)$ is the known denoiser (closed form because the forward process is fixed); $p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)$ is the learned one and has no access to $X_0$. Matching them at every $t$ means chaining $p_\theta$ from pure noise reproduces the reverse of the forward process without a real $X_0$ — that is what makes it generative.
  • Professor's summary: learn the denoising process "by matching the denoising process to the known denoising that we have enforced through the encoding".
  • Where it stopped: reducing (C) to a simple regression on $\epsilon$ was explicitly left for the next class (see the next page).
  • The professor urged: "work all the math" of this derivation yourself.
Common mistake

(i) Starting the sum at $t=1$ (the $t=1$ link is term A); (ii) writing the outer expectation of (C) over $q(X_{1:T}\mid X_0)$ or forgetting that it is over $q(X_t\mid X_0)$; (iii) putting $X_0$ inside $p_\theta(X_{t-1}\mid X_t)$ — the model cannot see the clean image at generation time; (iv) keeping (B) in the gradient (it is $\theta$-free).
