Lecture proofs · Oct 24 and Nov 07

Policy gradient, baselines, importance sampling, PPO

Every derivation of the RL block of the course, in the professor's route and notation: trajectory probability, the policy-gradient theorem (staged, with a picture per stage), reward-to-go, baselines, value/Q/advantage, the LLM as a policy, importance sampling, the off-policy surrogate with TRPO, and the PPO clipped objective. Each proof has a figure, numbered steps with the reason for each line, and a worked numeric check.

Notation (as used in class)

State $s_t$, action $a_t$, reward $r_t=r(s_t,a_t)$ (a scalar), transition kernel $P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$, initial distribution $\rho_0(s_0)$, discount $\gamma\in[0,1]$, policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$. Trajectory $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots)$, return $R(\tau)=\sum_t\gamma^t r_t$, return-to-go $G_t=\sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^{k-t}r_k$, objective $J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[R(\tau)]$. All derivatives are with respect to $\theta$, and $\nabla\equiv\nabla_\theta$. Sums over actions become integrals for continuous actions.

1. Probability of a trajectory

Statement

For a trajectory $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,\dots,s_T)$ under policy $\pi_\theta$ in an MDP, $p_\theta(\tau)=\rho_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$. Source: Oct 24 (section 14) and Nov 07 (section 6). Used in the policy-gradient theorem (section 2 of this page), where exactly the factors that do not contain $\theta$ vanish, and in the importance-sampling ratio of trajectories (section 7).

  1. Chain rule of probability on the ordered variables: $p_\theta(\tau)=p(s_0)\,p(a_0\mid s_0)\,p(s_1\mid s_0,a_0)\,p(a_1\mid s_0,a_0,s_1)\cdots$ $$p_\theta(\tau)=p(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}p(a_t\mid s_0,a_0,\dots,s_t)\;p(s_{t+1}\mid s_0,a_0,\dots,s_t,a_t)$$chain rule
  2. The agent only looks at the current state, so the action distribution is the policy: $$p(a_t\mid s_0,a_0,\dots,s_t)=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$$policy depends on $s_t$ only
  3. The environment is Markov: the next state depends only on the current state and action: $$p(s_{t+1}\mid s_0,a_0,\dots,s_t,a_t)=P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$$Markov property
  4. The first state is drawn from the initial distribution, $p(s_0)=\rho_0(s_0)$. Substituting steps 2 and 3 into step 1: $$p_\theta(\tau)=\rho_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$$substitute

Two steps, $\rho_0(s_0)=0.5$, $\pi_\theta(a_0\mid s_0)=0.6$, $P(s_1\mid s_0,a_0)=0.9$, $\pi_\theta(a_1\mid s_1)=0.7$, $P(s_2\mid s_1,a_1)=0.8$. Remember for the exam
  • The MDP is the tuple $(\mathcal S,\mathcal A,P,r,\gamma,\rho_0)$; the policy is outside it (the MDP fixes the problem; the learned policy changes).
  • $\rho_0$ is not $P$: $\rho_0$ gives the first state, $P$ governs all later transitions.
  • The professor wrote the product over time; for finite horizons it runs to $T-1$.

2. The policy-gradient theorem (log-derivative trick), in four stages

Statement

$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[\big(\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\big)R(\tau)\big]$: the gradient of the expected return can be estimated from sampled trajectories, and neither $P$ nor $\rho_0$ needs to be known or differentiated. Source: Oct 24 (section 16) and Nov 07 (section 8). Used by REINFORCE, PPO, GRPO, and (through the log-ratio) DPO; the professor said all of RLHF is built on this result in various forms.

Stage 1 — Write the objective as an integral and move the gradient inside

  1. Expectation as an integral: $$J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[R(\tau)]=\int p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau$$definition of expectation
  2. Gradient of an integral, with $R(\tau)$ not depending on $\theta$: $$\nabla_\theta J=\int \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau$$$\nabla$ is linear; swap with $\int$

Stage 2 — The log-derivative trick restores a probability weight

  1. Chain rule on the logarithm: $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)}{p_\theta(\tau)}\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)=p_\theta(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)$$log-derivative trick
  2. Substitute into the integral of Stage 1: $$\nabla_\theta J=\int p_\theta(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\,d\tau$$density is back
  3. An integral against $p_\theta$ is an expectation under $p_\theta$: $$\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\big]$$definition of expectation

Stage 3 — Expand the log-probability: the environment drops out

  1. Take the log of the product from section 1; log of a product is the sum of logs: $$\log p_\theta(\tau)=\log\rho_0(s_0)+\sum_t\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)+\sum_t\log P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$$$\log\prod=\sum\log$
  2. $\rho_0$ and $P$ are properties of the environment and contain no $\theta$: $$\nabla_\theta\log\rho_0(s_0)=0,\qquad \nabla_\theta\log P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)=0$$dynamics independent of θ
  3. Only the policy terms remain: $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$$linearity of $\nabla$

Stage 4 — The theorem and its Monte-Carlo estimator

  1. Combine Stage 2 (step 3) with Stage 3 (step 3): $$\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\Big[\Big(\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big)R(\tau)\Big]$$substitute the expanded score
  2. Replace the expectation by an average over $N$ trajectories sampled by running the policy in the environment: $$\widehat{\nabla_\theta J}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N R(\tau_i)\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\big(a_t^{(i)}\mid s_t^{(i)}\big)$$Monte-Carlo estimate
  3. Unbiasedness: $\mathbb E[\widehat{\nabla J}]=\nabla J$ because each $\tau_i\sim p_\theta$. Update by gradient ascent (we maximise $J$): $$\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+\alpha\,\widehat{\nabla_\theta J}(\theta_k)$$ascent on $J$

Worked numeric check: a 2-armed softmax bandit (exact gradient vs score-function formula)

One step ($T=1$), $\pi_\theta=\mathrm{softmax}(\theta)$ with $\theta=(\ln2,\,0)$ so $\pi=(\tfrac23,\tfrac13)$, rewards $r=(3,1)$. Here $\tau=(a)$ and $p_\theta(\tau)=\pi_\theta(a)$.

Exact: $J=\pi_1\cdot3+\pi_2\cdot1=\tfrac73$. With $\pi_1=e^{\theta_1}/(e^{\theta_1}+e^{\theta_2})$, $\partial\pi_1/\partial\theta_1=\pi_1\pi_2$, so $\partial J/\partial\theta_1=\pi_1\pi_2(r_1-r_2)=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac13\cdot2=\tfrac49$ and $\partial J/\partial\theta_2=-\tfrac49$.

Score function: $\log\pi_a=\theta_a-\log\sum_b e^{\theta_b}$, so $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_a=e_a-\pi$, i.e. $(\tfrac13,-\tfrac13)$ for $a=1$ and $(-\tfrac23,\tfrac23)$ for $a=2$. Then $\nabla J=\sum_a\pi_a\,r_a\,(e_a-\pi)=\tfrac23\cdot3\cdot(\tfrac13,-\tfrac13)+\tfrac13\cdot1\cdot(-\tfrac23,\tfrac23)=(\tfrac23,-\tfrac23)+(-\tfrac29,\tfrac29)=(\tfrac49,-\tfrac49)$. The two agree. (Python finite-difference check: $0.444444$.)

Remember for the exam
  • The three moves, in order: (i) $\nabla$ inside the integral, (ii) $\nabla p=p\nabla\log p$, (iii) $\log p_\theta(\tau)$ is a sum, and $\rho_0$, $P$ have zero gradient.
  • Key takeaway the professor stressed: we do not need to know the transition kernel or the initial distribution; the environment is only sampled.
  • It is gradient ascent on $J$ (the lecture said "descent"; the notes correct this).
  • The expectation is over all trajectories; in practice approximated with sampled ones.
Common mistake

Do not claim that $\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)$ "contains" $\nabla P$. The trick works because $\nabla\log P=0$ term by term, not because the product's derivative is simple. Also: the samples must come from the current policy $p_\theta$; the formula is on-policy.

3. Causality: reward-to-go

Statement

In the policy gradient, action $a_t$ only needs to be credited with rewards from time $t$ on: $\nabla J=\mathbb E\big[\sum_t\gamma^t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t\big]$, because $\mathbb E[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,r_k]=0$ for $k\lt t$. Source: the lecture's final form uses $G_t$ (Nov 07, section 9) and the Oct 24 note (section 17) says the equivalence "uses the return-to-go"; the proof below is step filled in — not written out in class. Used to justify the lower-variance estimator inside the advantage form.

  1. Start from the theorem with $R(\tau)=\sum_k\gamma^k r_k$ and distribute the double sum: $$\nabla J=\sum_t\sum_k\gamma^k\,\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,r_k\big]$$linearity of $\mathbb E$
  2. Fix $k\lt t$. Let $h_t=(s_0,a_0,\dots,s_t)$ be the history up to the moment $a_t$ is drawn. The reward $r_k=r(s_k,a_k)$ with $k\lt t$ is a function of $h_t$. Condition on $h_t$: $$\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,r_k\big]=\mathbb E_{h_t}\Big[r_k\;\mathbb E_{a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)}\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\big]\Big]$$tower property
  3. The inner expectation is zero (log-derivative trick backwards): $$\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)]=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\frac{\nabla\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}=\nabla\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\nabla 1=0$$probabilities sum to 1
  4. Hence every term with $k\lt t$ vanishes and only $k\ge t$ survive: $$\nabla J=\mathbb E\Big[\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^k r_k\Big]$$drop zero terms
  5. Factor $\gamma^t$ out of the inner sum and use $G_t=\sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^{k-t}r_k$: $$\nabla J=\mathbb E\Big[\sum_t\gamma^t\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t\Big]$$$\gamma^k=\gamma^t\gamma^{k-t}$
  6. Variance: the dropped terms have zero mean but non-zero variance, so removing them keeps the estimator unbiased and can only lower its variance (sum of fewer noisy terms).same mean, less noise
▪
Worked numeric check

Take the bandit of section 2 played twice independently (actions $a_0,a_1$, rewards $r_0=r(a_0)$, $r_1=r(a_1)$). The cross-term is $\mathbb E[\nabla\log\pi(a_1)\,r_0]=\mathbb E[\nabla\log\pi(a_1)]\cdot\mathbb E[r_0]=(0,0)\cdot\tfrac73$, because $\mathbb E[\nabla\log\pi]=\tfrac23(\tfrac13,-\tfrac13)+\tfrac13(-\tfrac23,\tfrac23)=(0,0)$. The sampled value is not zero, though: e.g. $a_1=1,r_0=3$ gives $(1,-1)$, pure noise.

Remember for the exam
  • Reason in one line: the past reward does not depend on the future action, so its covariance with the score of the future action is zero.
  • The professor wrote the final form with the return from time $t$ and omitted the $\gamma^t$ factor for simplicity; the exact form carries $\gamma^t$ (often dropped in practice).

4. A state-dependent baseline is unbiased (and reduces variance)

Statement

For any function $b(s_t)$ that depends on the state but not on $a_t$: $\mathbb E\big[\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,(G_t-b(s_t))\big]=\mathbb E\big[\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t\big]$, i.e. subtracting $b$ changes the variance but not the expected gradient. Source: Oct 24 (section 17.1, stated without proof) and Nov 07 (section 9); the proof is step filled in — not written out in class. Used to define the advantage $A=Q-V$ and by PPO/GRPO (group mean as baseline).

  1. The extra term in the gradient is $\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,b(s_t)\big]$. Condition on the history $h_t$ up to $s_t$; $b(s_t)$ is a function of $h_t$ and factors out of the inner expectation over $a_t$: $$\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,b(s_t)\big]=\mathbb E_{h_t}\Big[b(s_t)\,\mathbb E_{a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)}\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\big]\Big]$$tower property
  2. Inner expectation: $$\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\sum_a\nabla\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\nabla\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\nabla 1=0$$$\nabla\pi=\pi\nabla\log\pi$; normalisation
  3. So the baseline term has zero expectation, and by linearity: $$\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,(G_t-b(s_t))\big]=\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t\big]-0$$unbiased
  4. Variance (one scalar component, score $X=\partial_{\theta_j}\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$, return $G$, state fixed). The mean of $X(G-b)$ does not depend on $b$, so only the second moment matters: $$\mathbb E\big[X^2(G-b)^2\big]=\mathbb E[X^2G^2]-2b\,\mathbb E[X^2G]+b^2\,\mathbb E[X^2]$$expand the square
  5. This is a parabola in $b$, minimised where the derivative vanishes: $$b^\star=\frac{\mathbb E[X^2G]}{\mathbb E[X^2]}$$$\frac{d}{db}=0$ (filled in)
  6. $b^\star$ is a score-weighted average return for that state; it is close to the state's average return $V^\pi(s)$, which is why the professor's natural choice is $b(s)=V^\pi(s)$ (next section). It is not exactly optimal, but cheap and effective.choice of $b$

Remember for the exam
  • Core identity: $\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)]=0$; hence any $b(s)$ is allowed.
  • Baseline reduces variance, never the bias: "unbiased, lower variance". The professor stated the variance claim without proof.
Common mistake

The baseline must not depend on the action $a_t$ (or on anything after $a_t$ is drawn). If $b=b(s_t,a_t)$, then $b$ cannot be pulled out of $\mathbb E_{a_t}$ and the bias is $\mathbb E_a[\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)\,b(s,a)]\ne0$ in general.

5. Value, Q, advantage and the REINFORCE update

Statement

With $V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid s_t=s]$, $Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid s_t=s,a_t=a]$ and $A^\pi=Q^\pi-V^\pi$: (a) $\mathbb E[\nabla\log\pi\,G_t]=\mathbb E[\nabla\log\pi\,Q^\pi]$; (b) $Q^\pi(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb E_{s'}[V^\pi(s')]$ and $V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[Q^\pi(s,a)]$; (c) $\nabla J=\mathbb E\big[\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,A^\pi(s_t,a_t)\big]$. Source: Oct 24 (section 17) and Nov 07 (section 9); the lecture said the algebra to go from "return minus baseline" to the advantage was skipped, so steps (a)-(c) are filled in — not written out in class. Used by PPO (the $\hat A_t$ in the objective).

  1. (a) $G_t\to Q$. Condition on the history $h_t$, then on $a_t$. By the Markov property, the future after $(s_t,a_t)$ does not depend on older history, so $\mathbb E[G_t\mid h_t,a_t]=Q^\pi(s_t,a_t)$. The score $\nabla\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)$ is a function of $(s_t,a_t)$ and passes through: $$\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t\big]=\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,Q^\pi(s_t,a_t)\big]$$tower property + Markov
  2. (b) Bellman relation. The return obeys $G_t=r_t+\gamma G_{t+1}$. Take $\mathbb E[\,\cdot\mid s_t=s,a_t=a]$; $r_t=r(s,a)$ is known and $\mathbb E[G_{t+1}\mid s_{t+1}=s']=V^\pi(s')$: $$Q^\pi(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V^\pi(s')$$recursion of return
  3. Average $Q$ over the first action drawn from the policy: $$V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,Q^\pi(s,a)$$tower property
  4. (c) Advantage form. $V^\pi(s_t)$ depends on the state only, so by section 4 it is a legal baseline: $$\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,(Q^\pi(s_t,a_t)-V^\pi(s_t))\big]=\mathbb E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,Q^\pi(s_t,a_t)\big]$$baseline lemma, $b=V$
  5. Chain steps (a) and (c) with the reward-to-go form of section 3: $$\nabla J=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\Big[\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,A^\pi(s_t,a_t)\Big],\qquad A^\pi=Q^\pi-V^\pi$$definition of $A$ (lecture drops $\gamma^t$)
  6. Centering property: using step 3, $$\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[A^\pi(s,a)]=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s)=0$$advantage averages to zero
▪

REINFORCE (the canonical algorithm)

  1. Run the current policy $\pi_\theta$ in the environment to collect $N$ trajectories $\tau_i$.on-policy sampling
  2. For each time step compute the return-to-go $G_t^{(i)}$ (and, with a baseline, a value estimate $\hat V(s_t^{(i)})$). The advantage estimate is $\hat A_t=G_t-\hat V(s_t)$ (the lecture: "return or $Q$ estimate minus $V$"; the one-step TD form $r_t+\gamma\hat V(s_{t+1})-\hat V(s_t)$ is a standard alternative, not derived in class).estimate $A$
  3. Form $\hat g=\frac1N\sum_i\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t^{(i)}\mid s_t^{(i)})\,\hat A_t^{(i)}$ and update $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\hat g$.likelihood-ratio update
Worked numeric check

Figure 8: $\pi=(0.5,0.3,0.2)$, $Q=(4,2,-1)$. Then $V=0.5\cdot4+0.3\cdot2+0.2\cdot(-1)=2.4$ and $A=(1.6,-0.4,-3.4)$. Check: $\sum\pi A=0.8-0.12-0.68=0$. Bellman check: if from this state each action gives $r=(1,0,-1)$ and next-state value $V(s')=(3,2,0)$ with $\gamma=1$ (deterministic), then $Q=r+\gamma V(s')=(4,2,-1)$, matching. In the bandit of section 4, $V=\tfrac23\cdot3+\tfrac13\cdot1=\tfrac73$, $A=(\tfrac23,-\tfrac43)$ and $\sum_a\pi_aA_a\nabla\log\pi_a$ component 1 $=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac13+\tfrac13\cdot(-\tfrac43)\cdot(-\tfrac23)=\tfrac4{27}+\tfrac8{27}=\tfrac49$, the exact gradient again.

Remember for the exam
  • $V$ conditions only on the state; $Q$ also fixes the first action; $A=Q-V$ = "how much better is this action than the policy's average action in this state".
  • It is not literally "one reward minus $V$": it is a return (or $Q$) estimate minus $V$.
  • Algorithms (PPO, TRPO, DPO, GRPO) differ mainly in how they estimate the advantage and how they keep updates stable.

6. The LLM as an RL policy

Statement

Identify state = prompt plus tokens so far, action = next token, trajectory = full response. Then $p_\theta(\tau)=\rho_0(x)\,\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ with $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_t\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})$, and $\nabla J=\mathbb E_{x,\,y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[R(x,y)\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})\big]$: reward-weighted next-token log-likelihood. Source: Nov 07 (section 10; the question was deferred from Oct 24, section 13). The transition-kernel simplification is step filled in — not written out in class. Used by RLHF/PPO/GRPO for LLMs.

  1. Dictionary: $s_t=(x,y_{\lt t})$, $a_t=y_t$, $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)=\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})$ (the next-token softmax), $s_0=x\sim\rho_0$, terminal at the end token.definition
  2. The "environment" only concatenates: $P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)=1$ if $s_{t+1}=(s_t,a_t)$ and $0$ otherwise. In the trajectory probability of section 1, along the realised trajectory every $P$ factor equals $1$: $$p_\theta(\tau)=\rho_0(x)\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})\cdot1=\rho_0(x)\,\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$$deterministic transition
  3. Hence the trajectory log-probability is exactly the autoregressive log-likelihood: $$\nabla\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})=\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$$$\rho_0$ has no θ; log of product
  4. Apply the theorem of section 2 with $R(\tau)=R(x,y)$: $$\nabla J=\mathbb E_{x\sim\rho_0,\;y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[R(x,y)\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})\Big]$$policy-gradient theorem
  5. Compare with pre-training/SFT: the loss gradient is $-\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t})$ on given data. The RL gradient is the same score on the model's own samples, scaled by the reward (or by the advantage, with a baseline). With $R\equiv1$ on demonstration data it reduces to SFT.same score, different weights
  6. Sampling is the same as decoding: the policy is the post-softmax distribution; logits are the unnormalised parametrisation and the gradient w.r.t. the logits $z$ is $e_{y_t}-\pi$ (as in the bandit of section 2).softmax score

Worked numeric check

Response "New Delhi" with $\pi_\theta(\text{New}\mid x)=0.5$, $\pi_\theta(\text{Delhi}\mid x,\text{New})=0.4$, $\pi_\theta(\texttt{eos}\mid\cdot)=1$: $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=0.5\cdot0.4=0.2$ and $\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\log0.5+\log0.4=-0.693-0.916=-1.609=\log0.2$. With $R=1$, the sample's gradient is $1\cdot(\nabla\log0.5\text{-term}+\nabla\log0.4\text{-term})$, which raises the probability of both tokens.

Remember for the exam
  • Table to know: state = prompt + generated prefix; action = next token; policy = next-token distribution; trajectory = full response (can be truncated for long interactions); reward = response quality/correctness (RLVR: checker, RLHF: learned reward model).
  • The policy is the normalised softmax distribution; logits are only the parametrisation.

7. Importance sampling

Statement

For densities $p,q$ with $q(x)>0$ wherever $p(x)f(x)\ne0$: $\mathbb E_{x\sim p}[f(x)]=\mathbb E_{x\sim q}\big[\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x)\big]$. For trajectories the weight is a product of per-step policy ratios, $\tfrac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\mathrm{old}}(\tau)}=\prod_t\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}$. Source: Nov 07 (section 11; the trajectory product is step filled in — not written out in class). Used to reuse data from a frozen old policy in TRPO/PPO (next sections).

  1. Write the target expectation as an integral (or sum): $$\mathbb E_{x\sim p}[f(x)]=\int p(x)\,f(x)\,dx$$definition
  2. Multiply and divide by $q(x)$, allowed wherever $q(x)>0$ (support condition: $q$ must cover wherever $p f\ne0$): $$\int p(x)f(x)\,dx=\int q(x)\,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,f(x)\,dx$$multiply by $q/q=1$
  3. The integral is now against $q$, hence an expectation under $q$ with importance weight $w=p/q$: $$\mathbb E_{x\sim p}[f(x)]=\mathbb E_{x\sim q}\Big[\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\,f(X)\Big]$$change of measure
  4. Estimator: draw $x_i\sim q$ and average $\frac1N\sum_i w(x_i)f(x_i)$; unbiased by step 3.Monte Carlo
  5. Variance (filled in): the mean is $\mathbb E_p f$, so $$\mathrm{Var}_q[wf]=\mathbb E_q[w^2f^2]-(\mathbb E_pf)^2=\mathbb E_p[w\,f^2]-(\mathbb E_pf)^2$$$\mathbb E_q[w^2f^2]=\mathbb E_p[wf^2]$ Large $w=p/q$ where $p f^2$ is big makes this large: this is the variance caveat.
  6. Trajectories. For policies, take $p=p_\theta$ and $q=p_{\mathrm{old}}$ (a frozen earlier snapshot of the same model). The factors $\rho_0$ and $P$ are identical in both and cancel: $$\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\mathrm{old}}(\tau)}=\frac{\rho_0(s_0)\prod_t\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)}{\rho_0(s_0)\prod_t\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)}=\prod_t r_t(\theta),\quad r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}$$dynamics cancel A product of many ratios drifts far from $1$, which is why PPO works with the per-step ratio $r_t$ and keeps it near $1$.
▪
Worked numeric check

$p=(0.7,0.2,0.1)$, $f=(1,2,10)$, so $\mathbb E_pf=0.7+0.4+1.0=2.1$. Uniform $q=(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)$ gives $w=(2.1,0.6,0.3)$ and $\mathbb E_q[wf]=\tfrac13(2.1+1.2+3.0)=2.1$. Same value, as the identity says. Single-sample variances: sampling directly from $p$: $7.09$; uniform $q$: $0.54$ (a better proposal, since it visits the large-$f$ outcome more often); $q=(0.8,0.19,0.01)$: $w=(0.875,\,1.053,\,10)$, still $\mathbb E_q[wf]=0.7+0.4+1.0=2.1$ but variance $\approx97$.

Remember for the exam
  • The identity is a one-line "multiply and divide by $q$", plus the support condition ($q>0$ wherever $p f\ne0$).
  • On-policy: samples come from the policy being optimised. Off-policy: samples come from a behaviour policy such as a frozen earlier snapshot.
  • The old policy is the same model frozen earlier, not an unrelated architecture.
Common mistake

Importance sampling is unbiased but not "free": if $q$ is far from $p$, the weights are unreliable and the variance blows up. That is the motivation for keeping $\pi_\theta$ close to $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ (TRPO's KL bound, PPO's clip).

8. The off-policy surrogate and the TRPO constraint

Statement

The importance-weighted surrogate $L^{\mathrm{IS}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t[r_t(\theta)\hat A_t]$, with data from $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$, has at $\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ the same gradient as the policy gradient, since $\nabla r_t(\theta_{\mathrm{old}})=\nabla\log\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)$. TRPO maximises it subject to $\mathbb E_t[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t))]\le\delta$. Source: Nov 07 (section 12 and Q&A 13). The derivation from the policy-gradient form is step filled in — not written out in class. Used to motivate PPO.

  1. Start from the advantage form of section 5, one time step, where the state $s_t$ comes from the old rollouts and $A$ is estimated from them: $$g=\mathbb E_{s}\,\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,\hat A(s,a)\big]$$section 5
  2. The data were generated by $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$, not $\pi_\theta$. Apply importance sampling over actions: $$g=\mathbb E_{s}\,\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid s)}\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a\mid s)}\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,\hat A(s,a)\Big]$$section 7, $p=\pi_\theta,q=\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$
  3. Use $\pi_\theta\nabla\log\pi_\theta=\nabla\pi_\theta$ and note $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ does not depend on $\theta$: $$\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}}\nabla\log\pi_\theta=\frac{\nabla\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}}=\nabla r(\theta),\qquad r(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a\mid s)}$$log-derivative trick again
  4. So $g$ is the gradient of the surrogate (with $\hat A$ and the state distribution held fixed): $$g=\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb E_{t}\big[r_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t\big]=\nabla L^{\mathrm{IS}}(\theta)$$$\nabla$ passes through the fixed-data expectation This is the answer to the student's question "where did the log-policy term go?": it is recovered by differentiating the ratio.
  5. At $\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$: $r_t=1$ and $\nabla r_t=\nabla\log\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$, so $\nabla L^{\mathrm{IS}}(\theta_{\mathrm{old}})$ equals the on-policy policy gradient exactly. Away from $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ the approximation (fixed $\hat A$, old state distribution) degrades.$r(\theta_{\mathrm{old}})=1$
  6. Nothing in $L^{\mathrm{IS}}$ prevents huge steps, and large policy changes can destroy what was learnt in pre-training/SFT and make weights unreliable (section 7). TRPO adds a trust region: $$\max_\theta\;\mathbb E_t\big[r_t(\theta)\hat A_t\big]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb E_t\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)\big]\le\delta$$limit policy movement
  7. Extra, standard but not derived in class: near $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\approx\tfrac12(\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{old}})^\top F(\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{old}})$ with $F$ the Fisher matrix, so the region is an ellipsoid, as drawn.second-order expansion of KL
▪
Worked numeric check (bandit: $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}=(\tfrac23,\tfrac13)$, $r=(3,1)$, $V=\tfrac73$, $\hat A=(\tfrac23,-\tfrac43)$)

Surrogate $L(\theta)=\sum_a\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a)\,\frac{\pi_\theta(a)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a)}\hat A_a=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a)\hat A_a$. Its $\theta_1$-derivative at $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ is $\sum_a\pi_a\hat A_a(e_a-\pi)_1=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac13+\tfrac13\cdot(-\tfrac43)\cdot(-\tfrac23)=\tfrac{12}{27}=\tfrac49$: exactly the policy gradient from section 2. Directly: $\nabla_{\theta_1}r_{a=1}=\pi_1(1-\pi_1)/\pi_{\mathrm{old},1}=1-\pi_1=\tfrac13=\nabla_{\theta_1}\log\pi_1$ at $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$.

Remember for the exam
  • $r_t(\theta)=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)$; at $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$: $r=1$ and $\nabla r=\nabla\log\pi$.
  • TRPO: surrogate + KL trust region ($\le\delta$). PPO: surrogate + clipping, no explicit constraint.
  • Reason for the constraint: stability, not to lose pre-training/SFT behaviour, and to keep importance weights reliable.

9. The PPO clipped objective, case by case

Statement

$L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\big[\min\big(r_t\hat A_t,\;\mathrm{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t\big)\big]$ equals $\hat A\min(r,1+\epsilon)$ when $\hat A>0$ and $\hat A\max(r,1-\epsilon)$ when $\hat A\lt 0$; its gradient is $\hat A\nabla r$ when the unclipped branch is active and $0$ otherwise, so there is no incentive to push $r$ beyond the band in the direction that the advantage favours. Source: Nov 07 (section 12). The case analysis is step filled in — the lecture stated the objective and the intuition but did not tabulate the cases. Used in RLHF with PPO (actor update).

  1. Define $\mathrm{clip}(r,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)=\min(\max(r,1-\epsilon),1+\epsilon)$: it equals $1-\epsilon$ below the band, $r$ inside, $1+\epsilon$ above. Inside the band the two arguments of the $\min$ coincide: $L=rA$.definition of clip
  2. Case $\hat A>0$. Multiplying by a positive number keeps the order of $r$ and the clip. If $r\lt 1-\epsilon$: $r\lt \mathrm{clip}=1-\epsilon$, so $\min=rA$. If $r>1+\epsilon$: $\mathrm{clip}=1+\epsilon\lt r$, so $\min=(1+\epsilon)A$. Altogether $$L=\hat A\,\min(r,\,1+\epsilon)$$$A>0$ keeps inequalities
  3. Case $\hat A\lt 0$. Multiplying by a negative number reverses inequalities. If $r\lt 1-\epsilon$: $rA>(1-\epsilon)A$, so $\min=(1-\epsilon)A$. If $r>1+\epsilon$: $rA\lt (1+\epsilon)A$, so $\min=rA$. Altogether $$L=\hat A\,\max(r,\,1-\epsilon)$$$A<0$ flips inequalities
  4. Gradient. Since $r=\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$, $\nabla r=r\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta$. A constant (clipped) branch has zero gradient, so $$\nabla L=\begin{cases}\hat A\,r\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)&\text{if }(\hat A>0\text{ and }r\lt 1+\epsilon)\text{ or }(\hat A\lt 0\text{ and }r>1-\epsilon)\\[2pt]0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$flat branch has zero slope
  5. Interpretation, $\hat A>0$: raise the probability of the action (increase $r$) until $r=1+\epsilon$; after that the objective is flat, so no further push. $\hat A\lt 0$: lower the probability until $r=1-\epsilon$, then stop. So the policy is not rewarded for moving far from $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ in the direction that benefits the sampled action.removes incentive to move far
  6. The other direction stays active: for $\hat A>0$ and $r\lt 1-\epsilon$ (policy made a good action less likely) the gradient is still $\hat A\nabla r$ and corrects the mistake; likewise $\hat A\lt 0$, $r>1+\epsilon$. Equivalently $L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}\le r\hat A$ always (pessimistic lower bound of $L^{\mathrm{IS}}$).min = pessimistic bound
  7. Per-sample objective is averaged over time steps/samples: $L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t[\cdot]$. PPO commonly adds a value loss for the critic, an entropy bonus and sometimes a KL penalty/early stopping; $\epsilon$ is tuned (larger: bigger updates, risk of instability; smaller: conservative).practical recipe

Remember for the exam
  • $L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}=\mathbb E_t[\min(r_t\hat A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t)]$; for $A>0$ the clip bites at $1+\epsilon$, for $A\lt 0$ at $1-\epsilon$.
  • Say it as: "clipping removes the incentive to push the ratio outside $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ in the advantageous direction".
  • The professor stressed that PPO uses a frozen old policy, the same model snapshot, for rollouts; the current policy is optimised for several steps against it.
  • Four roles in a PPO-style RLHF pipeline: actor, old/reference policy, reward model, critic; only the actor is deployed.
Common mistake

Clipping does not set the objective to zero outside the band and does not guarantee the policy cannot move farther (other samples, momentum, shared parameters can still drag $r$ beyond the band). It only flattens the incentive in one direction; KL monitoring may still be used. Also, do not confuse the two cases: for $A\lt 0$ the active region is $r>1-\epsilon$, not $r\lt 1+\epsilon$.
